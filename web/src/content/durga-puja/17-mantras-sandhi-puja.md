## Forty-eight minutes of words

*Sandhi puja* (সন্ধিপূজা) compresses the whole festival into forty-eight minutes, and its mantras match: fewer words than any other rite of the pujo, and the darkest. The rite occupies the *sandhikshan* (সন্ধিক্ষণ) — the "juncture moment" made of the last 24 minutes of the Ashtami tithi and the first 24 minutes of Nabami (see [Ashtami](09-ashtami.md) and [Nabami](10-nabami.md)). Tradition holds that at exactly this juncture the goddess took the form of **Chamunda** (চামুণ্ডা) and destroyed the demon generals Chanda and Munda. So for these forty-eight minutes the worship turns away from the familiar ten-armed golden mother and addresses her most terrifying aspect: the gaunt, dark, sword-bearing goddess who sprang from the furrowed brow of Ambika in the seventh chapter of the *Devi Mahatmya* — the "Glory of the Goddess" embedded in the Markandeya Purana and recited across Bengal as the *Chandi path* (see [The Chandi](20-mantras-the-chandi.md)).

The visible ritual is famous: 108 oil lamps lit together, 108 lotuses offered, garlands of *bel* (বেল) leaves and red *jaba* (জবা) hibiscus, the dhak rising to a frenzy, and — where the custom survives — a symbolic *balidan* (বলিদান), today almost everywhere a pumpkin, ash-gourd, sugarcane or banana rather than an animal. It is also the one rite of the pujo performed *against the clock*: begun and completed within the forty-eight minutes, whatever hour of day or night they fall.

A note on what this chapter can and cannot give you. The full spoken liturgy of sandhi puja — every invocation, every *nyasa*, every offering sentence — lives in the priestly manuals of Bengal, above all the *Purohit Darpan* (পুরোহিত দর্পণ), and those step-by-step texts are not reliably available in a form we could verify. What follows are the core texts that ARE well attested across sources: the dhyana of Chamunda used in the Bengali tradition, the Navarna mantra, the Devi Mahatmya verses the ritual re-enacts, and the offering formulas at the level our sources support. Where something varies or could not be verified, the notes say so.

## The dhyana of Chamunda — the form in the lamplight

The *dhyana* (ধ্যান) is the meditation verse the *purohit* (পুরোহিত) recites while visualizing the deity, holding a flower at his heart, before worship can begin. In the Bengali tradition the dhyana of Chamunda for sandhi puja is taken directly from the Devi Mahatmya's own description of Kali's emergence — the scripture's picture of the goddess becomes the priest's inner image of her:

> ॐ कालीं करालवदनां विनिष्क्रान्तामसिपाशिनीम्।
> विचित्रखट्वाङ्गधरां नरमालाविभूषणाम्॥
> द्वीपिचर्मपरीधानां शुष्कमांसातिभैरवाम्।
> अतिविस्तारवदनां जिह्वाललनभीषणाम्॥
> निमग्नारक्तनयनां नादापूरितदिङ्मुखाम्॥
>
> ওঁ কালীং করালবদনাং বিনিষ্ক্রান্তামসিপাশিনীম্।
> বিচিত্রখট্বাঙ্গধরাং নরমালাবিভূষণাম্॥
> দ্বীপিচর্মপরীধানাং শুষ্কমাংসাতিভৈরবাম্।
> অতিবিস্তারবদনাং জিহ্বাললনভীষণাম্॥
> নিমগ্নারক্তনয়নাং নাদাপূরিতদিঙ্মুখাম্॥
>
> *oṁ kālīṁ karāla-vadanāṁ viniṣkrāntām asi-pāśinīm ·
> vicitra-khaṭvāṅga-dharāṁ nara-mālā-vibhūṣaṇām ·
> dvīpi-carma-parīdhānāṁ śuṣka-māṁsāti-bhairavām ·
> ativistāra-vadanāṁ jihvā-lalana-bhīṣaṇām ·
> nimagnā-rakta-nayanāṁ nādāpūrita-diṅmukhām*

**In simple words:** Kali of the gaping, terrible face, sprung forth bearing sword and noose; carrying the strange skull-topped staff (*khatvanga*), wearing a garland of human heads; wrapped in a tiger's skin, dreadful in her gauntness of wasted flesh; her mouth stretched enormously wide, terrifying with her lolling tongue; her reddened eyes deep-sunken; filling every direction of the sky with her roars. The liturgy does not soften her: the year's most crowded rite faces the tradition's most frightening face, and calls her Mother.

Notes worth keeping in mind:

- This verse set, published as "the dhyana of Chamunda" on Bengali purohit-training resources, is **word for word the Devi Mahatmya's description of Kali in 7.6–8** (given in full below) — the only change is the case-endings, which the dhyana turns into the accusative because the priest is visualizing *her, the one who…*; some printings keep the scripture's own nominative reading unchanged. Minor spelling variants occur between printings (e.g. *khaṭṭāṅga* for *khaṭvāṅga*, *paridhānā* for *parīdhānā*).
- It is recited by the purohit alone, as part of the dhyana-and-avahana (visualization and invocation) that opens the sandhi worship; the congregation watches.
- Do not confuse this with the famous dhyana of *Dakshina Kalika* used at Kali Puja — *karāla-vadanāṁ ghorāṁ mukta-keśīṁ caturbhujām / kālikāṁ dakṣiṇāṁ divyāṁ…* ("of terrible face, dishevelled hair, four arms…"). The two open with almost the same word and describe closely related forms, which is why they are often mixed up; the sandhi puja verse is the Devi Mahatmya text.

## The Navarna mantra — nine syllables

The most concentrated utterance of the entire Shakta tradition, chanted through the sandhi and throughout Chandi recitation:

> ॐ ऐं ह्रीं क्लीं चामुण्डायै विच्चे।
>
> ওঁ ঐং হ্রীং ক্লীং চামুণ্ডায়ৈ বিচ্চে।
>
> *oṁ aiṁ hrīṁ klīṁ cāmuṇḍāyai vicce*

**In simple words:** After the opening *oṁ*, the mantra is nine syllables — *aiṁ hrīṁ klīṁ cā-muṇ-ḍā-yai vic-ce* — which gives it its name: *navarna* (নবার্ণ), "nine-lettered," also called the Navakshari or Chandi mantra. The first three are *bija* (বীজ), "seed" syllables — sound as invocation, carrying condensed divine power rather than dictionary meaning. Tradition assigns them to the three great forms of the goddess honoured in the three episodes of the Devi Mahatmya: **aiṁ** to Mahasaraswati (wisdom and speech), **hrīṁ** to Mahalakshmi (sustenance and majesty), **klīṁ** to Mahakali (power and time). *Cāmuṇḍāyai* is "to Chamunda" — the one word in the mantra with plain grammar, a dative of address. *Vicce* has no agreed literal translation; traditional commentators read it variously as a seed-sound of its own or as an appeal to the goddess as consciousness. Taken whole, the mantra addresses the total power of the goddess under the name she won at the sandhikshan.

Notes:

- A sober caution against treating this as a "sandhi puja mantra" only: the Navarna is THE root mantra of the entire Durga Saptashati / Chandi tradition. It frames the full recitation of the 700 verses, it is the mantra of Chandi *japa* year-round, and it appears in the Siddha Kunjika Stotra (see [The Chandi](20-mantras-the-chandi.md)). Sandhi puja is simply the moment in the pujo when the deity it names — Chamunda — is directly before you.
- Spelling of the last word varies across sources and regions: *vicce*, *vicche*, *viccey* (বিচ্চে). The nine-syllable count is standard, though some schools count with the *oṁ* included and describe it as ten.
- Priests use it with formal preliminaries (*viniyoga*, *nyasa*) in ritual settings; as plain repetition it is one of the most widely chanted goddess mantras and is not restricted to priests.

## Devi Mahatmya 7.5–8 — the emergence of Kali

These are the verses the sandhikshan re-enacts. Chanda and Munda, generals of the demon kings Shumbha and Nishumbha, have come with an army to seize the beautiful goddess Ambika. Verse numbers follow the common printed editions; numbering shifts by a verse or two between recensions.

> ततः कोपं चकारोच्चैरम्बिका तानरीन् प्रति।
> कोपेन चास्या वदनं मषीवर्णमभूत्तदा॥ ५॥
> भ्रुकुटीकुटिलात्तस्या ललाटफलकाद्द्रुतम्।
> काली करालवदना विनिष्क्रान्तासिपाशिनी॥ ६॥
> विचित्रखट्वाङ्गधरा नरमालाविभूषणा।
> द्वीपिचर्मपरीधाना शुष्कमांसातिभैरवा॥ ७॥
> अतिविस्तारवदना जिह्वाललनभीषणा।
> निमग्नारक्तनयना नादापूरितदिङ्मुखा॥ ८॥
>
> ততঃ কোপং চকারোচ্চৈরম্বিকা তানরীন্ প্রতি।
> কোপেন চাস্যা বদনং মষীবর্ণমভূত্তদা॥ ৫॥
> ভ্রুকুটীকুটিলাত্তস্যা ললাটফলকাদ্দ্রুতম্।
> কালী করালবদনা বিনিষ্ক্রান্তাসিপাশিনী॥ ৬॥
> বিচিত্রখট্বাঙ্গধরা নরমালাবিভূষণা।
> দ্বীপিচর্মপরীধানা শুষ্কমাংসাতিভৈরবা॥ ৭॥
> অতিবিস্তারবদনা জিহ্বাললনভীষণা।
> নিমগ্নারক্তনয়না নাদাপূরিতদিঙ্মুখা॥ ৮॥
>
> *tataḥ kopaṁ cakāroccair ambikā tān arīn prati ·
> kopena cāsyā vadanaṁ maṣī-varṇam abhūt tadā ॥5॥
> bhrukuṭī-kuṭilāt tasyā lalāṭa-phalakād drutam ·
> kālī karālavadanā viniṣkrāntāsipāśinī ॥6॥
> vicitra-khaṭvāṅga-dharā nara-mālā-vibhūṣaṇā ·
> dvīpi-carma-parīdhānā śuṣka-māṁsāti-bhairavā ॥7॥
> ativistāra-vadanā jihvā-lalana-bhīṣaṇā ·
> nimagnā-rakta-nayanā nādāpūrita-diṅmukhā ॥8॥*

**In simple words:** Then Ambika's fury rose high against those enemies, and with that fury her face went black as ink. From the flat of her forehead, knotted in a frown, Kali burst out at once — terrible-faced, armed with sword and noose, bearing the strange khatvanga staff, garlanded with human heads, clad in a tiger's skin, ghastly with wasted flesh, her mouth gaping wide, her tongue lolling horribly, her sunken eyes red, her roars flooding every quarter of the sky.

Notes:

- The image is of wrath itself taking form: the fierce goddess is not summoned from outside but erupts out of the serene one. This is the theological point of sandhi puja — Durga and Chamunda are one being at two intensities, and the juncture of the two tithis is the crack through which the fiercer one steps out.
- In the verses that follow (7.9 onward), Kali devours the demon army, crunching elephants, chariots and horses — verses relished in every Chandi path recitation.

## Devi Mahatmya 7.23–27 — the naming of Chamunda

From the same chapter: the great Devi's own words after the victory, recited as the theology of the night.

> शिरश्चण्डस्य काली च गृहीत्वा मुण्डमेव च।
> प्राह प्रचण्डाट्टहासमिश्रमभ्येत्य चण्डिकाम्॥ २३॥
> मया तवात्रोपहृतौ चण्डमुण्डौ महापशू।
> युद्धयज्ञे स्वयं शुम्भं निशुम्भं च हनिष्यसि॥ २४॥
> तावानीतौ ततो दृष्ट्वा चण्डमुण्डौ महासुरौ।
> उवाच कालीं कल्याणी ललितं चण्डिका वचः॥ २६॥
> यस्माच्चण्डं च मुण्डं च गृहीत्वा त्वमुपागता।
> चामुण्डेति ततो लोके ख्याता देवि भविष्यसि॥ २७॥
>
> শিরশ্চণ্ডস্য কালী চ গৃহীত্বা মুণ্ডমেব চ।
> প্রাহ প্রচণ্ডাট্টহাসমিশ্রমভ্যেত্য চণ্ডিকাম্॥ ২৩॥
> ময়া তবাত্রোপহৃতৌ চণ্ডমুণ্ডৌ মহাপশূ।
> যুদ্ধযজ্ঞে স্বয়ং শুম্ভং নিশুম্ভং চ হনিষ্যসি॥ ২৪॥
> তাবানীতৌ ততো দৃষ্ট্বা চণ্ডমুণ্ডৌ মহাসুরৌ।
> উবাচ কালীং কল্যাণী ললিতং চণ্ডিকা বচঃ॥ ২৬॥
> যস্মাচ্চণ্ডং চ মুণ্ডং চ গৃহীত্বা ত্বমুপাগতা।
> চামুণ্ডেতি ততো লোকে খ্যাতা দেবি ভবিষ্যসি॥ ২৭॥
>
> *śiraś caṇḍasya kālī ca gṛhītvā muṇḍam eva ca ·
> prāha pracaṇḍāṭṭahāsa-miśram abhyetya caṇḍikām ॥23॥
> mayā tavātropahṛtau caṇḍa-muṇḍau mahāpaśū ·
> yuddha-yajñe svayaṁ śumbhaṁ niśumbhaṁ ca haniṣyasi ॥24॥
> tāv ānītau tato dṛṣṭvā caṇḍa-muṇḍau mahāsurau ·
> uvāca kālīṁ kalyāṇī lalitaṁ caṇḍikā vacaḥ ॥26॥
> yasmāc caṇḍaṁ ca muṇḍaṁ ca gṛhītvā tvam upāgatā ·
> cāmuṇḍeti tato loke khyātā devi bhaviṣyasi ॥27॥*

**In simple words:** Taking up the severed heads of Chanda and of Munda, Kali came before Chandika and, her words mixed with a wild, pealing laugh, said: "Here I bring you Chanda and Munda, two great beasts offered in this sacrifice of battle; Shumbha and Nishumbha you shall slay yourself." Seeing the two great demons brought to her, the gracious Chandika answered Kali playfully: "Because you seized Chanda and Munda and came to me, you, O goddess, shall be famed in the world as **Chamunda**." It is the moment a deed becomes a name — the name the forty-eight minutes worship.

Notes:

- This is the charter-verse of the entire sandhi puja: it explains the name of the deity worshipped in the forty-eight minutes and why the moment of the two demons' death is celebrated as a juncture.
- Note the sacrificial language the scripture itself uses — *mahāpaśū*, "two great sacrificial beasts," and *yuddha-yajña*, "the sacrifice of battle." This is the scriptural root of the balidan performed at sandhi puja, and equally the justification pujas cite for offering a pumpkin, sugarcane or cucumber in its place: what the goddess receives is the ego-demon, not the flesh.
- Verse numbering here follows the printed edition we checked, which runs 24 then 26; other editions smooth the sequence. The wording of the naming verse itself is stable across sources.

## The 108 lamps and the flower offerings

The most photographed moment of sandhi puja is *pradip dan* — the lighting and offering of 108 earthen lamps — together with 108 lotuses, bel leaves and jaba garlands. Here honesty is required about the texts: the specific consecration liturgy the purohit murmurs over the lamps at sandhi puja is part of the manual tradition (Purohit Darpan and allied *paddhati* books) and is not verifiably published, so we do not reproduce it. What the sources do support is the general shape of the offering formulas, which every upachara in Bengali puja follows: the object is named, then presented with the deity's own mantra.

For the lamps, the standard pattern joins *eṣa dīpaḥ* ("this is the lamp") to the deity's mula mantra — for Chamunda, the Navarna:

> एष दीपः … ॐ ऐं ह्रीं क्लीं चामुण्डायै विच्चे नमः।
>
> এষ দীপঃ … ওঁ ঐং হ্রীং ক্লীং চামুণ্ডায়ৈ বিচ্চে নমঃ।
>
> *eṣa dīpaḥ … oṁ aiṁ hrīṁ klīṁ cāmuṇḍāyai vicce namaḥ*

**In simple words:** This lamp — oṁ, salutation to Chamunda of the nine syllables. The same skeleton serves flowers (*etat puṣpam / eṣa puṣpāñjaliḥ*), incense (*eṣa dhūpaḥ*), and food.

Each lotus and flower is given with the *ete gandhapushpe* formula in Chamunda's name:

> एते गन्धपुष्पे ॐ ह्रीं चामुण्डायै नमः।
>
> এতে গন্ধপুষ্পে ওঁ হ্রীং চামুণ্ডায়ৈ নমঃ।
>
> *ete gandhapuṣpe oṁ hrīṁ cāmuṇḍāyai namaḥ*

**In simple words:** With these fragrant flowers — oṁ, hrīṁ, honour to Chamunda.

The congregational flower offering in Durga puja uses formulas of the same shape, ending:

> एष सचन्दनगन्धपुष्पबिल्वपत्राञ्जलिः ॐ ह्रीं दुर्गायै नमः।
>
> এষ সচন্দনগন্ধপুষ্পবিল্বপত্রাঞ্জলিঃ ওঁ হ্রীং দুর্গায়ৈ নমঃ।
>
> *eṣa sacandana-gandha-puṣpa-bilvapatrāñjaliḥ oṁ hrīṁ durgāyai namaḥ*

**In simple words:** This handful of flowers and bel leaves, with sandal paste and fragrance — oṁ, hrīṁ, honour to Durga.

And one of the standard anjali verses addresses the goddess in precisely her sandhi-puja identity:

> ॐ महिषघ्नि महामाये चामुण्डे मुण्डमालिनि।
> आयुरारोग्यविजयं देहि देवि नमोऽस्तु ते॥
>
> ওঁ মহিষঘ্নি মহামায়ে চামুণ্ডে মুণ্ডমালিনি।
> আয়ুরারোগ্যবিজয়ং দেহি দেবি নমোঽস্তু তে॥
>
> *oṁ mahiṣaghni mahāmāye cāmuṇḍe muṇḍamālini ·
> āyur-ārogya-vijayaṁ dehi devi namo'stu te*

**In simple words:** O slayer of Mahisha, great Maya, Chamunda wearing the garland of severed heads — give us long life, health and victory; we bow to you, O goddess.

Notes:

- The 108 lamps are read as light piercing the darkness of the juncture-hour, and the number 108 recurs deliberately: 108 lamps, 108 lotuses, and in many pujas a garland of 108 bel leaves. The lotuses recall the tradition that Rama offered 108 blue lotuses in his untimely autumn worship of the goddess — the *akal bodhan* story.
- Who does what: the purohit performs the dhyana, invocation and lamp offering, prompted by the *tantradharak* (তন্ত্রধারক) reading from the manual; family or samiti members light the physical lamps; if a pushpanjali is given at sandhi, everyone repeats the anjali mantras after the priest, flowers and bel leaf in cupped hands.
- Exact wording at the lamp offering varies from paddhati to paddhati; treat the formulas above as the attested pattern, and your purohit's manual as the authority on the night.

## The balidan and the close

The balidan — sugarcane, ash-gourd, pumpkin or banana in almost all pujas today — is offered at the heart of the sandhikshan, its scriptural warrant the *mahāpaśū* verse above. The rite then closes with the pranam verses — the *Jayanti mangala kali* and *Sarvamangala mangalye* salutations — whose gentleness lands differently after this chapter's fire: the same goddess, both faces, one salutation.

## Standing in the crowd

For the lay devotee, sandhi puja asks only presence and, if you wish, the Navarna held silently in the mind while the lamps burn. The dhyana, the invocation, and the lamp liturgy belong to the purohit; the lit lamps, the anjali if one is given, and the stillness belong to everyone. The tradition's counsel is simple: be there, be still, and let the hundred and eight lamps do the speaking.
