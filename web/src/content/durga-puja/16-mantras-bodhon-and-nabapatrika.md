# Mantras: Bodhon and the Nabapatrika

These are the working words of the pujo's two consecration days: Shashthi's
awakening and invitation, and Saptami's bathing and enlivening. They are the
purohit's mantras rather than the congregation's — but hearing them with
their meaning transforms those two mornings from spectacle into speech. The
ritual choreography they belong to is told in full in the
[Shashthi](07-shashthi.md) and [Saptami](08-saptami.md) chapters; the
mantras that close the visit — visarjan and the shanti chant — have their
own chapter at the other end of the shelf
([Visarjan and Shanti Mantras](19-mantras-visarjan-and-shanti.md)).

A word of honesty before the texts. The full bodhon, adhibas, and
nabapatrika liturgies live in priests' manuals — *paddhati* (পদ্ধতি) texts
such as the *Purohit Darpan* (পুরোহিত দর্পণ) — and much of that material is
not reliably available in print outside them; wording also genuinely varies
between family traditions. What follows are the verses well attested across
published sources, presented with their known variants. Where only the
*shape* of a rite is documented, we describe the shape and do not invent
the words.

## The bodhon mantra — waking her as Rama was served

Recited beneath the bel tree on Shashthi evening, this is the year's most
direct citation of the akal bodhon story: the priest names the precedent —
the untimely waking once performed for Rama's sake — and claims it for
tonight.

> ॐ ऐं रावणस्य वधार्थाय रामस्यानुग्रहाय च।
> अकाले ब्रह्मणा बोधो देव्यास्त्वयि कृतः पुरा॥
> अहमप्याश्विने षष्ठ्यां सायाह्ने बोधयाम्यहम्॥
>
> ওঁ ঐং রাবণস্য বধার্থায় রামস্যানুগ্রহায় চ।
> অকালে ব্রহ্মণা বোধো দেব্যাস্ত্বয়ি কৃতঃ পুরা॥
> অহমপ্যাশ্বিনে ষষ্ঠ্যাং সায়াহ্নে বোধয়াম্যহম্॥
>
> *oṁ aiṁ rāvaṇasya vadhārthāya rāmasyānugrahāya ca ·
> akāle brahmaṇā bodho devyās tvayi kṛtaḥ purā ·
> aham apy āśvine ṣaṣṭhyāṁ sāyāhne bodhayāmy aham*

**In simple words:** For the slaying of Ravana and as a grace to Rama, an
untimely awakening was once performed upon you, O Devi, by Brahma. So I
too, on this Shashthi evening of Ashvin, awaken you. The syllable *aiṁ* at
the head is a *bija* (বীজ), a seed-syllable of the goddess; the verse
compresses the whole charter of autumn worship into three lines — pujo in
Ashwin is *akal*, out of season, inside the gods' sleeping half-year, and
it is legitimate because it re-enacts the awakening performed before Rama's
war.

**Variants worth knowing.** A well-documented reading turns the second line
into the first person and the present moment: *akāle tu śive bodhas tava
devyāḥ kṛto mayā* — "out of season, O gracious one, this awakening of you,
Goddess, is made *by me*." The two readings mirror the two tellings of the
story: in the Puranic frame (Kalika Purana and its kin) it is Brahma who
wakes the goddess on Rama's behalf; in Krittibas's Bengali Ramayana it is
Rama himself who performs the autumn worship, and the priest reciting the
"by me" variant steps directly into that role. The closing word of the
third line also varies between printings (*bodhayāmy aham* / *bodhayāmi
vai*). Either way, the theology is identical: every bodhon since is that
first bodhon, repeated. *What was done in the epic, we do now.*

Bengali sources also record a short performative utterance at the awakening
itself, addressed to the goddess asleep in the tree:

> ऐं श्रीवृक्षे बोधयामि त्वां यावत् पूजां करोम्यहम्।
>
> ঐং শ্রীবৃক্ষে বোধয়ামি ত্বাং যাবৎ পূজাং করোম্যহম্।
>
> *aiṁ śrīvṛkṣe bodhayāmi tvāṁ yāvat pūjāṁ karomy aham*

**In simple words:** In this *shri*-tree I awaken you, for as long as I
perform your worship. Exact wording of this formula varies by paddhati.

## Amantran — inviting the bel tree

After the awakening comes *amantran* (আমন্ত্রণ) — in some manuals *bilva
nimantran*, "the invitation at the bel tree." The goddess is first honoured
in the bel itself, the *shriphala* (শ্রীফল), "tree of the fortune-fruit,"
before she is ever installed in clay. Bengali liturgical compilations
derived from the Purohit Darpan open the address to the tree like this:

> ॐ मेरुमन्दरकैलासहिमवच्छिखरे गिरौ।
> जातः श्रीफलवृक्ष त्वमम्बिकायाः सदा प्रियः॥
>
> ওঁ মেরুমন্দরকৈলাসহিমবচ্ছিখরে গিরৌ।
> জাতঃ শ্রীফলবৃক্ষ ত্বমম্বিকায়াঃ সদা প্রিয়ঃ॥
>
> *oṁ meru-mandara-kailāsa-himavac-chikhare girau ·
> jātaḥ śrīphala-vṛkṣa tvam ambikāyāḥ sadā priyaḥ*

**In simple words:** O bel tree — born on the peaks of Meru, Mandara,
Kailash and the Himalaya — you are ever dear to Ambika, the Mother. The
tree is greeted as a mountain-born being, kin to the goddess's own
Himalayan home, and therefore her natural first lodging on earth. The
liturgy speaks to the tree as a person, and asks rather than takes.

**The continuation.** In the paddhatis the address goes on — a branch is
requested of the tree, with words to the effect of "born on the holy
mountain, abode of *shri* — I must take you with me; come, and be
worshipped as a very form of Durga" (*pūjyo durgā-svarūpataḥ*). Like most
of the amantran–adhibas sequence, the exact continuation varies between
manuals, so beyond that first well-attested couplet we give the sense and
not a fixed text. What the rite does is documented beyond doubt, and the
Kalika Purana already prescribes it for the autumn worship: the goddess is
awakened in the bilva, invited to remain, and consecrated there overnight,
with the nabapatrika leaf-worship following on Saptami.

## The invitation itself — avahana

Beneath every elaborate formula sits the simplest and most universal one,
spoken with folded hands as the goddess is invited into the ghot, the tree,
and the image:

> ॐ भगवति दुर्गे देवि इहागच्छ इह तिष्ठ।
>
> ওঁ ভগবতি দুর্গে দেবি ইহাগচ্ছ ইহ তিষ্ঠ।
>
> *oṁ bhagavati durge devi ihāgaccha iha tiṣṭha*

**In simple words:** O blessed Devi Durga — come here; stay here. Every
rite of Shashthi evening is finally this one sentence, the household
sentence said to an arriving guest.

## Adhibas — the formula pattern and the dala

*Adhibas* (অধিবাস), the consecration-residence that settles the awakened
goddess into the bel branch and the ghot overnight, works by touch: the
priest takes up each auspicious article from the *adhibas dala* (অধিবাস
ডালা) in turn and touches it to the branch — and in many pujos to the ghot
and the idol's forehead — reciting for each its own short consecration
formula. Those per-item formulas are paddhati material and differ house to
house, so we do not print a reconstructed set; the *pattern* is one line
per article, naming the article and settling its auspiciousness on the
goddess, the same gesture by which a Bengali bride is blessed at her
wedding-eve adhibas.

The dala itself is old and specific, though the manuals differ on roster
and count. A classical list runs: earth (*mati* — মাটি), sandal or perfume
(*gandha* — গন্ধ), a stone (*shila* — শিলা), unhusked rice (*dhan* — ধান),
*durba* grass (দূর্বা), flowers, fruit, curd (*doi* — দই), ghee (*ghrita* —
ঘি), the *swastika* mark, vermilion (*sindoor* — সিঁদুর), a conch shell
(*shankha* — শঙ্খ), collyrium (*kajal* — কাজল), *rochana* (রোচনা — a yellow
sacred pigment), white mustard seed (*shwet sarisha* — শ্বেত সরিষা), gold,
silver, copper, a lamp (*pradip* — প্রদীপ), and a mirror (*darpan* —
দর্পণ). Many Bengali lists add turmeric, the yak-tail whisk (*chamor* —
চামর), *alta*, a garland or *chandmala*, and iron. Twenty articles in one
pujo, twenty-six in another — both follow genuine manuals. The grammar of
the list is the point: earth and stone for steadfastness, grain and grass
for abundance, curd and ghee for nourishment, metals for permanence,
sindoor and alta and mirror and kajal for the adornment of the married
daughter, the lamp for light, mustard against evil, the chamor for
royalty. It is everything a mother's household would gather to bless a
bride.

## The nabapatrika's mantra

At Saptami's dawn bath and in the worship after it, the nine-plant goddess
is addressed with her own collective name:

> ॐ नवपत्रिकावासिन्यै नवदुर्गायै नमः।
>
> ওঁ নবপত্রিকাবাসিন্যৈ নবদুর্গায়ৈ নমঃ।
>
> *oṁ navapatrikā-vāsinyai navadurgāyai namaḥ*

**In simple words:** Salutation to the ninefold Durga who dwells in the
nine plants. This is the one nabapatrika formula printed consistently
across public sources. Behind it, each of the nine plants is honoured as a
goddess in her own right:

| Plant (Bengali name) | Ritual Sanskrit name | Presiding goddess |
|---|---|---|
| Kola — কলা (banana/plantain) | *rambhā* | Brahmani (ব্রহ্মাণী) |
| Kochu — কচু (colocasia/taro) | *kacu* | Kalika (কালিকা) |
| Holud — হলুদ (turmeric) | *haridrā* | Durga (দুর্গা) herself — some sources name Uma (উমা) |
| Jayanti — জয়ন্তী (a *Sesbania* tree) | *jayantī* | Kartiki (কার্তিকী) |
| Bel — বেল (wood-apple) | *bilva* | Shiva (শিবা), the goddess as Shiva's consort |
| Dalim — ডালিম (pomegranate) | *dāḍimba* | Raktadantika (রক্তদন্তিকা), "she of the blood-red teeth" |
| Ashok — অশোক (ashoka tree) | *aśoka* | Shokarahita (শোকরহিতা), "she who removes sorrow" |
| Maan — মান (arum) | *māna* | Chamunda (চামুণ্ডা) |
| Dhan — ধান (rice paddy) | *dhānya* | Lakshmi (লক্ষ্মী) |

Several of these names — Brahmani, Kalika, Chamunda — belong to the
*matrikas*, the ancient mother-goddesses of the Devi Mahatmya's battle
scenes; Raktadantika appears in the Devi Mahatmya's own list of the Devi's
future forms. In the paddhatis each of the nine has her own dhyana and
invocation lines for the Saptami worship; those lines are transmitted in
the manuals and are not reliably published, so we give the mapping and not
reconstructed verses.

## The kola bou snan — the bathing liturgy

Before sunrise on Saptami the bundle — eight plants tied to the banana
plant's trunk with the *aparajita* creeper and yellow thread — is carried
in procession to the water, bathed, dressed in a red-bordered sari,
touched with sindoor, and installed to the right of Ganesh. That
choreography, and the affectionate "Ganesher bou" misnomer, are told in
the [Saptami chapter](08-saptami.md). What belongs here is the shape of
the bathing liturgy itself.

The classical prescription is astonishingly orchestral: each of the nine
plant-goddesses is to be bathed with her own water — the manuals specify
waters gathered from different holy sources — to her own mantra, and even
to her own music, the old texts assigning particular ragas and particular
drum-beats to particular pourings. Published accounts of the full roster
mention, for example, lotus-pollen water poured to the Gaur raga, spring
water to the Barari raga with conch, and water of all the tirthas to the
Basanta raga — a royal abhisheka performed on a bundle of kitchen-garden
plants. The nine per-goddess bathing mantras themselves, like the dhyana
lines, live in the paddhatis; public sources document that they exist and
how the rite is scored, not their full text, and we leave it at that
honest limit. In practice most pujos today perform a simpler immersion and
sprinkling — with Ganga water if the water body is not the Ganga herself —
the priest reciting what his lineage's manual gives him, and closing with
the *navapatrikā-vāsinyai* salutation above.

## Pran pratishtha — calling the breath into clay

Back at the pandal, the rite that makes the image a presence uses, in most
paddhatis, a form of the ancient establishing-formula of the *prāṇāḥ
pratiṣṭhantu* family, spoken with the priest's hand — or a blade of kusha
grass, or a flower — on the protima's heart:

> ॐ अस्यै प्राणाः प्रतिष्ठन्तु अस्यै प्राणाः क्षरन्तु च।
> अस्यै देवत्वमर्चायै मामहेति च कश्चन॥
>
> ওঁ অস্যৈ প্রাণাঃ প্রতিষ্ঠন্তু অস্যৈ প্রাণাঃ ক্ষরন্তু চ।
> অস্যৈ দেবত্বমর্চায়ৈ মামহেতি চ কশ্চন॥
>
> *oṁ asyai prāṇāḥ pratiṣṭhantu asyai prāṇāḥ kṣarantu ca ·
> asyai devatvam arcāyai māmaheti ca kaścana*

**In simple words:** May the life-breaths be established in her; may the
life-breaths flow in her; may divinity come to this image for worship.
This core is the publicly attested skeleton of the rite. The full liturgy
that surrounds it — the chains of seed-syllables, the *nyasa* by which the
priest first installs the goddess limb by limb in his own body, the
breath-control, the inner offering of the goddess from his own heart-lotus
into the clay, and the ritual awakening of the eyes in *chakshu dan* — is
priest-only, esoteric, and transmitted in manuals rather than on websites,
so here we describe its intent and do not pretend to quote it. It asks
that all her senses — speech, mind, sight, hearing — arrive and dwell in
happiness for as long as the worship endures. It is the moment after
which, as the artisans' saying goes, you lower your voice around her.

## A note on hearing these mantras

At most pandals these words are half-audible under the dhak — and that is
fine. The tradition's own understanding is that the congregation
participates by presence and assent, the priest by utterance. But stand
close on Shashthi evening once, with this chapter's meanings in mind, and
the pujo's grandest idea becomes plain: before a single light was strung,
somebody *asked* — asked the earth, asked a tree, asked the goddess
herself — and everything that follows, down to the farewell words of
[Dashami](19-mantras-visarjan-and-shanti.md), is her consent.
