## Who is coming

Before the first drumbeat, it is worth knowing exactly who is coming.

This book is about a festival, and the festival is about a person. Bengal knows her three ways at once: as the war-goddess of an ancient Sanskrit scripture, born of the pooled fire of all the gods to kill a demon no god could touch; as Uma, a married daughter far from home, whose four-day visit to her parents is the emotional engine of the whole season; and, underneath both, as the generous earth itself, thanked at harvest. Holding the invincible and the intimate together — the trident and the tears — is the particular genius of the Bengali pujo, and it begins with her story. So we begin there too.

## The book beneath the festival

The story that stands under everything is told in the **Devi Mahatmya** (দেবীমাহাত্ম্য) — "The Glory of the Goddess" — a Sanskrit text of seven hundred mantras embedded in the Markandeya Purana, composed somewhere around the fifth to seventh century CE; an inscription of 608 CE already quotes it. It is also called the **Durga Saptashati**, "the seven hundred of Durga," and it is the oldest complete scripture in the Hindu tradition in which ultimate reality — the power that creates, sustains and dissolves the universe — is a woman. Bengalis call it simply the **Chandi** (চণ্ডী), and its ritual recitation, the *Chandipath*, runs through the festival from Mahalaya's dawn broadcast (see [Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md)) to the readings at the pandal on Ashtami. In a Chandipath every utterance of the text counts as a mantra with power of its own — even "the Goddess said." The text gets its own full chapter later in this book ([the Chandi](20-mantras-the-chandi.md)); here we need its stories.

The thirteen chapters are themselves a story told inside a story. A king named **Suratha** (সুরথ), defeated by enemies and betrayed by his own ministers, rides alone into a forest. There he meets a merchant, **Samadhi** (সমাধি), thrown out of his house by the wife and sons who wanted his money. Both men discover the same absurdity in themselves: they cannot stop loving and worrying about the very people who ruined them. They put the question to the forest sage **Medhas** (মেধস): why do even those who *know better* behave like this? The sage answers that it is no personal failing — it is **Mahamaya**, the Great Illusion, the Goddess herself, who hurls even the minds of the wise into attachment, and who alone, when gracious, grants release. The whole Devi Mahatmya is his answer unfolding: three tales — the tradition calls them *charitas* — of who this Goddess is. At the end, king and merchant worship her for three years on a riverbank with an image made of earth — which Bengali tradition has always heard as the first clay protima — and each receives what he actually wanted: the king his kingdom back (and a future birth as Savarni, the eighth Manu), the merchant the knowledge that cuts attachment. The text refuses to rank the man who wanted the world above the man who wanted out of it.

## The first tale: Madhu and Kaitabha

In the first and shortest tale the Goddess never lifts a weapon. At the dissolution of the world, Vishnu sleeps on the serpent Shesha upon a single ocean; two demons, Madhu and Kaitabha, spring from the wax of his ears and rush to kill Brahma, who sits on the lotus of Vishnu's navel. Brahma, unable to wake the sleeper, does the only thing left: he praises the Goddess *as* the sleep itself — Yoganidra, the cosmic slumber lying over Vishnu. She consents and withdraws; Vishnu wakes and grapples with the demons for five thousand years. Drunk on their own strength — and befuddled by Mahamaya — they offer him a boon; he asks for their death; they stipulate, cunningly, that it happen only "where the earth is not covered by water," so Vishnu lifts them onto his own broad thigh and beheads them there. The lesson of the first tale is the Goddess's strangest power: she is the force *behind* every other force, the one who must move before even Vishnu can act.

## The demon with the perfect boon

The middle tale is the one the festival enacts. **Mahishasura** (মহিষাসুর), "the buffalo demon," son of the asura Rambha, performed austerities so severe that Brahma appeared and offered him a boon. Mahishasura asked for immortality and was refused — all that is born must die. So he crafted what he believed was the perfect loophole: let no god, demon, or man be able to kill him; let death come, if it must come, only from a woman. To an asura who had never met a woman who fought, this was immortality with extra steps. Boon secured, he did what boon-holders in these stories always do: made war on heaven. For a hundred years the gods fought him and lost. Mahisha took Indra's throne and set himself up as lord of the three worlds; the dispossessed gods walked the earth like mortals.

## A goddess assembled from light

The gods brought their story to Vishnu and Shiva, and as the tale of the demon's insolence was told, anger blazed out of the whole assembly as light — *tej* or *tejas* (তেজ), fiery radiance, is the text's word. From Vishnu's face, from Shiva's, from Brahma's, from the bodies of Indra and all the devas, streams of brilliance poured out, flowed together into a single mountain of flame, and condensed into the form of a woman — built limb by limb from the powers of the entire pantheon: her face from Shiva's light, her hair from Yama's, her arms from Vishnu's, her waist from Indra's, her eyes from Agni's, her feet from Brahma's, and so on, feature by feature. The Chandi's own verse for the moment:

> अतुलं तत्र तत्तेजः सर्वदेवशरीरजम् ।
> एकस्थं तदभून्नारी व्याप्तलोकत्रयं त्विषा ॥
>
> অতুলং তত্র তত্তেজঃ সর্বদেবশরীরজম্ ।
> একস্থং তদভূন্নারী ব্যাপ্তলোকত্রয়ং ত্বিষা ॥
>
> *atulaṁ tatra tat tejaḥ sarva-deva-śarīrajam,*
> *ekasthaṁ tad abhūn nārī vyāpta-loka-trayaṁ tviṣā.*

**In simple words:** that matchless blaze, born from the bodies of all the gods, gathered into one place — and became a woman, flooding the three worlds with her light.

Notice the direction of the theology, because the text is careful here. She is not a goddess made *by* the gods, assembled as their weapon. Their pooled power simply *is* her, momentarily visible: she is the primordial power, **Adi Shakti**, who existed before them all, whom the gods' light merely revealed. Later in the scripture she will say it herself, in its most important line: *"I am alone in this universe — who else is there beside me?"*

## The armoury of heaven

Then each god armed her from his own attribute — the scene every Bengali child has heard narrated over the radio in the dark of Mahalaya morning. As the Devi Mahatmya's second chapter lists the gifts (tellings vary slightly in detail): **Shiva** drew a trident from his own trident; **Vishnu** spun a discus from his discus; **Varuna** gave a conch and his noose; **Agni** a spear; **Vayu** a bow and two quivers that never empty; **Indra** a thunderbolt from his own vajra, and a bell from his elephant Airavata; **Yama** a staff from his rod of death; **Brahma** his water-pot and rosary; **Surya** filled her pores with his own rays; **Kala** — Time itself — gave a sword and a shining shield; the **ocean of milk** gave garments and jewels; **Vishwakarma**, architect of the gods, an axe, armour and ornaments; **Kubera** a cup of wine; the serpent-king **Shesha** a garland of snakes; and **Himavat**, lord of mountains — her grandfather, in the Bengali telling — gave her a lion to ride.

Ten arms — in Bengal she is *Dashabhuja* (দশভুজা), the ten-armed — each holding a gift, so that when she rides into battle, every power in the universe rides with her. That is the quiet theology of the image in the pandal: everything the gods had, given to her, because the power behind it all was hers to begin with. Then she laughed — a laugh that shook the three worlds and made the seas boil — and the war began.

## Nine nights of battle, and the tenth day

Mahishasura's generals came first — Chikshura, Chamara and the rest — and fell with their uncountable armies, the Goddess multiplying her own forces from her breath. When the demon king took the field himself, he fought as his nature allowed: shifting shape. A buffalo, storming her hosts, tossing mountains with his horns and lashing the ocean apart with his tail; snared by her noose, he abandoned the buffalo and became a lion; beheaded, a swordsman; cut down, a great elephant dragging her lion with his trunk; the trunk severed, the buffalo once more. The Devi, the text says, paused — laughed, drank from Kubera's cup, her eyes reddening — and told him: roar, fool, roar for a moment more, while I drink; when I have slain you, it is the gods who will roar. Then she leapt, pinned the buffalo's neck under her foot, and drove her spear — in the familiar iconography, the trident — through him; and as Mahishasura strained to emerge from the buffalo's mouth in his true form — half-out, sword in hand, caught forever in the moment of transformation, exactly as every Bengali idol shows him — she beheaded him.

The battle, tradition says, lasted nine days and nights — the nine nights the wider Hindu world keeps as Navaratri — and the victory belongs to the tenth: **Bijoya Dashami**, literally "the victorious tenth," the day the pujo ends. She is **Mahishasuramardini** (মহিষাসুরমর্দিনী), "the crusher of Mahishasura," and that killing frame — the goddess calm and almost gentle-faced above the violence, the lion tearing the buffalo, the demon emerging green-bodied from the severed neck — is precisely the tableau of every Bengali protima. The gods rained flowers and hymns, offered her a boon, and asked only this: that she return whenever they remember her in distress. She promised. The pre-dawn recitation of Mahalaya is, formally, the community holding her to that promise.

## The third tale: Shumbha, Nishumbha, and the goddesses within the Goddess

The final tale is the longest and strangest, and it seeds several later chapters of this book, so it belongs here at least in outline. New tyrants, the demon brothers **Shumbha and Nishumbha**, seize the three worlds; the stripped gods climb to the Himalaya and sing the Chandi's most famous litany — *Ya devi sarvabhuteshu* — to the Goddess who dwells in all beings. As they sing, Parvati arrives to bathe in the Ganga, asks whom they are praising, and from the sheath (*kosha*) of her own body a radiant goddess steps out — called **Kaushiki** for that reason, while Parvati, left dark, becomes Kalika. Shumbha, collector of all the universe's treasures, hears of her unbearable beauty and sends a proposal of marriage: choose me or my brother. She answers with mock regret that she once made a foolish vow — she can only marry the one who defeats her in battle. Let him come, then, and win her.

What follows is a rising ladder of battles. The general Dhumralochana — "Smoke-Eye" — is turned to ash with a single syllable of contempt. Then come **Chanda and Munda**; at the sight of them the Goddess's face darkens with wrath — the text says it turned ink-black — and from her knotted brow springs **Kali**: gaunt, terrible, skull-garlanded, tongue lolling, filling the directions with her roar. Kali devours the army, beheads the two generals, and delivers their heads as battle-trophies; and the Goddess names her for the deed — *Chamunda*, "she who took Chanda and Munda." Bengal has built its most electric ritual on that exact moment: the Sandhi Puja at the juncture of Ashtami and Nabami worships precisely this form, at this instant of the story. Then comes the nightmare demon **Raktabija**, "Blood-Seed," from whose every spilled drop of blood a full copy of himself springs up — until Kali spreads her tongue over the battlefield and drinks every drop before it lands. Nishumbha falls, a second self climbing from his pierced chest still shouting "Stop!"; and when Shumbha, alone, sneers that she fights puffed up with borrowed strength — all these other goddesses — she gives the reply that is the theological summit of the entire scripture: *I am alone in this universe; who else is there beside me?* — and Kali, the Mothers, every emanation walks back into her body, and she stands single, and finishes it.

The litany the gods sang deserves its verse here, because it is the passage every Bengali knows by heart from the Mahalaya broadcast, and because it says what the whole third tale proves — that she is not only in the pandal:

> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु शक्तिरूपेण संस्थिता ।
> नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमो नमः ॥
>
> যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু শক্তিরূপেণ সংস্থিতা ।
> নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমো নমঃ ॥
>
> *yā devī sarva-bhūteṣu śakti-rūpeṇa saṁsthitā,*
> *namas tasyai namas tasyai namas tasyai namo namaḥ.*

**In simple words:** to the goddess who lives in every being as strength itself — we bow to her, we bow to her, we bow to her, again and again. The full litany finds her in all beings as consciousness, intelligence, sleep, hunger, shadow, thirst, patience, modesty, peace, faith, beauty, fortune, memory, compassion, contentment, motherhood — even error. Bowing to her is bowing to that.

## Reading the protima like a text

Everything you will see in the pandal comes out of a verse. Before each day's worship the priest closes his eyes and recites the **dhyana** (ধ্যান) — a meditation mantra of thirteen verses from the Bengali priestly manuals that builds the goddess detail by detail in the mind before she is worshipped in clay. Its opening lines:

> ॐ जटाजूटसमायुक्तामर्द्धेन्दुकृतशेखराम् ।
> लोचनत्रयसंयुक्तां पूर्णेन्दुसदृशाननाम् ॥
> अतसीपुष्पवर्णाभां सुप्रतिष्ठां सुलोचनाम् ।
> नवयौवनसम्पन्नां सर्वाभरणभूषिताम् ॥
> सुचारुदशनां तद्वत् पीनोन्नतपयोधराम् ।
> त्रिभङ्गस्थानसंस्थानां महिषासुरमर्दिनीम् ॥
>
> ওঁ জটাজূটসমাযুক্তামর্দ্ধেন্দুকৃতশেখরাম্ ।
> লোচনত্রয়সংযুক্তাং পূর্ণেন্দুসদৃশাননাম্ ॥
> অতসীপুষ্পবর্ণাভাং সুপ্রতিষ্ঠাং সুলোচনাম্ ।
> নবযৌবনসম্পন্নাং সর্বাভরণভূষিতাম্ ॥
> সুচারুদশনাং তদ্বৎ পীনোন্নতপয়োধরাম্ ।
> ত্রিভঙ্গস্থানসংস্থানাং মহিষাসুরমর্দিনীম্ ॥
>
> *oṁ jaṭājūṭa-samāyuktām arddhendu-kṛta-śekharām,*
> *locana-traya-saṁyuktāṁ pūrṇendu-sadṛśānanām.*
> *atasī-puṣpa-varṇābhāṁ supratiṣṭhāṁ sulocanām,*
> *nava-yauvana-sampannāṁ sarvābharaṇa-bhūṣitām.*
> *sucāru-daśanāṁ tadvat pīnonnata-payodharām,*
> *tribhaṅga-sthāna-saṁsthānāṁ mahiṣāsura-mardinīm.*

**In simple words:** meditate on her whose piled, matted hair is crowned with the half-moon; who has three eyes and a face like the full moon; whose skin glows like the flax flower; who stands firm and beautiful, in the fullness of youth, adorned with every ornament — her body flexed in the graceful three-bend stance even as she destroys Mahishasura. The verses go on to place each weapon in each of her ten hands, the beheaded buffalo below, the demon emerging sword in hand, the lion mid-kill, her right foot on the lion and her left toe on the buffalo — and, above the whole battle, her face serene, granting every wish. The murti is this mantra in clay. (A small variant worth knowing: for her complexion, some manuals read *atasī-puṣpa-varṇābhāṁ*, "lustrous as the flax flower," and others *tapta-kāñcana-varṇābhāṁ*, "glowing like molten gold" — the second matches the golden-skinned Bengali Durga, traditionally likened also to ripe wheat.)

So read the image like the text it is. **The ten arms** are protection flung out in all ten directions at once — no quarter of the world she cannot reach. **The weapons** carry a traditional symbolism, told with small variations from teller to teller: the trident's three points for the three qualities of nature (or creation, preservation and destruction); the sword for knowledge that cuts through ignorance; the discus for the wheel of time and dharma; the conch for the primal sound; the bow and arrow for control of energy — potential and release in one grip; the thunderbolt for unbreakable resolve; the noose and goad for the catching and steering of the ego; the lotus for awakening rising unstained out of mud; the bell for the sound that scatters demons. **The lion** is dharma and courage — will, mastered and ridden. **Mahishasura underfoot**, forever half-emerged from the buffalo, is the shape-shifting ego that must be pinned before anything good can stand — and her face above the violence is not angry but calm, almost tender, because the Chandi insists that even her killing is grace. **Her eyes** are elongated like fish, and by long custom they are painted last of all, in the rite of *chokkhu dan* — the "gift of eyes," traditionally on Mahalaya morning ([Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md) tells that story).

The traditional Bengali frame is the **ek-chala** (একচালা): one backdrop, one arch, the whole family under a single roof. Durga stands at centre astride the lion; flanking her are her four children — **Lakshmi** with her owl, goddess of wealth and grace; **Saraswati** with her swan, goddess of learning and the arts; **Kartik** with his peacock, the beautiful young warrior-god; **Ganesh** with his mouse, the elephant-headed remover of obstacles under whose blessing all beginnings are made (beside him, from Saptami, stands the little *kola bou*, the banana-plant bride — a later chapter's story). Painted or moulded at the top of the chala, small and watchful, is **Shiva** — the husband from whose Himalayan home she has come. Prosperity, wisdom, valour, auspicious beginnings, gathered around protective power: the ek-chala is a complete curriculum. It is also, unmistakably, a family portrait — which brings us to the story Bengal added.

## Uma, and the daughter who comes home

The Sanskrit texts give her a hundred names. Two matter most in Bengal.

**Durga** (দুর্গা) is the warrior of the Chandi. The name is usually read from *durga*, "fortress" — "the one hard to reach, the inaccessible" — or, as devotion prefers, "she who removes *durgati*, distress." The Chandi itself adds its own story: among her promised future deeds is the slaying of a demon named Durgama, "whereby," she says, "my name shall be Durga."

**Uma** (উমা) — also Parvati, also Gauri, "the fair one" — is the same goddess in her other key: daughter of Himalaya the mountain-king and his queen **Menoka**, married to the great ascetic Shiva and living far away on Mount Kailash. Bengal, with characteristic domestic genius, fused the two: the cosmic victory festival is *also* the few days each autumn when Uma comes home to her parents' house, bringing her four children. The whole emotional weather of the pujo flows from this. The **agomoni** (আগমনী) songs sung in the weeks before are Menoka's songs of waiting — dreaming of her daughter, scolding her husband Giriraj into going to Kailash to fetch her; the drums of Saptami are the sound of her arrival; the sindoor and tears of Dashami are a mother sending her daughter back to her husband's house. Her stay is counted from the Shashthi-evening welcome to the Dashami farewell — the four days of the pujo proper — and its shortness is not a liturgical accident but the story's very point: daughters' visits are always too short. Every probasi family, in Pune as in New Jersey, knows exactly what that feels like — which is why this frame, of all frames, owns the Bengali heart. For four days the mother of the universe consents to be somebody's daughter, and every home she is welcomed into becomes, briefly, her parents' house.

## Akal bodhon — worship out of season

Ask why Durga Puja happens in autumn — in Sharat, the month of Ashvin — and you reach the festival's most Bengali idea. In the classical reckoning the gods' "day" runs through the northern half of the year, and spring is the natural season for the Devi's worship: the older **Basanti Puja** (বাসন্তী পূজা), still performed in Chaitra by some families. Autumn falls in the gods' "night"; worship then requires waking a sleeping goddess.

The Bengali Ramayana of **Krittibas** (কৃত্তিবাস, fifteenth century) supplies the story. Rama, on the eve of battle with Ravana, needs the Devi's blessing and cannot wait for spring. So he performs **akal bodhon** (অকালবোধন) — the "untimely awakening" — rousing the goddess in Ashvin with an offering of a hundred and eight blue lotuses. When he finds himself one lotus short, he prepares to offer one of his own lotus-like eyes in its place; the Devi stays his hand, satisfied by the devotion itself. (In the Krittibas telling it is actually the gods, through Brahma, who first wake her so that she may aid Rama — which is why the bodhon mantra recited on Shashthi evening says "as Brahma once woke you for Rama's sake…") Every autumn pujo since is, formally, a repetition of that emergency awakening. This is why Shashthi's rituals are not an invitation so much as a waking, and why the whole autumn festival carries, under its joy, a note of urgency: this is worship summoned for a fight — the blessing you ask for when the battle is tomorrow and spring is too far away.

## How Bengal made it the greatest festival

The Chandi itself contains the festival's charter. In its twelfth chapter, prescribing her own liturgy, the Goddess singles out one occasion above all:

> शरत्काले महापूजा क्रियते या च वार्षिकी ।
> तस्यां ममैतन्माहात्म्यं श्रुत्वा भक्तिसमन्वितः ॥
> सर्वाबाधाविनिर्मुक्तो धनधान्यसमन्वितः ।
> मनुष्यो मत्प्रसादेन भविष्यति न संशयः ॥
>
> শরৎকালে মহাপূজা ক্রিয়তে যা চ বার্ষিকী ।
> তস্যাং মমৈতন্মাহাত্ম্যং শ্রুত্বা ভক্তিসমন্বিতঃ ॥
> সর্বাবাধাবিনির্মুক্তো ধনধান্যসমন্বিতঃ ।
> মনুষ্যো মৎপ্রসাদেন ভবিষ্যতি ন সংশয়ঃ ॥
>
> *śarat-kāle mahāpūjā kriyate yā ca vārṣikī,*
> *tasyāṁ mamaitan māhātmyaṁ śrutvā bhakti-samanvitaḥ;*
> *sarvābādhā-vinirmukto dhana-dhānya-samanvitaḥ,*
> *manuṣyo mat-prasādena bhaviṣyati na saṁśayaḥ.*

**In simple words:** at the great annual worship performed in autumn, whoever listens to this glory of mine with devotion will, by my grace, be freed of every trouble and filled with wealth and grain — have no doubt of it. *Sharatkale mahapuja*, "the great autumn worship": for Bengal, that is this festival — and notice that the promised blessing is *dhana-dhanya*, wealth **and grain**. The harvest was in the text all along.

The autumn Durga Puja as a grand public event is documented from the sixteenth century. Tradition credits an early lavish celebration to **Raja Kangshanarayan of Taherpur** — in Rajshahi, in what is now Bangladesh — with landholding houses of both Bengals following: Nadia, Hooghly, Dhaka, Bikrampur, Mymensingh. Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the pujo was above all a **zamindari** spectacle. The great houses of Kolkata — Shobhabazar Rajbari most famously, whose celebrated pujo of 1757 was mounted by Raja Nabakrishna Deb in the year of Plassey, with the East India Company's officers among the guests — and the *bonedi bari* mansions of the countryside competed in splendour, with ordinary people as spectators at someone else's festival.

Two inventions democratised it. Around 1790 at **Guptipara** in Hooghly, twelve friends, slighted at a household pujo, collected public subscriptions and ran their own — the first **barowari** (বারোয়ারি, "of twelve friends") puja. And in the early twentieth century Kolkata's neighbourhoods took the idea further with the **sarbojanin** (সর্বজনীন, "of all people") puja — Bagbazar from 1919 is the emblem — community worship funded by everyone, open to everyone, soon entwined with the nationalist movement's spirit of collective self-assertion. After Partition in 1947, as the zamindari order ended and refugee neighbourhoods multiplied, the sarbojanin format became *the* Bengali pujo. Kolkata alone now hosts thousands of them; and in December 2021, UNESCO inscribed "Durga Puja in Kolkata" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — the drummers, idol-makers, pandal-builders and neighbourhood committees of one city standing in, on the world's ledger, for a festival that belongs to all Bengalis everywhere.

### The pujo in East Bengal — today's Bangladesh

For Durga Puja is not a West Bengal possession that Bangladesh borrows; the festival's roots run straight through East Bengal. Taherpur, where tradition places the first grand pujo, is in Rajshahi. Dhaka's **Dhakeshwari temple** — "the goddess of Dhaka," from whom the city arguably takes its name, founded by tradition in the twelfth century under the Sena kings — remains the national centre of the celebration. Every autumn Bangladesh raises tens of thousands of mandaps across the country, hundreds in Dhaka alone; the days of the pujo are a national holiday, and Muslim, Buddhist and Christian neighbours routinely share in the festival. Families displaced by Partition carried their household deities, their *paddhati* and their protima styles west with them, and many a Kolkata or probasi pujo still follows "the way it was done in our village in Barisal." When this book says "Bengali Durga Puja," it means the whole of Bengal — Epar and Opar, Kolkata and Dhaka — and everywhere Bengalis have carried the festival since.

### The harvest underneath

One more layer, older than either the scripture or the history, runs beneath both. Sharat is the season after the monsoon — washed skies, white *kash* grass along the rivers, the paddy standing heavy in the fields — and under the Sanskrit liturgy the pujo has always kept the shape of an agrarian thanksgiving. The nine plants of the *nabapatrika* bathed at dawn on Saptami, the paddy and durba grass of every blessing, the full-moon Lakshmi of the granary who closes the season a few nights after Dashami — these are the earth being thanked at harvest, in the same breath that heaven is being defended. The pujo has always been able to hold both readings at once, which is one more reason it holds so many people.

## Words to know

- *Devi Mahatmya / Chandi* (চণ্ডী) — the 700-mantra scripture of the Goddess's victories, embedded in the Markandeya Purana; Bengal's most-recited sacred text. Also called *Durga Saptashati*.
- *charita* (চরিত) — one of the Chandi's three tales: Madhu-Kaitabha; Mahishasura; Shumbha-Nishumbha.
- *Mahamaya* (মহামায়া) — the Goddess as the Great Illusion that binds every mind, and the grace that frees it.
- *Adi Shakti* (আদি শক্তি) — the primordial power; the Devi as source, not creation, of the gods.
- *tej / tejas* (তেজ) — fiery radiance; the pooled tej of all the gods took form as the Goddess.
- *Mahishasura* (মহিষাসুর) — the shape-shifting buffalo demon; *Mahishasuramardini* (মহিষাসুরমর্দিনী), his crusher.
- *Dashabhuja* (দশভুজা) — "the ten-armed," Bengal's name for Durga bearing the gods' gifts.
- *Kaushiki, Kali, Chamunda, Raktabija* — figures of the Chandi's third tale, behind Sandhi Puja and the imagery of Kali.
- *Uma / Gauri / Parvati* — the Goddess as Himalaya and Menoka's daughter and Shiva's wife; Bengal's homecoming daughter.
- *agomoni* (আগমনী) — the song-genre of the mother waiting for Uma's arrival.
- *akal bodhon* (অকালবোধন) — the "untimely awakening" of the Goddess in autumn, first performed for Rama's sake.
- *Basanti Puja* (বাসন্তী পূজা) — the older spring worship of the same Goddess, still kept by some families in Chaitra.
- *dhyana* (ধ্যান) — the meditation verse that paints the deity in words before worship; the murti is its portrait in clay.
- *ek-chala* (একচালা) — the single-frame protima: Durga, her four children, and Shiva above, under one arch.
- *bonedi bari* (বনেদি বাড়ি) — the old aristocratic households whose family pujas predate the community format.
- *barowari / sarbojanin* (বারোয়ারি / সর্বজনীন) — community pujas by public subscription: "of twelve friends" / "of all people."
- *Bijoya Dashami* (বিজয়া দশমী) — "the victorious tenth," the day of the Goddess's triumph and her farewell.
