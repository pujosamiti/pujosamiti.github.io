## Where these fit

Every Durga Puja closes the way it opened: with words spoken at a doorway. On Shashthi evening the goddess was awakened out of season, invited, and given lodging — that liturgy of *bodhon* (বোধন), *amantran* (আমন্ত্রণ), and *adhibas* (অধিবাস) has its own chapter (see [the bodhon and nabapatrika mantras](16-mantras-bodhon-and-nabapatrika.md)). Four days later, on *Bijoya Dashami* (বিজয়া দশমী), the same priestly voice speaks the mirror-image liturgy: *visarjan* (বিসর্জন), the "release." The morning's *dashami puja* — the goddess's final worship — is performed and declared complete; the farewell verse is spoken over her reflected face at the *darpan visarjan* (দর্পণ বিসর্জন); the whole invited company of gods is dismissed with courtesy; in the afternoon she is honoured once more as *Aparajita* (অপরাজিতা), the Unconquered; and at the very end the gathering bows its heads under a sprinkle of *shanti jal* (শান্তিজল), peace-water, consecrated with the oldest peace-prayer in the tradition. The full choreography of the day — the procession, the sindur khela, the immersion itself — is in [the Dashami chapter](11-dashami.md); this chapter gives the words.

A note of honesty before the texts. The complete visarjan liturgy lives in priests' manuals — *paddhati* (পদ্ধতি) texts such as the *Purohit Darpan* (পুরোহিত দর্পণ) — and much of that material is not reliably available in public sources; wording also genuinely varies between family traditions. What follows are the verses that are well attested across published sources, presented with their known variants. Where only the shape of a rite is documented, we describe the shape and do not invent the words.

## What is being released — the installed presence, remembered

To feel what visarjan undoes, remember what Saptami morning did. Between the opening and closing texts of the festival stands *pran pratishtha* (প্রাণপ্রতিষ্ঠা), the "establishing of life-breath" — the rite that turned clay into presence. It is priest-only, esoteric, and transmitted in manuals rather than on websites, so we describe rather than quote; but its documented elements are these. The priest performs *nyasa* (ন্যাস) — touching seed-syllables onto the parts of his own body and then the image's, mapping divinity limb by limb. He recites an installation formula built from a chain of *bija* syllables followed by the *prāṇāḥ pratiṣṭhantu* family of phrases — "may the life-breaths of this deity be established here; may her *jiva* stand here; may all her senses — speech, mind, sight, hearing — arrive here and dwell in happiness for long." The rite culminates in *chakshu dan* (চক্ষুদান), the giving of eyes, after which the image is no longer a sculpture but the living Mother. That is the honest limit of what public sources give: the formula's skeleton and its intent, not a recitable text — and reciting it is, in any case, precisely the priest's office.

Visarjan is that gift returned. The breath ceremonially invited in must be ceremonially seen out — which is why the immersion of the clay is, ritually speaking, an afterthought. The real departure happens in words, and in a mirror.

## The great farewell — "gaccha gaccha," go and come again

At the heart of the visarjan stands one of the most quoted verses in all of Bengali ritual — a farewell that is also an invitation back.

> ॐ गच्छ गच्छ परं स्थानं स्वस्थानं परमेश्वरि ।
> संवत्सरव्यतीते तु पुनरागमनाय च ॥
>
> ওঁ গচ্ছ গচ্ছ পরং স্থানং স্বস্থানং পরমেশ্বরি ।
> সংবৎসরব্যতীতে তু পুনরাগমনায় চ ॥
>
> *oṃ gaccha gaccha paraṃ sthānaṃ svasthānaṃ parameśvari |
> saṃvatsara-vyatīte tu punar āgamanāya ca ||*

**In simple words:** "Om. Go, go to the highest place — to your own abode, O Supreme Lady; and, when a year has passed, come again." The doubled imperative *gaccha gaccha* is tender rather than brusque — the gentle urging with which one finally releases a guest who really must leave. All the weight rests on the last quarter-verse: *punar āgamanāya ca*, "and for coming again." The farewell is granted, every Bengali will tell you, only on that condition — a legal loophole of love written straight into the liturgy.

**Variants — preserved, because they matter.** The second line is also widely printed as *saṃvatsare vyatīte tu* (সংবৎসরে ব্যতীতে তু / संवत्सरे व्यतीते तु) — "when the year has passed" as two words rather than one compound; the sense is identical and both readings circulate. More substantively, the old Bengali encyclopedia *Vishvakosh* records the vocative as *svasthānaṃ devi caṇḍike* (স্বস্থানং দেবি চণ্ডিকে / स्वस्थानं देवि चण्डिके) — "to your own place, O Goddess Chandika" — and another reading of the second quarter as *yatra devo maheśvaraḥ* (যত্র দেবো মহেশ্বরঃ / यत्र देवो महेश्वरः) — "go to your highest place, *where the god Maheshvara is*." That variant names Kailash and Shiva outright, which is exactly how Bengal imagines the departure: Uma going back to her husband's house. Whatever the reading, this verse *is* the Bengali sentiment "আসছে বছর আবার হবে" — "it will happen again next year" — in Sanskrit, a thousand years older than the slogan shouted at every immersion. The farewell is never goodbye; it is a booking.

**Where it is spoken.** In Bengali practice the verse belongs to the *darpan visarjan*: a mirror is laid in a vessel of water so that the goddess's face appears in it, and the immersion is first performed on that reflection — the ghot's water poured over the mirror, the farewell said to the reflected face. The *Vishvakosh* describes exactly this. The presence is released ritually before the clay ever reaches the river; the procession to the water, described in [the Dashami chapter](11-dashami.md), then immerses an image that is — ritually speaking — already empty.

## The general dismissal — "let the gods depart, taking my worship"

Alongside the great farewell, priests use a broader dismissal verse, standard across Hindu puja generally and given by sources such as Drik Panchang for visarjan:

> ॐ यान्तु देवगणाः सर्वे पूजामादाय मामकीम् ।
> इष्टकामसमृद्ध्यर्थं पुनरागमनाय च ॥
>
> ওঁ যান্তু দেবগণাঃ সর্বে পূজামাদায় মামকীম্ ।
> ইষ্টকামসমৃদ্ধ্যর্থং পুনরাগমনায় চ ॥
>
> *oṃ yāntu deva-gaṇāḥ sarve pūjām ādāya māmakīm |
> iṣṭa-kāma-samṛddhy-arthaṃ punar āgamanāya ca ||*

**In simple words:** "Let all the hosts of gods depart, taking with them this worship of mine — for the flourishing of what is wished and longed for, and for coming again." In a Durga Puja this covers the whole invited company of the *ek-chala* frame and beyond — Ganesh, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Kartik, the lion, the nabapatrika, the attendant deities of every corner of the ritual space, even the demon underfoot — each released with the same courtesy shown the goddess herself, each carrying the year's offerings home. Notice that the same *punar āgamanāya ca* cadence closes this verse too: the request to come back is built into the grammar of every Hindu farewell to a deity.

**Note.** Some traditions add a plea for forgiveness — *kshama prarthana* (ক্ষমা প্রার্থনা) — before the dismissal: "forgive whatever was done wrongly or left incomplete." The element is well documented; its exact wording varies by paddhati, so we do not put words in its mouth.

## Aparajita — Dashami's verses of victory

Dashami is not only farewell; it is *Vijaya* — victory day. Before or alongside the visarjan, many traditions worship the goddess as Aparajita, "She Who Is Undefeated," often together with the blue-flowered aparajita creeper, whose blossoms are tied on the wrist or worn after the immersion — her victory carried on your pulse into the year's battles. Sources for the Vijayadashami aparajita rite give this invocation:

> हारेण तु विचित्रेण भास्वत्कनकमेखला ।
> अपराजिता भद्ररता करोतु विजयं मम ॥
>
> হারেণ তু বিচিত্রেণ ভাস্বৎকনকমেখলা ।
> অপরাজিতা ভদ্ররতা করোতু বিজয়ং মম ॥
>
> *hāreṇa tu vicitreṇa bhāsvat-kanaka-mekhalā |
> aparājitā bhadraratā karotu vijayaṃ mama ||*

**In simple words:** "May Aparajita — adorned with a wondrous necklace, girdled in gleaming gold, delighting in the welfare of her devotees — grant me victory." Spoken on the day of Durga's triumph, it asks that her victory be lent to the worshipper for the year ahead — the same logic by which kings once began their campaigns on Vijayadashami.

At its simplest, the whole rite compresses into a single salutation:

> ॐ अपराजितायै नमः ।
>
> ওঁ অপরাজিতায়ৈ নমঃ ।
>
> *oṃ aparājitāyai namaḥ*

**In simple words:** "Om. Salutation to the Unconquered One." The smallest mantra of the day is also its summary: she who leaves is she who cannot be defeated — and neither, wearing her flower, are you meant to be.

**Notes.** The aparajita rite is traditionally performed in the *aparahna* (অপরাহ্ণ) — the afternoon period — of Dashami. Distinct from these short invocations is the longer *Aparajita stuti* of the Devi Mahatmya ("yā devī sarvabhūteṣu…" and its sister verses), which some households also recite on the day; the two share a name but are different texts.

## The shanti mantra and shanti jal — peace-water at the end

After the immersion — or after the darpan visarjan, before the procession — comes the quietest moment of the whole festival. The priest dips a bel leaf or a flower into sanctified water and sprinkles it over the bowed heads of everyone present: *shanti jal*, the peace-water. The text spoken over it is the great peace-chant of the Shukla Yajurveda (36.17) — a verse that asks peace of the entire cosmos, element by element:

> ॐ द्यौः शान्तिरन्तरिक्षं शान्तिः पृथिवी शान्तिरापः शान्तिरोषधयः शान्तिः ।
> वनस्पतयः शान्तिर्विश्वेदेवाः शान्तिर्ब्रह्म शान्तिः सर्वं शान्तिः
> शान्तिरेव शान्तिः सा मा शान्तिरेधि ॥
> ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः ॥
>
> ওঁ দ্যৌঃ শান্তিরন্তরিক্ষং শান্তিঃ পৃথিবী শান্তিরাপঃ শান্তিরোষধয়ঃ শান্তিঃ ।
> বনস্পতয়ঃ শান্তির্বিশ্বেদেবাঃ শান্তির্ব্রহ্ম শান্তিঃ সর্বং শান্তিঃ
> শান্তিরেব শান্তিঃ সা মা শান্তিরেধি ॥
> ওঁ শান্তিঃ শান্তিঃ শান্তিঃ ॥
>
> *oṃ dyauḥ śāntir antarikṣaṃ śāntiḥ pṛthivī śāntir āpaḥ śāntir oṣadhayaḥ śāntiḥ |
> vanaspatayaḥ śāntir viśvedevāḥ śāntir brahma śāntiḥ sarvaṃ śāntiḥ
> śāntir eva śāntiḥ sā mā śāntir edhi ||
> oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ ||*

**In simple words:** "Om. Peace be the sky; peace the space between; peace the earth; peace the waters; peace the plants; peace the great trees; peace all the gods; peace Brahman; peace everything; peace, peace itself — and may that very peace come to me. Om, peace, peace, peace." It is less a request than a roll-call: every layer of the world, from heaven down through air, earth, water, and green things, up through the gods to the absolute, is named and declared at peace — and then that universal peace is asked to settle on the people standing there with wet foreheads and an emptying pandal behind them. The chant names, one by one, everything the festival used — sky, air, earth, water, plants, trees — and dismisses each into calm. It is the sound of the pandal being spiritually folded away.

**Notes.** The threefold closing *śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ* is traditionally understood to quiet the three sources of disturbance — from one's own self, from other beings, and from the gods and fate. Many priests precede or follow the sprinkle with the Vedic *svasti* benedictions — the *svasti vachana* of Rigveda 1.89, *oṃ svasti na indro vṛddhaśravāḥ…* (ওঁ স্বস্তি ন ইন্দ্রো বৃদ্ধশ্রবাঃ… / ॐ स्वस्ति न इन्द्रो वृद्धश्रवाः…) — "well-being for us may Indra of ancient fame grant, well-being Pushan who knows all…" — so that the last Sanskrit anyone hears at the pujo is a blessing for the road. Then the *dhak* changes rhythm, elders are greeted, sweets appear, and Bijoya begins.

## What these words add up to

Read against the opening liturgy of Shashthi, the closing mantras reveal what a Bengali Durga Puja actually is underneath the lights: a visit, conducted with the full courtesy owed to the most honoured guest imaginable — who is also, unmistakably, a daughter come home. She was woken gently, with a story about why it was all right to wake her; invited by name and lodged in a tree from her own mountains; given breath, senses, and eyes so she could be truly present, not merely represented. And when the four days are over she is not dismissed but *seen off* — told where she is going ("where Maheshvara is"), released first in a mirror so the parting can be done tenderly, and made to promise, in the very grammar of the farewell verse, to come back when the year has turned.

Notice what the visarjan liturgy never says: goodbye. Its operative words are *svasthānam* — your own place, conceding that the pandal was only ever a guest room — and *punar āgamanāya ca* — come again. The tradition treats the goddess's absence the way Bengal treats a married daughter's: real, grieved, and strictly temporary. The peace-chant then does what every good host does at the door — sends blessing out over everyone, on both sides of the parting. Which is why the correct response to the immersion, prescribed by custom and practised by millions, is not silence but a greeting and a sweet: *Shubho Bijoya*. *Punar āgamanāya ca* — for the coming again. Asche bochor, abar hobe.
