## What it is

Step into a Bengali home in the weeks before Durga Puja and you may catch a particular smell — warm jaggery melting into fresh coconut in a hot *karai* (কড়াই), with a whisper of cardamom underneath. That smell means someone is making *naru* (নাড়ু): small, hand-rolled balls of sweetness that are, for many families, the surest sign that Pujo is on its way.

Bengal is world-famous for its shop sweets — the *rosogolla*, the *sandesh* (সন্দেশ) — but the naru belongs to another world entirely. It is almost never sold in the great sweet shops. It is made at home, usually by mothers, grandmothers, and aunts working together, and it exists for one purpose: to welcome. Naru welcomes the goddess when it is placed on her offering plates as *bhog* (ভোগ) and on the arranged *naibedya* (নৈবেদ্য) trays through the pujo days. Naru welcomes every guest who knocks during *Bijoya* (বিজয়া), the season of visiting that follows Dashami. Naru is pressed into the hands of children along with blessings. A tin of naru travels in suitcases to hostels and foreign cities, carrying home along with it. These are not snacks that happen to be seasonal; they are offerings first, and hospitality made edible second.

"Anando naru" — *ananda naru* (আনন্দ নাড়ু), literally "the naru of joy" — is also the name of one specific, old-fashioned sweet within this family: a fried ball of coconut, flour or rice flour, and jaggery, rolled in white sesame seeds, traditionally made only for the most joyful family occasions — a wedding, a baby's first rice ceremony (*annaprashan*, অন্নপ্রাশন), a sacred-thread ceremony. Food writers record a charming folk rule attached to it: because it was reserved for celebrations, making it casually was considered improper — so resourceful housewives who craved it anyway would make the identical sweet and simply rename it *ichchha naru* (ইচ্ছা নাড়ু), the "naru of one's own wish," and the rule was satisfied. Not every Bengali family makes ananda naru — it belongs to particular household traditions rather than to all of Bengal — but its name captures perfectly what this whole chapter is about: sweetness made by hand, for joy, for Ma.

## When it happens

Home sweet-making follows the festival calendar, not any recipe book.

The season really opens in late monsoon with *Janmashtami* (জন্মাষ্টমী), Krishna's birthday, which falls in the Bengali months of Shrabon–Bhadro when the ripe palm fruit, *tal* (তাল), rains down from the trees. Janmashtami's signature offering, *taler bora* (তালের বড়া), is the first great home-frying event of the autumn, and for many families it flips a switch: from now until Bhai Phonta, the kitchen belongs to the festivals.

Then comes the run-up to Durga Puja itself, in the fortnight of *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ) that follows Mahalaya. This is when narus are rolled in quantity — some destined for the goddess's naibedya plates during the Pujo days and for the great Dashami platter, and a much larger batch stored away in glass jars, *boyam* (বয়াম), for what comes after: *Bijoya Dashami* and the ten-odd days of visiting that follow it, when anyone may knock on the door and must be fed sweets. No respectable household could risk running out. Many families keep the biggest rolling session for Panchami evening itself, the festival's eve, so that the welcome smells fresh when the goddess arrives ([Panchami: The Threshold Day](06-panchami.md)).

Barely a week after Dashami comes *Kojagori Lakshmi Puja* (কোজাগরী লক্ষ্মীপূজা), on the full moon, when narkel naru, the sesame *tiler naru* (তিলের নাড়ু), and *khoi-er moa* (খইয়ের মোয়া) are near-mandatory offerings to the goddess of prosperity. And as winter arrives, the new date-palm jaggery — *nolen gur* (নলেন গুড়) — opens the season of the great moas. The wheel turns all autumn, and the naru rolls through the middle of it.

## Step by step

Every named sweet on the Pujo table has its own small ritual of making. Here is the canon, roughly in the order a family would produce it.

### Narkel naru — the coconut heart of the tradition

The undisputed centrepiece. *Narkel naru* (নারকেল নাড়ু) — some families, especially those with East Bengal roots, say and spell it *nadu* — needs only two or three ingredients: fresh coconut, a sweetener, and perhaps cardamom (*elach*, এলাচ). Its two great persuasions divide by sweetener:

- *Gurer naru* (গুড়ের নাড়ু) — jaggery-dark, made classically with the soft cane jaggery called *akher gur* (আখের গুড়); deep brown, smoky-sweet, the "old" taste.
- *Chinir naru* (চিনির নাড়ু) — sugar-pale, sometimes called *sada naru* ("white naru"); milder, prettier on a plate, and in some households enriched with a spoon of *kheer* or *khoya* (ক্ষীর).

Most families make both, and most families argue affectionately about which is superior. There is no settling this.

The making begins with breaking coconuts — cracked, drained, and then grated on a *kuruni* (কুরুনি), the serrated scraper that in older homes was mounted on the curved blade of the *bnoti* (বঁটি). A grandmother seated on the floor, coconut half in hand, working it against the scraper while a heap of white *narkel kora* (নারকেল কোরা) grows on the plate below — this is one of the most beloved images in Bengali food memory, and grating is the job children were always allowed to "help" with (and to eat from).

### Pak — the stage where love becomes skill

The grated coconut goes into a heavy pan — traditionally iron or brass — with the jaggery or sugar, over a low flame. What follows is called *pak* (পাক), or *pak dewa*, "giving the pak": the slow, continuously stirred cooking in which the sweetener melts, bubbles, releases its water, and gradually binds the coconut into a rollable mass that begins to leave the sides of the pan. It takes twenty to twenty-five minutes of patient stirring, and it is where all the skill lives.

Bengali cooks name two end-points. *Norom pak* (নরম পাক), the "soft pak," is taken off the heat early: the naru stays moist, chewy, and tender, but keeps for only a few days. *Kora pak* (কড়া পাক), the "hard pak," is cooked on until the mass darkens and tightens: the naru sets firm, almost toffee-like, and — crucially — survives for weeks in the boyam, which is exactly what the long hospitality season demands. (The same soft/hard vocabulary is used for sandesh in the sweet shops.) The classic doneness test is hands-on: take a little of the hot mixture and try to roll a ball on your palm. If it holds, the pak is ready; if it crumbles, keep stirring. Some cooks fold in a handful of coarse sugar crystals near the end so the finished naru carries a faint crunch.

Then comes the part that separates the experienced from the enthusiastic: the mixture must be rolled while still hot, because it stiffens as it cools. Take it off too soon and the ball will not hold; wait too long and it sets solid in the pan — which is why rolling naru is a skill grandmothers demonstrate rather than explain. Palms are moistened with water or smeared with ghee, and the women work fast, tossing the scalding mixture from hand to hand, shaping ball after ball — each about a mouthful — while trading news and jokes. Blistered fingertips are worn like medals.

### Ananda naru — the naru of joy

The sweet that lends this chapter its name deserves its own entry. *Ananda naru* is a fried naru: grated coconut is worked with flour (wheat flour in some households, rice flour in others), jaggery, and a pinch of salt into a dough, shaped into small balls, rolled generously in white sesame seeds, and deep-fried until golden. The result is darker, denser, and nuttier than narkel naru, and because it contains no water, it keeps remarkably well — old sources say a jar could last a month.

Its defining feature, though, is not the recipe but the occasion. Ananda naru was by custom made only when the family had something to celebrate — hence *ananda*, joy — and first offered to the gods before anyone ate. Accounts of the tradition describe it as a household rather than universal sweet: some families have made it for generations, others have never heard of it. That, too, is honest Bengali food history — the repertoire varies house to house, and every family's canon is slightly different.

### Tiler naru and chhola naru — the wider family

Coconut does not have the field to itself. *Tiler naru*, of roasted sesame seeds bound in jaggery, is the winter and Lakshmi Puja specialist — nutty, brittle-chewy, and long-keeping. And many family traditions add *chholar naru* (ছোলার নাড়ু), rolled from roasted gram flour or whole roasted gram worked with jaggery or sugar and a little ghee — a rustic, protein-rich cousin that appears on household offering plates alongside its coconut relatives. As always, which narus a house makes is part of that house's inheritance.

### Moa — puffed and popped rice, bound in gur

A *moa* (মোয়া) is the naru's rustic cousin: puffed or popped rice bound with hot jaggery syrup and pressed into a ball. There are two distinct bases, and the words matter. *Muri* (মুড়ি) is puffed rice — the everyday snack — and *murir moa* (মুড়ির মোয়া) is the everyman's moa, crunchy and light, jaggery-lacquered, cheap enough to be childhood pocket-money food. *Khoi* (খই) is popped paddy — rice popped in its husk, like tiny white blossoms — and *khoi-er moa* is the more delicate, more ceremonial version that appears on Lakshmi Puja plates, bound in date-palm or cane jaggery according to season.

The aristocrat of the family is *Joynagarer moa* (জয়নগরের মোয়া), from the town of Joynagar south of Kolkata: khoi of a special scented paddy called *kanakchur*, bound with fresh winter *nolen gur* and enriched with ghee, khoya, and raisins. It can only be made in winter, when both key ingredients exist, and in 2015 it became the first Bengali sweet to receive a Geographical Indication (GI) tag. It arrives after Pujo — but no honest account of the moa can leave it out.

### Taler bora — the monsoon's parting gift

*Taler bora* — fritters of ripe sugar-palm pulp — technically belongs to Janmashtami rather than Durga Puja, but every Bengali counts it in the same festive canon. Making it is an ordeal cheerfully endured: the fibrous golden pulp must be scraped and squeezed out of the stubborn tal fruit (a job that stains everything it touches and perfumes the whole house), then beaten into a batter with rice flour, wheat flour or semolina, grated coconut, and jaggery, and deep-fried into dark, honeyed, chewy rounds. It is loved across West Bengal, Bangladesh, and Odisha alike, and for many families the smell of frying tal is the true overture to the festive season.

### Nimki — the salty counterpoint

No Bijoya plate is complete without its savoury guard: *nimki* (নিমকি), crisp diamonds of flour dough spiced with *kalonji* (nigella seed) and sometimes a breath of *hing*, folded, cut, and fried to a shattering crispness. The bite-sized version is *kucho nimki* (কুচো নিমকি) — *kucho* means "tiny." After two narus and a piece of sandesh, the nimki is what rescues the palate; the alternation of sweet and salty is the whole architecture of Bijoya hospitality. Many households add *niramish ghugni* (ঘুগনি) — yellow peas simmered without onion or garlic, finished with slivers of coconut — and *chirer polao*, and the plate is complete.

## Why it matters

Why insist on homemade, when Kolkata has the finest sweet shops on earth — and Pune has perfectly good ones?

The first answer is ritual purity. Anything offered to the goddess must be *niramish* (নিরামিষ) — strictly vegetarian, and in the Bengali sacred kitchen that means no onion and no garlic either, which are traditionally classed as heating, worldly ingredients unfit for offerings. Food cooked for a deity is prepared in a clean kitchen, by people who have bathed, often before tasting a morsel themselves — because you never taste what is meant for Ma first. A shop cannot promise any of that. A home can. Only after the offering does the food return to the family as *prasad* (প্রসাদ), sanctified leftovers of the divine meal.

The second answer is the logic of welcome. Durga, in the Bengali imagination, is not a distant queen but a daughter coming home to her parents for four days. And when your daughter comes home, you do not order in. You cook. The naru is "Ma's own welcome" — the thing her mother would have made — and the traditions of East Bengal in particular hold the homemade sweet to be indispensable to the goddess's hospitality: no shop-bought sandesh can quite replace what was made for her at home. By extension it is the welcome owed to every guest during Bijoya, when a sweet placed in the mouth (*mishti mukh*, মিষ্টিমুখ) seals reconciliation, respect, and affection for the year.

The third answer is the one everyone actually feels: the naru is a memory you can eat. Nearly every Bengali who grew up near a grandmother carries the same scene — the scraper, the white heap of coconut, the forbidden hot pan, the first naru slipped to the child who "helped." Making the sweets together — children shaping lopsided narus that get offered anyway — is its own observance: the day the festival moves out of the calendar and into the body, through the hands and the sweet tooth. Rolling naru is how that scene is handed forward.

## The story behind it

The naru is old — far older than the syrupy shop sweets. Bengal cooked with *gur*, the boiled-down sap of cane and date palm, long before refined sugar and the Portuguese-influenced cheese sweets arrived in the colonial centuries; a ball of coconut and jaggery, needing no milk-curdling and no shop, is the kind of sweet a village household could always make. That is why the naru remained the people's offering — and the goddess's.

It is also a sweet both Bengals share. The famous kitchen rivalry between *ghoti* (ঘটি) families — rooted on this side, *Epar Bangla* (এপার বাংলা), in West Bengal — and *bangal* (বাঙাল) families, who came from *Opar Bangla* (ওপার বাংলা), erstwhile East Bengal, now Bangladesh, runs hot on fish, mustard, and sugar-in-curry, but it falls largely silent before the naru. Coconut palms and jaggery belong to both deltas, and narkel naru is rolled before Pujo in Dhaka and Barishal exactly as in Kolkata and Howrah. The differences are of accent, not substance: East Bengal families often say *nadu*, tend to carry a larger repertoire of *pithe* (পিঠে) and homemade confections generally — the deep countryside tradition travelled with them in 1947 when so much else could not be carried — and some houses favour their own proportions of khoi, muri, and til. For families who crossed over during Partition, rolling the same naru in a new land was one of the quietest, most stubborn ways of keeping home alive. A probasi pujo in Pune, it turns out, repeats an old gesture.

And the ananda naru's little legend — the sweet lawfully made only in joy, and the wink of renaming it ichchha naru to make it whenever you please — says something true about Bengali ritual life: rules are honoured, human appetite finds a loophole, and everyone smiles.

## In a probasi pujo

A community pujo far from Bengal faces a choice every autumn: buy the sweets, or make them. Buying is easy. Making is the point.

Our samiti chose making early on — the account books from 2022 carry a line item that says, simply, "Anando Naru Making," sitting among the pandal and purohit expenses as if it were just another cost of holding a pujo. In a sense it is. A few evenings before Pujo, a clubhouse room in Magarpatta City turns into a grandmother's kitchen at scale: sacks of coconuts, slabs of jaggery, borrowed heavy pans, and a circle of members — aunts and young mothers, a few brave uncles, children hovering at exactly the radius from which hot mixture can be begged — grating, stirring, testing the pak, and rolling hundreds of narus while the room fills with steam, gossip, and three generations of conflicting instructions about kora pak.

The compromises of distance are real and freely admitted. Fresh coconuts in a Pune market are not the coconuts of a village pukur-side tree; the soft akher gur of Bengal must be hunted down or substituted; nolen gur is a winter courier's treasure. Some batches lean on desiccated coconut and condensed milk, and the grandmothers who remember better pretend not to notice. None of it matters very much. The narus made that evening go first to Ma as bhog — onto the naibedya plates from Shashthi onward ([Maha Shashthi — The Awakening](07-shashthi.md)) — then into the boyams for Bijoya, when members visit one another's flats with plates of naru, nimki, and ghugni exactly as their parents did across two Bengals. For children growing up in Marathi-speaking Pune, an evening spent rolling scalding coconut into lopsided balls may be the most direct inheritance the pujo gives them: joy, made by hand. Anando naru, in every sense.

## Words to know

- *Naru* (নাড়ু) — a hand-rolled ball sweet; the homemade sweet par excellence. East Bengal families often say *nadu*.
- *Narkel* (নারকেল) — coconut; *narkel kora* is freshly grated coconut.
- *Gur* (গুড়) — jaggery. *Akher gur* is from sugarcane; *nolen gur* is fresh winter date-palm jaggery; *patali* (পাটালি) is jaggery set into solid cakes.
- *Chini* (চিনি) — white sugar; hence *chinir naru* vs *gurer naru*.
- *Karai* (কড়াই) — the deep, heavy pan in which the pak is given.
- *Pak* (পাক) — the stirred-cooking stage that binds a sweet; *norom pak* is soft-set, *kora pak* is hard-set and long-keeping.
- *Kuruni* (কুরুনি) — the serrated coconut scraper; *bnoti* (বঁটি) is the floor-mounted curved blade it often sits on.
- *Tiler naru* (তিলের নাড়ু) — sesame-jaggery naru; *chholar naru* (ছোলার নাড়ু) is the roasted-gram naru of many family traditions.
- *Moa* (মোয়া) — a ball of puffed rice (*muri*) or popped paddy (*khoi*) bound with jaggery; *Joynagarer moa* is the celebrated GI-tagged winter version.
- *Taler bora* (তালের বড়া) — fried fritters of ripe sugar-palm pulp, the Janmashtami special.
- *Nimki* (নিমকি) — crisp, savoury fried diamonds; *kucho nimki* is the tiny Bijoya version.
- *Niramish* (নিরামিষ) — strictly vegetarian cooking without onion or garlic, required for anything offered to the deity.
- *Bhog* (ভোগ) — food offered to the goddess; *naibedya* (নৈবেদ্য) is the arranged offering plate; *prasad* (প্রসাদ) is that food returned, blessed, to the devotees.
- *Bijoya* (বিজয়া) — the season of visits, blessings, and *mishti mukh* (মিষ্টিমুখ, "sweetening the mouth") that follows Dashami.
- *Ananda naru* (আনন্দ নাড়ু) — the fried coconut-sesame "naru of joy," traditionally made only for celebrations; renamed *ichchha naru* (ইচ্ছা নাড়ু) by those who refused to wait for one.
