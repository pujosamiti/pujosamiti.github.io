## The night after the farewell

The pujo does not quite end at the river. Durga Pujo goes out with a bang — drums, tears, dancing, the splash of immersion ([Bijoya Dashami](11-dashami.md)) — and then, just as the pandal falls quiet and the lights start coming down, Bengal does something lovely and unexpected. Four or five evenings after Bijoya Dashami, on the biggest, brightest full moon of the year, every Bengali home lights a lamp, draws little footprints on the floor walking *into* the house, and sits down to welcome one more guest: *Ma Lokkhi* (মা লক্ষ্মী), the goddess of wealth, harvest and household well-being.

This is *Kojagari Lakshmi Puja* (কোজাগরী লক্ষ্মীপূজা) — in everyday Bengali, simply *Lokkhi Pujo* (লক্ষ্মীপূজো). If Durga Pujo is the roaring public festival, Kojagari is its soft coda, kept in a completely different key: no *dhak* (ঢাক), no crowds, no spectacle. Just moonlight, incense, a grandmother reading aloud from a slim red-bordered book, and the belief that on this one night the goddess herself moves from house to house, seeing who has stayed awake for her. Where Durga arrived to thunder, Lakshmi arrives in silence — and must find the house awake.

## What it is

Kojagari Lakshmi Puja is the Bengali worship of Lakshmi — goddess of *sri* (শ্রী): wealth, abundance, grace and good order — held on *Sharad Purnima* (শারদ পূর্ণিমা), the full-moon night of the month of Ashwin. Most of India worships Lakshmi a few weeks later, on the new-moon night of Diwali. Bengal, along with Odisha, Assam and Mithila, does it earlier and differently: on the radiant autumn full moon, in a puja that is above all *domestic*. Almost every Hindu Bengali household performs its own Lokkhi Pujo at home, usually led not by a priest but by the women of the family.

The name carries the festival's whole story inside it. *Kojagari* comes from the Sanskrit question **"Ko jāgarti?"** (কো জাগর্তি?) — *"Who is awake?"* The belief, told and retold for centuries, is that on this night Lakshmi descends to earth and wanders from door to door asking exactly that. The household she finds awake — lamps lit, floors clean, family gathered in prayer — receives her blessing of prosperity for the year; the house she finds dark, dirty or noisy, she quietly passes by. In Bengali the question softens into *"Ke jege achho?"* — who's still up? — and the whole festival becomes a night-long answer: *we are*.

Because Lakshmi is imagined as a guest who might arrive at any moment, the puja is really a lesson in hospitality dressed up as ritual. The house is cleaned as if for a beloved relative, her footprints are painted leading in from the doorway — never out — her favourite foods are laid out, and the family stays up in *jagaran* (জাগরণ), the vigil, keeping her company by lamplight. One more thing worth saying at the start: Lakshmi's famous epithet is *chanchala* (চঞ্চলা), "the restless one." Wealth, the tradition says, never sits still and does not stay where it is not respected. Vigilance, the night teaches, is the price of grace. Kojagari night is when Bengal remembers this, and quietly teaches its children what makes Lakshmi stay.

## When it happens

Kojagari Lakshmi Puja falls on **Sharad Purnima**, the full moon (*purnima*, পূর্ণিমা) of the lunar month of Ashwin — usually in October. It comes four or five days after *Bijoya Dashami* (বিজয়া দশমী), the last day of Durga Pujo, and feels like the season's gentle epilogue: Durga has returned to Kailash, and now her daughter Lakshmi — who stood beside her mother in every Durga Pujo tableau — pays her own visit.

This full moon is considered the loveliest of the year. The monsoon clouds have gone, the air is newly crisp, and the moon — called *kaumudi* (কৌমুদী), "moonlight," in old texts — is said to shine with all sixteen of its phases at once. Across India this night carries the belief that the moon rays drip *amrita*, nectar; in many homes a bowl of *payesh* or kheer is set out under the open sky to soak in the moonlight before being eaten the next morning — the moonlight itself an ingredient. The exact date shifts each year with the lunar calendar — in 2026, Sharad Purnima falls on **25 October** — and Bengali almanacs (*panjika*, পঞ্জিকা) give the precise *tithi* window. The puja is always performed in the evening and night, never the morning, because the whole point is the vigil: the day is for fasting and preparing, and the goddess arrives with the moon.

## The rituals, step by step

What follows is the common shape of a household Kojagari puja. Every family does it slightly differently — which grandmother taught you matters more than any book — and that variety is itself part of the tradition. Nothing below is compulsory in every home; everything below is beloved in some home.

### Upobash — the day's fast

In most households, the person who will perform the puja — very often the mother or grandmother of the house — observes an *upobash* (উপবাস), a fast, from the morning of purnima. Some fast completely until the puja ends; others take fruit or water; practices vary widely by family and by health. In older texts this observance is a formal *vrata* (ব্রত) — a vow — called the *Kojagara vrata*, and the fasting-and-vigil combination is its heart. The fast is broken only after the evening puja and *anjali*, usually with the offered fruits and sweets themselves.

### Cleaning the house and drawing the alpona

Through the day the house is swept and washed, because Lakshmi is believed to enter only clean, cared-for homes. Then comes the most beautiful preparation of all: the *alpona* (আলপনা) — Bengal's ritual floor-painting, drawn freehand in white with a paste of soaked rice ground with water. Traditionally there is no brush: a small twist of cotton or cloth, or simply two fingers dipped in the paste, draws flowing lines straight onto the mud or cement floor.

Alpona has its own grammar, and on Lokkhi Pujo three motifs matter most:

- **Lakshmi's footprints** — small stylised feet, drawn in a trail from the front door, through the rooms, right up to the puja spot — toward the granary and the safe, as the old houses had it. They always walk *into* the house. This is the tenderest image of the whole festival: the goddess is shown her way in, step by painted step, and never shown the way out.
- ***Dhaner chhora*** (ধানের ছড়া) — sprays of rice paddy, drawn as wavy stalks heavy with grain. Lakshmi is at root a harvest goddess, and Ashwin purnima sits close to the rice harvest; the painted paddy is a prayer that the granary stay full.
- **The lotus and the owl** — Lakshmi's seat (the *padma*, পদ্ম) and her mount (the *pecha*, পেঁচা), often sketched around the central design, along with conch shells, creepers, geometric borders and, in many households, the *kulo* (কুলো) — the winnowing fan, the granary's own emblem drawn onto the floor.

Children are usually recruited for the alpona, which is exactly how the art survives — it is muscle memory passed from hand to hand, not written down.

### Ghot sthapana — seating the goddess

At dusk the puja is set up. The centre of a Bengali Lokkhi Pujo need not be an idol at all: the essential form is the *ghot* (ঘট), a rounded clay or brass pot, installed through *ghot sthapana* (ঘট স্থাপন). The ghot is filled with water (in many homes, with rice paddy), a swastika or stylised figure is drawn on its belly with *sindur* (সিঁদুর, vermilion), five mango leaves are arranged at its mouth, and a green coconut with its stalk — often wrapped with a small red-bordered cloth or *gamchha* — is set on top, with a betel nut and leaf. The filled pot is the goddess in symbol: a vessel brimming with life.

Beside or instead of the ghot, different families keep different forms of Lakshmi — a *murti*, a painted *shora*, or the heirloom *lokkhir jhampi* (লক্ষ্মীর ঝাঁপি), the wicker basket of paddy, cowrie shells and sindur that is Lakshmi in perhaps her oldest form: the harvest itself, kept safe in a basket and passed down through generations. This is where the deepest family differences show (more on this under iconography below). What almost every arrangement includes is a bundle of real rice paddy, the *dhaner shish* (ধানের শিষ), and a small oil lamp (*pradip*, প্রদীপ) that will be kept burning through the night.

### The puja and the panchali reading

The puja itself is simple and homely: flowers, incense, sandal paste, lamp, and the offerings arranged before the goddess. Then comes the ritual that defines the evening's sound — the reading of the ***Lakshmir Panchali*** (লক্ষ্মীর পাঁচালী).

A *panchali* is a Bengali verse narrative meant to be read aloud, and every Bengali household owns a copy of Lakshmi's — usually a thin red-covered booklet bought for a few rupees and treasured for decades. The lady of the house reads it in a sing-song metre while the family listens; in many homes it is read again every Thursday (*Lokkhibar*, লক্ষ্মীবার — "Lakshmi's day") throughout the year.

Inside the panchali is a *brotokotha* (ব্রতকথা), a vow-story. Printed versions vary, but the widely known one begins on a serene full-moon night in Vaikuntha, where the sage Narad describes the misery and quarrelling of humans on earth; Lakshmi, moved, comes down to spread her vrata among them. The tale then follows households that lose her — through arrogance, waste, disrespect to food or grain, or ceaseless quarrelling — and are ruined, until someone humbly takes up the Lokkhi brata and the goddess returns. The verses in between are pure household wisdom set to rhyme: keep the home clean, do not waste rice, do not insult food, speak gently, live within your means, honour the women of the house. Generations of Bengali children have absorbed their first economics and their first ethics from this little book without ever noticing they were being taught.

### The night's quiet rules

Alongside the liturgy runs a set of small, telling customs, kept with varying strictness from house to house. No iron touches the puja arrangements — Lakshmi's things are clay, brass, bronze, cloth and leaf. No one who comes to the door that evening is turned away unfed; the goddess may arrive in any guise, and hospitality is the whole point of the night. And money is neither lent nor, in many homes, spent on this day — so that Lakshmi is not seen leaving the house. Superstition, if you like; but notice how every one of these rules is the theology in miniature: gentleness, generosity, and holding on to what you have been given.

### Anjali and breaking the fast

After the reading and the offering of *bhog*, the family offers *anjali* (অঞ্জলি) — flower petals held in cupped hands, released toward the goddess with mantras, usually repeated three times, just as at Durga Pujo but on a miniature, familial scale. Conch shells (*shankh*, শাঁখ) are blown and the women give the warbling *ulu* (উলু) call — but softly; this is not a puja of noise.

The pranam offered to her at the close is one of the loveliest small mantras in the household repertoire:

> ॐ विश्वरूपस्य भार्यासि पद्मे पद्मालये शुभे।
> सर्वतः पाहि मां देवि महालक्ष्मि नमोऽस्तु ते॥
>
> ওঁ বিশ্বরূপস্য ভার্যাসি পদ্মে পদ্মালয়ে শুভে।
> সর্বতঃ পাহি মাং দেবি মহালক্ষ্মি নমোঽস্তু তে॥
>
> *om viśvarūpasya bhāryāsi padme padmālaye śubhe ·
> sarvataḥ pāhi māṁ devi mahālakṣmi namo'stu te*

**In simple words:** O Padma, lotus-dweller, auspicious one, consort of the all-formed Lord — protect me on every side, great Lakshmi; I bow to you.

With the anjali done, the fast is broken, and prasad — fruit, *naru*, *khoi*, *moa*, the whole sweet spread — is shared with everyone, including the neighbours who inevitably drop in.

### The jagaran — the night vigil

Then, in the old ideal, nobody goes to bed. The lamp stays lit and the family stays up talking, singing, playing games — for whoever sleeps might miss the goddess's visit. The old texts even sanction dice on this night: in Mithila's parallel festival *Kojagra*, and in old zamindar households of Bengal, a game of dice or *pasha* on Kojagari night was considered auspicious rather than sinful — a playful nod to the goddess of fortune, whose oldest symbols include the cowrie shell, once both currency and dice. In most modern homes the vigil has softened to a late, chatty, moonlit evening — but the festival's name still remembers the question the vigil answers, and children allowed to stay up "for Lakshmi" remember the night longer than any pandal.

## Why it matters

Kojagari Lakshmi Puja matters precisely because of everything it is *not*. It is not grand. It is not public. It needs no pandal, no priest, no subscription, no loudspeaker. It distils the entire festive season down to one lamp, one pot, one book read aloud — and in doing so it teaches things the big festival cannot.

**It teaches what wealth is.** The Lakshmi of this night is not money; she is *sri* — the well-being of a household: full granaries, clean floors, food treated with respect, a family that does not quarrel. The paddy on the altar and the paddy drawn on the floor say it plainly: for an agrarian civilisation, wealth meant the harvest. Every naru and grain of khoi on the plate is rice, coconut and sugar — the harvest, transformed by a household's own hands. The puffed and flattened rice of the offerings are, quite literally, the granary's own liturgy.

**It teaches what drives wealth away.** Here the *chanchala* idea does its quiet work. Lakshmi abandons homes that are noisy, dirty, wasteful or quarrelsome — and where she leaves, her inauspicious opposite *Alakshmi* (অলক্ষ্মী) is said to move in. Strip away the theology and you have a startlingly practical proverb: prosperity does not stay in a disordered, bickering house. This is why there is **no dhak at Lokkhi Pujo** — no drums, no crashing cymbals, none of Durga Pujo's glorious din, for the goddess of this night dislikes noise. Every Bengali child feels the deliberate contrast: Durga arrives to thunder; Lakshmi arrives to silence, and you must be quiet enough to hear her ask her question.

**It keeps women at the centre.** This is the great puja that women own outright — the fasting, the alpona, the panchali, the mantras. In a tradition where large public rituals are usually led by male priests, Lokkhi Pujo is the sacred authority of the grandmother and the mother, passed daughter to daughter.

**And it closes the season with grace.** The mother has gone; her daughter visits; the household exhales. The festival season does not crash to an end — it dims, like a lamp carried from the pandal back into the kitchen.

## The story behind it

The night-vigil legend is genuinely old. A *Kojagara vrata* — fast by day, Lakshmi worship and wakefulness by night on the Ashwin full moon, with the "Ko jāgarti?" question on the goddess's lips — appears in the Sanskrit vrata literature that medieval digest-writers compiled; popular accounts variously cite the Skanda, Brahma, Linga and Brahma-vaivarta Puranas and later ritual compendia such as the *Vratarāja*, and the night is also called the *Kaumudi* festival after its moonlight. (As with much vrata literature, the attributions differ from source to source — what is certain is that the observance has been prescribed in almost these exact terms for many centuries, and honesty requires saying the paper trail is a scholar's tangle even if the practice is continuous.)

The vrat-katha most often told for Kojagara concerns a poor, learned Brahmin named **Valit**, tormented by poverty and by an ill-tempered wife who ruins even his father's memorial rites. In despair he leaves home on the Ashwin full moon and, by a riverbank, falls in with serpent-maidens — *Nag-kanyas* — who are playing dice through the night in observance of the vrata. He joins their game, loses everything down to his sacred thread — and at midnight, Vishnu and Lakshmi, passing over the earth to see who is awake, take pity on the ruined man who is, after all, awake. Lakshmi's grace transforms his fortune entirely; he returns home wealthy. The story stitches together every strand of the night: the vigil, the moonlight, the dice, and the goddess's soft spot for those still awake when everyone else has given up.

Behind the legend sits the plainer, older story: the harvest. Ashwin purnima marks the turn toward the *aman* rice harvest in Bengal. A festival of the full granary, held under the year's clearest moon, addressed to a goddess whose very icons are paddy and the overflowing pot — Kojagari is agricultural thanksgiving wearing a mythological shawl.

And for lakhs of Bengali families there is a third, more painful layer. For the *Bangal* (বাঙাল) families of East Bengal — who lost homesteads, granaries and ancestral ponds in the Partition of 1947 — Kojagari Lokkhi Pujo carried across the border as a portable piece of the lost desh. In refugee colonies of Calcutta, keeping the Kojagari rituals exactly as they had been done in Dhaka or Faridpur or Barisal became an act of memory and identity. Some of the festival's most distinctive traditions are theirs, which brings us to the family differences.

### Bangal and Ghoti: two Bengals, one goddess

Bengali culture famously divides — with great affection and great rivalry — into *Ghoti* (ঘটি), families rooted in West Bengal, and *Bangal*, families whose roots lie in East Bengal (today's Bangladesh). Lokkhi Pujo is one of the places the difference shows most clearly, and neither way is more correct; they are two dialects of the same devotion.

**Form of the goddess.** Many Ghoti households traditionally worship Lakshmi in the ghot alone, in a vessel of paddy, or in the heirloom *lokkhir jhampi* — no image at all. East Bengal's hallmark is the ***Lokkhir shora*** (লক্ষ্মীর সরা): a shallow painted clay disc bearing bright folk images of Lakshmi with her owl, sometimes with Durga's whole family, painted by hereditary potter-painter families. The shora tradition belongs especially to the Dhaka–Faridpur–Barisal region, and connoisseurs distinguish regional painting styles (Faridpuri, Dhakai, Sureshwari and others). Many probasi Bangal families still hunt down a new shora every year. Fully modelled clay *murtis* — Lakshmi seated on her lotus, paddy in hand, owl at her feet — are common in both communities now, especially for community pujas.

Pause on that owl, the *pecha* (পেঁচা) — in Bengali puja tradition, *Pechak*. Why would the radiant goddess of wealth ride the least glamorous of birds? Because the owl is awake and sharp-eyed all night — the ideal companion for the goddess who asks "Who is awake?" — and because it guards the harvest, hunting the rats that raid granaries. But the owl is also blind in daylight, and Bengal reads a warning in that: wealth pursued without wisdom blinds its owner; greed sees only in the dark. Lakshmi rides the owl, the elders say, so that her devotees remember both halves — stay vigilant, and don't go blind.

**The bhog.** Broadly: Ghoti households favour a hot cooked *bhog* (ভোগ) — *khichuri* (খিচুড়ি) with *labra* (লাবড়া, a mixed-vegetable dish), fried vegetables, chutney, *payesh* (পায়েস) — with luchi in some houses. Many Bangal households instead centre the offering on uncooked and hand-made things — *chire* (চিঁড়ে, flattened rice) with grated coconut, and a whole artillery of home-made sweets: *naru*, *moa*, *murki*, *khoi*, *batasha* (all introduced properly in the sweets chapter, 05-anando-naru.md). And in nearly every home, the bowl of payesh that has sat out under the full moon crowns the spread. Countless families, of course, happily do both.

**The jora ilish question.** Some East Bengal families observe a striking custom involving a pair of hilsa fish — ***jora ilish*** (জোড়া ইলিশ). A pair (never one; the pair signifies completeness) is brought home ceremonially, welcomed at the threshold with oil, turmeric and sindur almost like a bride, offered to the goddess, then cooked and shared as prasad. But families genuinely differ here: in many Bangal homes the jora ilish belongs to **Saraswati Puja** in spring — the day the ilish season ritually opens — while others offer it at Lakshmi Puja or on Bijoya Dashami and then abstain from ilish until Saraswati Puja, letting the fish breed in peace through winter (notice the quiet ecological wisdom in the ritual calendar). Whichever date your grandmother kept is the right one; and if offering fish to a goddess startles you, remember that Bengal has never seen fish as opposed to holiness — for Bangal families especially, *machh* is auspicious life itself.

## In a probasi pujo

For a *probasi* (প্রবাসী — expatriate) Bengali community like ours in Magarpatta City, Kojagari has a special sweetness, because it is the one major puja that scales down perfectly to a flat. It travels, as it were, in a suitcase: a panchali booklet, a shora or small murti, chire and khoi from the grocer, alpona at an apartment door. And the full moon, helpfully, works in every time zone.

**At home.** You need no pandal and no priest: a ghot or a framed image, a lamp, fruit, some sweets, and the panchali (paper booklet, PDF, or a YouTube recitation playing while you follow along). Rice-paste alpona works beautifully on tile and marble — soak a handful of rice for a few hours, grind it with water to a thin cream, and draw with a cotton bud or fingertip; it wipes clean afterwards. Families in a hurry use white rangoli chalk, alpona stencils, or the (slightly guilty) sticker-alpona sold online — and honestly, a child drawing wobbly rice-paste footprints from the front door to the puja shelf is worth more than the most perfect sticker. Fasting is adapted to real life; many working parents keep a fruit-only day and do the puja after office hours, since the timing is meant for evening anyway.

**Sourcing the spread in Pune.** *Khoi*, *batasha*, *naru* and *moa* are not standard Maharashtrian stock, but they are findable: Bengali sweet shops and pujo-season stalls carry them, the city's Kali Bari and Bengali associations know the suppliers, and online grocers now ship khoi and batasha year-round. Murmura (puffed rice) is everywhere in Pune and makes an honest moa; grated coconut and jaggery make naru in any kitchen — many probasi families discover that making naru together the evening before *is* the festival. Sugarcane is in Pune markets in this season, so the traditional cut offerings — *narkel* (নারকেল, coconut) and *aankh* (আখ, sugarcane) — are easy to keep.

**At the pandal.** And then there is the samiti's own Lokkhi Pujo. Our community, like many probasi samitis, worships Lakshmi in the **same pandal where Durga was worshipped** days earlier — and the experience is moving precisely because of the contrast: the frame that held the ten-armed goddess and a thousand-person crowd now holds a small Lakshmi, a ghot, a reader with a panchali, and a few dozen families sitting quietly on the floor. The same pandal, a different silence. No dhak; just the shankh, soft ulu and the reading. The household puja and the community puja do not compete — they nest. Lokkhi Pujo is intimately domestic, *every* home its own temple that night, yet the *barowari* (বারোয়ারি, community) puja lets the neighbourhood share one more evening together and gives families without a home setup a goddess to bring their anjali to. When the pandal Lakshmi is immersed, the festive season is truly, gently over.

## Words to know

- ***Kojagari*** (কোজাগরী) — from Sanskrit *Ko jāgarti?*, "Who is awake?"; the name of this puja and its vigil.
- ***Sharad Purnima*** (শারদ পূর্ণিমা) — the autumn full moon of Ashwin; also called *Kaumudi*, "moonlight."
- ***Ghot*** (ঘট) — the water-filled sacred pot, with mango leaves and green coconut, that embodies the goddess; installing it is *ghot sthapana* (ঘট স্থাপন).
- ***Alpona*** (আলপনা) — ritual floor art in white rice-paste; tonight it features Lakshmi's inward-walking footprints, *dhaner chhora* (ধানের ছড়া) — sprays of paddy — and often the *kulo* (কুলো), the winnowing fan.
- ***Panchali*** (পাঁচালী) — the verse booklet (*Lakshmir Panchali*) read aloud during the puja, containing the *brotokotha* (ব্রতকথা), the vow-story.
- ***Anjali*** (অঞ্জলি) — offering flowers from cupped hands with mantras.
- ***Upobash*** (উপবাস) — the day's fast, broken after the evening rituals.
- ***Jagaran*** (জাগরণ) — the night vigil for the goddess's visit.
- ***Bhog*** (ভোগ) — food offered to the deity and shared as *prasad*: *khichuri* (খিচুড়ি) and *labra* (লাবড়া) in many Ghoti homes, *chire* (চিঁড়ে) and sweets in many Bangal homes.
- ***Naru*** (নাড়ু) — hand-rolled coconut or sesame balls with jaggery; ***moa*** (মোয়া) — puffed-rice balls bound in jaggery; ***khoi*** (খই) — popped rice; ***batasha*** (বাতাসা) — feather-light drops of hardened sugar: the four pillars of the Lokkhi Pujo plate.
- ***Payesh*** (পায়েস) — rice pudding; on this night set out under the full moon, the moonlight itself an ingredient.
- ***Jora ilish*** (জোড়া ইলিশ) — a pair of hilsa ceremonially offered by some East Bengal families, at Lakshmi Puja in some households, at Saraswati Puja or Bijoya Dashami in others.
- ***Pecha*** (পেঁচা) — the owl, Lakshmi's mount (*vahana*), called *Pechak* in Bengali puja tradition; awake and sharp-eyed by night, blind by day — vigilance rewarded, greed warned against.
- ***Dhaner shish*** (ধানের শিষ) — the sheaf of rice paddy on the altar; Lakshmi's oldest emblem.
- ***Lokkhir jhampi*** (লক্ষ্মীর ঝাঁপি) — the heirloom wicker basket of paddy, cowries and sindur; Lakshmi as the harvest itself, passed down the generations.
- ***Lokkhir shora*** (লক্ষ্মীর সরা) — the painted clay disc bearing Lakshmi's image, the beloved worship-form of Dhaka–Faridpur–Barisal families.
- ***Chanchala*** (চঞ্চলা) — "the restless one"; the teaching that prosperity leaves noisy, quarrelsome homes — and why there is no dhak tonight.
- ***Alakshmi*** (অলক্ষ্মী) — Lakshmi's inauspicious opposite, said to occupy homes Lakshmi abandons.
- ***Ghoti / Bangal*** (ঘটি / বাঙাল) — Bengalis rooted in West and East Bengal respectively; two affectionately rival streams of one culture, each with its own Lokkhi Pujo flavour.
