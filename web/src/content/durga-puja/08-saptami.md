## What it is

If Shashthi is the evening the Goddess is welcomed and unveiled, Maha Saptami (মহাসপ্তমী) is the morning she truly arrives. Saptami begins in the dark, at the water's edge, with a banana plant wrapped in a sari — and ends with the Goddess alive in her image. It is the pujo's dawn of consecrations: the first of the three great days of worship, and by common consent the busiest morning of the whole festival for the priest. Before most of the neighbourhood has finished its tea, an extraordinary sequence has already unfolded. A small procession has carried a banana plant to the water and bathed it as a goddess. The Devi herself has received a royal bath of dozens of waters and soils, poured not on the clay but on her reflection in a mirror. And, in the ritual on which everything else turns, the priest has breathed life into the image — so that what stood in the pandal yesterday as a beautiful sculpture stands today, in the eyes of the devotee, as the living Mother.

Saptami is a day of two intertwined stories. One is the familiar, courtly story of the Puranas — Durga the demon-slayer, honoured like a visiting queen with sixteen offerings and a feast. The other is older and earthier: a harvest festival hiding in plain sight, in which the Goddess is worshipped not as a sculpted warrior but as a bundle of nine living plants — banana, turmeric, paddy, pomegranate — the crops and greens of an agrarian Bengal giving thanks at the end of the rains. It is the festival's frankest admission that beneath the Sanskrit liturgy lives an old religion of the growing world. Saptami is the day these two stories are tied together, quite literally, with a yellow thread.

Saptami is also the day the pujo becomes fully public. In Kolkata and Dhaka alike, offices are shut, the streets turn into rivers of people, the year's new clothes get their first outing, the bhog kitchens fire up at scale for the first time, and the dhak settles into the three-day groove it will keep until everything ends. For a child at a probasi pujo, Saptami is the day the festival stops being about pandal decoration and starts being about ritual: the first *pushpanjali* (পুষ্পাঞ্জলি) with flowers in your cupped hands, the first full *bhog* (ভোগ) lunch, the dhak finding its rhythm for the days ahead.

## When it happens

Maha Saptami falls on the seventh *tithi* (তিথি) — lunar day — of the bright fortnight of the month of Ashwin, the fortnight Bengalis call *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ). It follows Shashthi, the sixth tithi, on which the Goddess was awakened (*bodhon*) and welcomed. In the Gregorian calendar this lands in late September or October, shifting each year with the lunar calendar.

The day's most photogenic ritual, the bathing of the *nabapatrika* (নবপত্রিকা), is prescribed for the very early morning — traditionally before or around sunrise, within an auspicious window the almanacs (*panjika*, পঞ্জিকা) publish each year. The main Saptami puja follows through the morning, with pushpanjali typically offered late morning and bhog around midday. Because everything downstream depends on the tithi and the almanac window, the exact clock times differ from year to year and from panjika to panjika — one reason the priest's day starts in darkness.

## The rituals, step by step

### Nabapatrika — the nine plants of the Goddess

The heart of Saptami morning is the *nabapatrika* — literally "nine leaves" or "nine plants" — a bundle of nine specific plants bound together into a single figure. The most visible of the nine is a young banana plant with its leaves intact, which gives the bundle its affectionate household name: *kola bou* (কলা বউ), "the banana wife" or "banana bride."

The nine plants, and the form of the Goddess each is held to embody, are listed in the traditional paddhatis (ritual manuals) as follows:

1. **Kola** (কলা) — the banana or plantain plant, in ritual Sanskrit *rambha* — embodying **Brahmani** (ব্রহ্মাণী)
2. **Kochu** (কচু) — colocasia (taro) — embodying **Kalika** (কালিকা)
3. **Holud** (হলুদ) — turmeric, *haridra* — embodying **Durga** (দুর্গা) herself
4. **Jayanti** (জয়ন্তী) — the jayanti tree (a *Sesbania* species) — embodying **Kartiki** (কার্তিকী)
5. **Bel** (বেল) — the wood-apple or bilva tree, sacred to Shiva — embodying **Shiva** (শিবা), the Goddess as consort of Shiva, whom some sources name as Uma
6. **Dalim** (ডালিম) — pomegranate — embodying **Raktadantika** (রক্তদন্তিকা), "she of the blood-red teeth"
7. **Ashok** (অশোক) — the ashoka tree — embodying **Shokrahita** (শোকরহিতা), "she who removes sorrow"
8. **Maan** (মান) — the arum plant, *manaka* — embodying **Chamunda** (চামুণ্ডা)
9. **Dhan** (ধান) — rice paddy — embodying **Lakshmi** (লক্ষ্মী), goddess of abundance

Together they are the Devi in her ninefold vegetal form. Notice what these plants have in common: nearly all are food, medicine, or crop. Banana, taro, turmeric, arum, and paddy are the staples of a Bengali kitchen garden; bel and pomegranate are fruit and remedy; ashoka and jayanti are healing trees of the village landscape. The nabapatrika is, in effect, the harvest itself gathered into the shape of a goddess.

**How the bundle is made.** The eight smaller plants are tied to the trunk of the banana plant with a twining vine of *aparajita* (অপরাজিতা) — the butterfly-pea creeper, whose name means "the undefeated" — and bound with yellow thread; many manuals specify nine strands or nine knots. The making of the bundle is done by the priest or under his direction, usually late on Shashthi or in the first darkness of Saptami morning, so that the figure is ready for her bath at dawn. Details of the binding vary by lineage and by what the local market can supply, but the nine plants themselves are remarkably consistent across sources.

### Kola bou snan — the pre-dawn bath

Before sunrise on Saptami, the nabapatrika is carried in procession to a river, pond, or other body of water for her ceremonial bath — the *kola bou snan* (কলা বউ স্নান). In Kolkata this is one of the festival's iconic sights: priests in silk dhotis wading into the Hooghly at first light, the banana plant held aloft, the *dhak* (ঢাক) drummers pounding out the pujo's signature rhythm, conch shells sounding, women in red-bordered saris raising the *ulu* (উলু) — the high trilling auspicious call. Family pujos send a small party; big community pujos can send a crowd.

At the water's edge the priest bathes the nabapatrika. The classical manuals make even this bath elaborate: each of the nine plant-goddesses is to be bathed with her own waters — sources describe waters brought from different holy places — to her own mantra, and older texts even prescribe different musical accompaniment for different goddesses. In practice, most pujos today perform a simpler immersion and sprinkling in the river or pond, with Ganga water if the water body is not the Ganga herself.

After the bath comes the dressing, and this is where the "bride" appears. The dripping banana plant is draped in a cotton sari — typically white or cream with a broad red border, the archetypal Bengali *lal-paar* sari — worn as a woman wears it, with the leaves emerging above like a veiled head. *Sindoor* (সিঁদুর), vermilion, is applied — commonly to the leaves or the "face" region — exactly as it would be to a married woman. The effect is uncanny and tender: a tree that is unmistakably a lady.

The procession then returns to the pandal — dhak in front, kola bou carried upright, often under a small canopy — and the nabapatrika is installed in the tableau **on the right side of Ganesh**, standing beside him on her own small pedestal, where she remains for the rest of the pujo, receiving worship along with the murti through Dashami. From this moment the pujo has formally begun: many priests say the Saptami worship is offered first to the nabapatrika, and through her to the murti.

**"Ganesh's wife" — the affectionate mistake.** Because she stands beside Ganesh dressed as a new bride, generations of Bengalis have called the kola bou "Ganesher bou" — Ganesh's wife. It is a charming story, and children at every pujo will hear it — but priests and scholars agree it is a misnomer. In the ritual texts the nabapatrika is not Ganesh's consort at all: she is a form of Durga herself, the Devi in her plant body, and the Saptami mantras address her as the Goddess. Those who enjoy the argument point out a telling detail of placement: in Hindu convention a wife stands or sits at her husband's *left* (she is his *vamangi*, the one of his left side), while the kola bou is installed at Ganesh's *right* — the position of a deity in her own standing, not of a spouse. Puranic tradition, moreover, gives Ganesh his own wives — Riddhi and Siddhi — and the kola bou is not among them. The scholarly reading is more beautiful than the folk one: standing beside her son Ganesh is not his bride but his mother, in her most ancient form. That said, the two readings have coexisted cheerfully for generations — which is itself very Bengali — and no purohit has ever stopped an anjali to correct a grandmother.

### Mahasnan — the great bath in a mirror

Back at the pandal, the Devi herself must now be bathed — but a consecrated clay image, painted and ornamented, cannot be doused with water. The tradition's solution is elegant: the bath is given to her reflection. A polished mirror — the *darpan* (দর্পণ) — is placed on a platter before the murti, angled so that the image of the Goddess appears in it, and the entire bath is poured over the mirror; some lineages pour instead, or additionally, over the consecrated water-pot, the *ghat*. What touches the reflection, the ritual logic holds, touches her. (The same mirror will appear again on Dashami, when the farewell rites are likewise performed through the reflection.)

And what a bath it is. The *mahasnan* (মহাস্নান), "the great bath," is one of the most maximalist rituals in the Hindu ritual repertoire — the manuals treat the Devi as a sovereign being royally bathed, and accounts of the full prescription count the ingredients in the dozens (one traditional roster runs to nearly seventy-five items), each poured in prescribed order. The lists vary between manuals and lineages, but they consistently include:

- **Many kinds of water**: warm water (*ushnodok*, উষ্ণোদক) and cold; rain water; sea water; spring water; pond water; river water — Ganga water above all, and waters gathered from various *tirthas* (তীর্থ), the holy crossing-places of India; *shishir* (শিশির), dew — in the most poetic prescriptions, dew collected from lotus flowers, along with water scented with lotus pollen; coconut water; and waters infused with herbs, sandal, and flowers.
- **Many kinds of earth** (*nana mati*, নানা মাটি): the manuals list soils from an astonishing range of places — from a riverbank (both banks), from the confluence of rivers, from a mountain, from an anthill, from a crossroads where four roads meet, from the door of a temple, from the door of a king's palace, and — most famously — from the door of a courtesan's house (*beshya dwara mrittika*), a prescription whose meaning is debated to this day (readings range from the Goddess's acceptance of every person society honours or shuns, to older ideas about where virtue collects). The celebration of animal power is there too: soil turned up by a boar's tusk, by a bull's horns, by an elephant's tusk.
- **The sacred fives**: *panchagavya* (পঞ্চগব্য), the five gifts of the cow — milk, curd, ghee, and the rest — and *panchamrita* (পঞ্চামৃত), the five nectars: milk, curd, ghee, honey, and sugar.
- **Juices, pastes, and powders**: sugarcane juice, fruit juices, turmeric and other auspicious pastes, *panchakashaya* (decoctions of five barks) in some lists, sandal, and fragrant oils.

Each pouring has its own mantra, and — in the fullest form of the rite — its own music: the old manuals specify which melodic mode and which instruments should sound for particular libations, so that the bath is orchestrated like a royal ceremony. Few pujos anywhere perform every item today; the priest performs as complete a mahasnan as his lineage's manual, his supplies, and his morning allow, and the dashakarma shops of Kolkata sell packaged sets of the waters and soils for exactly this purpose. The point to carry away is not the checklist but the grammar of its maximalist poetry: the whole world — hot and cold, high mountain and low anthill, palace and brothel door, beast and river and dew — is brought to the bathing of the Mother, because nothing in the world is outside her. Every water and every soil participates in the bath.

### Kalparambha and sankalpa — the vow that frames the worship

Every Durga Puja is framed by a *kalpa* (কল্প) — a resolved span of worship — which opens with the rite of *kalparambha* (কল্পারম্ভ), "the beginning of the kalpa." At its centre is the *sankalpa* (সংকল্প), the formal vow: the priest — often together with the host of the pujo, in a community pujo a representative of the committee — sits before a consecrated water-pot (*ghat*, ঘট), takes up water, rice, and *kusha* (কুশ) grass in hand, performs the sipping-purification of *achamana*, and pronounces aloud the intention: naming the place, the almanac date, the worshipper, and the resolve to worship Bhagavati Durga through the prescribed days for the welfare of all.

When this happens depends on the lineage, because the tradition recognises several kalpas of different lengths — some households begin the count as early as Krishna-navami before Mahalaya, many begin on Shashthi, and some follow a *Saptamyadi kalpa* (সপ্তম্যাদিকল্প), "the kalpa beginning on Saptami," in which the sankalpa is made on this morning. If your pujo's priest performs a sankalpa on Saptami before the main worship, this is what you are watching; if it was done on Shashthi (as is common), Saptami opens instead with the nabapatrika rites directly. Neither is wrong — they are different valid framings from different manuals.

### Pran pratishtha and chakshu dan — when the clay becomes the Mother

Now comes the hinge of the whole festival: *pran pratishtha* (প্রাণপ্রতিষ্ঠা), "the establishing of life-breath." Until this rite, the murti — however magnificent — is ritually an image: straw, clay, pigment, and ornament. After it, for the span of the pujo, the tradition holds that the Goddess herself is present in it. Everything that follows — the offerings, the anjali, the arati, even the grief of immersion — makes sense only in light of this moment. From this moment the protima is not a statue being admired but a person being served: offered water for washing, cloth, food, betel, a midday rest.

What does the priest actually do? The rite is built from three interlocking actions:

- ***Nyasa*** (ন্যাস) — "placing." The priest first installs the deity in his own body, touching his heart, head, limbs, and fingers in prescribed sequence while reciting seed-syllables and the letters of the alphabet (*anga-nyasa* and *matrika-nyasa*). The logic is strict: only one in whom the Goddess has been awakened can awaken her in the image. Worship, the texts say, is of the divine, by the divine. In the priest's meditation the Goddess is carried, as it were, petal by petal from the lotus of his own heart onto the image.
- **The life-breath mantras.** Holding *kusha* grass, *durba* grass, or a flower, the priest touches the heart of the murti — and in fuller versions the eyes, and other limbs — while reciting the pran pratishtha mantras, which call the vital breaths (*prana*) of the Goddess into the image: "may her breath, her life, all her senses, speech, mind, sight, hearing... come and dwell happily here, for as long as the worship endures." The gesture is intimate and precise: the divine life is conducted, as it were, through the blade of grass from the priest's hand into the clay heart.
- ***Chakshu dan*** (চক্ষুদান) — "the gift of eyes." The opening of the deity's eyes marks her awakening into sight. In Bengal this exists in two layers that are easily confused. The artisan's chakshu dan is the famous painting of the eyes by the image-maker — in Kumortuli tradition performed on Mahalaya, the master painting the third eye and pupils in single strokes. The ritual awakening of those eyes belongs to the priest's consecration: as part of pran pratishtha the eyes are ritually touched and enlivened with mantra, and many describe the Devi's gaze as "switched on" only now. Lineages narrate and time these layers differently; what is common to all is the conviction that before this rite the image does not see you, and after it, she does.

What does "the clay becomes the Mother" mean theologically? The tradition is careful here, and it is worth being careful with children's questions too. The claim is not that a statue has been magically turned into flesh, nor that Hindus "worship clay." The claim is that the omnipresent Devi, who pervades everything always, has been invoked to make herself *especially present and available* in this form, for this span of days, so that finite beings can love, serve, feed, and see her — the way the same electricity that is everywhere in the wiring becomes light at a lamp. The murti is called an *adhara* — a vessel, a support — for a presence that was never absent. That is also why the festival can end the way it does: on Dashami the presence is ritually released (*visarjan* means "letting go"), and what returns to the river is, once again, clay. The Mother is not drowned; she is un-hosted, and goes home.

### The Saptami puja — sixteen offerings for a royal guest

With the Goddess now present, the priest performs the full Saptami worship, whose backbone is *shodashopachar* (ষোড়শোপচার) — worship "with sixteen courtesies," the honours a gracious household would offer a beloved royal guest. Standard lists give the sixteen as:

1. *Avahana* — invocation, the welcome
2. *Asana* — offering a seat
3. *Padya* — water for washing the feet
4. *Arghya* — water offered in honour, at the hands
5. *Achamaniya* — water for sipping
6. *Snana* — the bath (already given in glory as the mahasnan)
7. *Vastra* — clothing, new garments
8. *Abharana* — ornaments and adornment (in many lists; texts written for male deities put the sacred thread, *yajnopavita*, here)
9. *Gandha* — fragrance: sandal paste, perfumes
10. *Pushpa* — flowers, including the bel leaves and hibiscus the Devi loves
11. *Dhupa* — incense
12. *Dipa* — the offered lamp
13. *Naivedya* — food offering
14. *Tambula* — betel leaf and areca, the after-meal refreshment
15. *Pranam / Namaskara* — obeisance (some lists place *aarti*, the waving of lamps, here)
16. *Mantra pushpanjali* — the closing offering of flowers with mantra

The exact sixteen vary slightly from manual to manual — some count *madhuparka* (the honey-sweet welcome drink) or the mirror-showing (*darpana*) among them — but the shape is constant: arrival, seating, washing, bathing, dressing, adorning, feeding, entertaining, honouring. It is the sixteen-fold hospitality of classical puja, and its logic is that of a home receiving its most honoured guest. Alongside this, the priest worships the nabapatrika, Ganesh, Kartik, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Shiva above the tableau, the lion, and even Mahishasura — everyone in the frame receives their due. A *tantradharak* (তন্ত্রধারক) — the prompter who holds the manual and feeds the priest his lines — often sits beside him through the hours this takes.

### Pushpanjali — the people's offering

The one moment of Saptami that belongs to everybody is *pushpanjali* — the offering of flowers with joined hands (*anjali*, অঞ্জলি, means the cup formed by two palms held together). Late in the morning the priest calls the gathered devotees to the front, flowers and bel leaves are distributed into every pair of hands, and the priest recites the Sanskrit mantras line by line for the crowd to repeat — three rounds, three throws of flowers toward the Mother's feet. Custom holds that anjali is offered fasting, with bathed body and clean clothes; families straggle in accordingly hungry and slightly damp. For children this is often the first ritual they ever perform themselves, and the roar of a hundred voices stumbling through the Sanskrit together is one of the festival's great sounds.

A detail that shorter guides flatten: **the anjali mantras are different on each day**. Saptami's rounds ask, Ashtami's rounds praise the demon-slayer, Nabami's rounds contemplate the Goddess as time itself. Saptami, the first day, is the asking day — the Devi is the mother to whom one may simply hold out a hand. The first round asks plainly for what we lack:

> नमः आयुर्देहि यशो देहि भाग्यं भगवति देहि मे ।
> पुत्रान् देहि धनं देहि सर्वान् कामांश्च देहि मे ॥
>
> নমঃ আয়ুর্দেহি যশো দেহি ভাগ্যং ভগবতি দেহি মে।
> পুত্রান্ দেহি ধনং দেহি সর্বান্ কামাংশ্চ দেহি মে।।

*namaḥ āyur dehi yaśo dehi bhāgyaṁ bhagavati dehi me ·
putrān dehi dhanaṁ dehi sarvān kāmāṁś ca dehi me*

**In simple words:** Bhagavati, give me long life; give me honour; give me good fortune; give me children, give me the means to live, give me everything my heart longs for. No preamble, no philosophy — just a child's open hand held out to the Mother.

The second round asks to be relieved of what we carry — a drumbeat of *hara, hara, hara*: take it, take it, take it:

> हर पापं हर क्लेशं हर शोकं हरासुखम् ।
> हर रोगं हर क्षोभं हर मारीं हरप्रिये ॥
>
> হর পাপং হর ক্লেশং হর শোকং হরাসুখম্।
> হর রোগং হর ক্ষোভং হর মারীং হরপ্রিয়ে।।

*hara pāpaṁ hara kleśaṁ hara śokaṁ harāsukham ·
hara rogaṁ hara kṣobhaṁ hara mārīṁ hara-priye*

**In simple words:** O beloved of Shiva — take away my wrongs, my suffering, my grief and unhappiness; take away disease, take away unrest, take away pestilence. Where the first round asked for gifts, this one hands over burdens.

And the third round asks for victory and a whole life:

> संग्रामे विजयं देहि धनं देहि सदा गृहे ।
> धर्मार्थकामसम्पत्तिं देहि देवी नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> সংগ্রামে বিজয়ং দেহি ধনং দেহি সদা গৃহে।
> ধর্মার্থকামসম্পত্তিং দেহি দেবী নমোঽস্তু তে।।

*saṁgrāme vijayaṁ dehi dhanaṁ dehi sadā gṛhe ·
dharmārtha-kāma-sampattiṁ dehi devī namo'stu te*

**In simple words:** grant me victory in every battle I must fight, and plenty always at home; give me the full wealth of a good life — righteousness, means, and joy. Salutation to you, Devi.

The flowers actually leave your hands under the offering refrain — *eṣa sacandana-puṣpa-bilvapatrāñjaliḥ...* — "this handful of flowers and bel leaves touched with sandal" — addressed to Bhadrakali, Bhagavati, Durga, she who moves surrounded by ten million yoginis. The day closes with the Saptami pranam, the most beloved of all Devi verses:

> सर्वमङ्गलमङ्गल्ये शिवे सर्वार्थसाधिके ।
> शरण्ये त्र्यम्बके गौरि नारायणि नमोऽस्तुते ॥
>
> সর্বমঙ্গলমঙ্গল্যে শিবে সর্বার্থসাধিকে।
> শরণ্যে ত্র্যম্বকে গৌরি নারায়ণি নমোঽস্তুতে।।

*sarva-maṅgala-maṅgalye śive sarvārtha-sādhike ·
śaraṇye tryambake gauri nārāyaṇi namo'stu te*

**In simple words:** O auspiciousness within everything auspicious, gracious one who accomplishes every aim, refuge of all, three-eyed Gauri, Narayani — we bow to you. If you learn one verse of the whole pujo by heart, learn this one.

All three days' anjali texts, with the opening purification, the offering refrain, and each day's pranam, are given in full in the pushpanjali chapter ([Pushpanjali Mantras — Saptami, Mahashtami, Nabami](15-mantras-pushpanjali.md)). If you miss a word on the morning itself, tradition is untroubled: the offering is the attention, not the elocution.

### Bhog — the first full feast

Saptami is the first full *bhog* day — the day the Goddess, now alive, is formally fed a cooked midday meal, and her leftovers (*prasad*, প্রসাদ) feed the whole community. The classic Saptami bhog is vegetarian and cooked without onion or garlic: *khichuri* (খিচুড়ি) of rice and moong dal, *labra* (লাবড়া) — the great mixed-vegetable mash — fried vegetables (*bhaja*), chutney, and *payesh* (পায়েস), rice pudding, with regional and household variations beyond counting (some traditional family pujos, following older Shakta prescriptions, include fish or meat; most community pujos do not). The food is offered behind a drawn curtain — the Mother dines in privacy — the priest presents it with mantra and water, and then the curtain opens and serving begins. This is also the first day bhog is cooked and distributed at scale, and sitting in long rows on the floor, eating khichuri-bhog from leaf plates on Saptami afternoon, is as central to the Bengali experience of pujo as anything the priest does.

The evening brings *sandhya arati* (সন্ধ্যা আরতি) — lamps, incense smoke, dhak — and the first full night of pandal-hopping, the queues thickening by the hour. The pujo is now truly on.

## Why it matters

Saptami matters because it is the day the festival's central mystery is enacted. Every tradition that uses images must answer the question: how can the infinite be present in a made thing? Saptami's answer — nyasa, mantra, the grass-blade touch on a clay heart — is one of the most thought-through answers any tradition has produced, and watching it teaches more theology than a shelf of books: presence is invoked, not assumed; the image is a door, not an idol in the dismissive sense; and what is invoked must, in the end, be let go.

Saptami also matters because it keeps the festival honest about its roots. The nabapatrika is the plainest evidence that beneath the Puranic warrior-queen lies an older worship: of grain, of greens, of the fertile earth at harvest time. Scholars widely read the nine-plant rite as an agrarian — possibly pre-Vedic, pre-Puranic — stratum of the festival, the thanksgiving of farming communities absorbed into the Brahminical liturgy so thoroughly that the two now begin the same morning together. When the paddy and the turmeric are bathed and dressed and set beside the sculpted Goddess, the festival is remembering where it came from — remembering that the Goddess *is* the growing world.

And for the community, Saptami is the great equaliser: pushpanjali puts the same flowers in every pair of hands, and bhog seats everyone in the same row on the same floor.

## The story behind it

The Puranic frame for the whole autumn festival is *akal bodhan* (অকালবোধন) — the "untimely awakening." In the Bengali telling, Rama, needing the Goddess's blessing before his battle with Ravana, worshipped her in autumn, the gods' night-time, when she would ordinarily not be roused; Durga Puja follows his out-of-season precedent, which is why the awakening rites of Shashthi and the enlivening of Saptami carry the flavour of waking someone who was asleep.

The goddess-names of the nine plants tell their own stories. Several — Brahmani, Kalika, Chamunda — belong to the *matrikas*, the ancient mother-goddesses of the Devi Mahatmya's battle scenes. Raktadantika, "she of the blood-red teeth," appears in the Devi Mahatmya's own list of the Devi's future forms, as does Shakambhari — "she who bears vegetables" — the form in which the Goddess promises to nourish the world with plants grown from her own body during a great drought. The nabapatrika is, in effect, Shakambhari's promise made ritual: the Goddess *as* vegetation, feeding her children.

As for the kola bou's bridal sari and sindoor — the tradition dresses her as a married woman because the Devi comes home to Bengal as a daughter married into Shiva's distant household, and every married daughter visiting her parents is dressed in her best. The "Ganesh's wife" tale is a later folk embroidery on that costume; the texts themselves know her only as the Mother.

## In a probasi pujo

Away from the Hooghly, Saptami morning is an exercise in loving improvisation.

**Kola bou snan without a river.** Most probasi pujos cannot walk a procession to a ghat at dawn. Common solutions, all ritually accepted: bathing the nabapatrika at the society's swimming pool or an apartment-complex water body; a bath from buckets or a large tub in which a few spoonfuls of Ganga water (brought back from India or bought bottled) are mixed, on the principle that Ganga water sanctifies whatever it touches; or a symbolic sprinkling at the pandal itself. The procession survives in miniature — a walk around the society block with the dhak (or a dhak recording), a few members in lal-paar saris, conch and ulu at full volume, to the amusement and delight of non-Bengali neighbours. Sourcing the nine plants is its own annual adventure; a potted banana plant from a nursery serves perfectly, turmeric and paddy can usually be found, and priests permit substitutions or a reduced bundle with the full nine invoked by mantra when jayanti or ashok cannot be had in Pune. What cannot be substituted is the dawn itself: communities that keep the early kola bou snan — sleepy children in new clothes at a cold poolside in the half-light — invariably name it among the most moving moments of their pujo.

**Compressed timelines.** When Saptami falls on a weekday, the pre-dawn schedule is often the ritual's salvation rather than its casualty: the nabapatrika snan and the start of the puja happen early precisely so that office-goers can attend anjali at a fixed, published time before logging in — or the anjali is scheduled for the evening, a pragmatic shift many probasi priests accept since the tithi often extends. Mahasnan is performed in a condensed form — the mirror, the principal waters, panchamrita, a representative set of the soils from a dashakarma kit shipped from Kolkata; the dozens of prescribed waters travel as a purohit's box of small bottles and pinches, symbolic quantities standing in for the world's rivers, exactly as tradition has always permitted. The grammar of the rite is kept while its length is trimmed. Bhog, cooked by volunteer rota in a community kitchen since dawn, remains gloriously uncompressed: whatever else is shortened on a working Saptami, nobody shortens the khichuri.

**For the children.** Saptami is the best day to give a child a job: carrying flowers for anjali, holding the kola bou's umbrella in the procession, ringing the bell during arati. And it is the day to stand them in front of the tableau and tell them the true story of the lady in the sari beside Ganesh — not his wife, but his mother, wearing her oldest dress: the green world itself.

## Words to know

- ***Nabapatrika*** (নবপত্রিকা) — "nine plants"; the bundle of nine sacred plants worshipped as nine forms of the Goddess.
- ***Kola bou*** (কলা বউ) — "banana bride"; the affectionate name for the nabapatrika, from its sari-draped banana plant.
- ***Snan*** (স্নান) — bath; *kola bou snan* is the pre-dawn bathing procession.
- ***Mahasnan*** (মহাস্নান) — the "great bath" of the Devi, performed on her mirror reflection with many waters, soils, and substances.
- ***Darpan*** (দর্পণ) — the mirror that receives the bath on the Goddess's behalf.
- ***Ushnodok*** (উষ্ণোদক) — warm water, one of the mahasnan's prescribed waters.
- ***Shishir*** (শিশির) — dew, another of the bath's poetic ingredients.
- ***Panchagavya / panchamrita*** (পঞ্চগব্য / পঞ্চামৃত) — the five cow-gifts and the five nectars (milk, curd, ghee, honey, sugar) used in ritual bathing.
- ***Kalparambha*** (কল্পারম্ভ) — the formal opening of the worship-span; ***sankalpa*** (সংকল্প) — the spoken vow that begins it.
- ***Pran pratishtha*** (প্রাণপ্রতিষ্ঠা) — "establishing the life-breath"; the rite that invokes the living presence of the Goddess into the image.
- ***Chakshu dan*** (চক্ষুদান) — "gift of eyes"; the painting and ritual awakening of the deity's eyes.
- ***Nyasa*** (ন্যাস) — the priest's "placing" of the deity in his own body by touch and mantra before consecrating the image.
- ***Kusha*** (কুশ) — the sacred grass held during sankalpa and used to touch the murti's heart.
- ***Shodashopachar*** (ষোড়শোপচার) — worship with sixteen offerings, from seat and foot-water to food, betel, and flowers.
- ***Pushpanjali / anjali*** (পুষ্পাঞ্জলি / অঞ্জলি) — the congregational offering of flowers from cupped hands, repeated after the priest; a different set of mantras each day.
- ***Bhog*** (ভোগ) — the cooked food offered to the Goddess and shared as *prasad*; Saptami is the first full bhog day.
- ***Tithi*** (তিথি) — a lunar day; the panjika almanac fixes each ritual's tithi and timing.
- ***Aparajita*** (অপরাজিতা) — "the undefeated"; the butterfly-pea creeper that binds the nine plants together — and also the name of a rite of Dashami, when the same undefeated Goddess is worshipped before farewell.
