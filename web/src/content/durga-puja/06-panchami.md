## What it is

Every festival has a first day. Durga Puja, being Bengali, also has a day *before* the first day — and loves it almost more. That day is *Panchami* (পঞ্চমী), the fifth day of *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ), the goddess's fortnight that began at *Mahalaya* (মহালয়া).

Strictly speaking, almost nothing is required to happen on Panchami. The scriptural machinery of the pujo — the invocation, the awakening, the consecration — belongs to *Shashthi* (ষষ্ঠী), the sixth day ([Maha Shashthi — The Awakening](07-shashthi.md)), and the great worship days are Saptami, Ashtami, and Navami. Panchami is the threshold, the day of homecomings: the pandal gets its last bamboo lashed and its last cloth pleated, the *protima* (প্রতিমা) — the sculpted image of Durga and her four children — arrives from the artisan's workshop if it has not already and takes her place on the *bedi* (বেদী), the platform, the lights are switched on, the *dhak* (ঢাক) sounds its first real volleys, and the para — the neighbourhood — exhales a year of waiting.

And precisely because nothing is officially required, everything unofficial happens. In Kolkata, Panchami evening now draws crowds that a generation ago would have been thought large for Saptami; newspapers joke every year that "Panchami is the new Saptami," and pandal-hopping — the competitive, joyous touring of pandals — effectively begins tonight. The cultural programmes open, the food stalls fire up, the new *punjabi* comes out of its packet a day early, and in the kitchens the coconut meets the jaggery for the welcome sweets ([Anando Naru: The Sweets Made at Home for Ma](05-anando-naru.md)). Panchami is the festival's eve — the door standing open, the goddess's footsteps on the stair.

## When it happens

Panchami is a *tithi* (তিথি) — a lunar day — not a calendar date. It is the fifth tithi of the bright (waxing) fortnight of the month of Ashwin, falling in late September or October, the day after Chaturthi and the day before Shashthi. Because lunar days do not line up neatly with sunrise-to-sunrise days, a tithi may begin in the middle of an afternoon and end the following morning; the *panjika* (পঞ্জিকা), the Bengali almanac, rules on which calendar day "counts" — and, as we will see below, this sliding of tithis is one honest reason different sources disagree about what happens on Panchami evening.

One caution about names, since it trips up many people: **Panchami of Durga Puja is not "Sri Panchami."** *Sri Panchami* (শ্রীপঞ্চমী), also called Vasant Panchami, is the fifth day of the bright fortnight of Magh, deep in late winter — the day of *Saraswati Puja* (সরস্বতী পূজা), when students lay their books before the goddess of learning. Same word, different fortnight, different goddess, different season. The Panchami of this chapter is sometimes called Maha Panchami to distinguish it.

## Step by step

Panchami has no single fixed liturgy, so what follows is the day as it actually unfolds — part ritual, part logistics, part pure atmosphere.

### The protima comes home

For weeks the image has stood in the workshop — in Kolkata, famously in *Kumortuli* (কুমোরটুলি), the potters' quarter where families of artisans have shaped the goddess from river clay and straw for generations. From Mahalaya onward the lanes there jam with waiting trucks, and club after club takes its goddess home. The loading is its own spectacle: bearers easing the great clay tableau onto a lorry padded with straw, committee members steadying her at every pothole, and often a *dhaki* (ঢাকি) drumming right there on the truck bed as it sways through streets where passers-by stop and fold their hands. Children run alongside shouting the two words that mean the year has turned: *Thakur esheche!* (ঠাকুর এসেছে) — "The deity has come!"

Many pujas fetch their protima days earlier; some install her only now; a probasi pujo may be unpacking a crate that left Kumortuli weeks ago. Either way, by Panchami she should be standing on her bedi, and there is a word for that too — the image is *sthapito*, from *sthapan* (স্থাপন), "establishing." The house that has been rising since the khunti puja finally has its resident. Established, but not yet awakened: until the rites of Shashthi she is, in the traditional understanding, the most beautiful sculpture in the world, waiting to become a presence. Priests differ on how much rite the day needs — in many traditions the deity is formally "brought" today with a brief welcome, in others everything waits for Shashthi — but no tradition is casual about the moment the ten-armed silhouette first stands in her own pandal.

### Boron at the gate — welcoming her like family

When the truck arrives at the pandal, the welcome is not left to the men with ropes. The women of the para come out with a *baran dala* (বরণডালা) — the round welcome-tray of Bengali households — bearing the auspicious things: *sindoor* (সিঁদুর, vermilion), paddy grains (*dhaan*, ধান), *durba* grass (দূর্বা), betel leaf and areca nut (*paan-supari*, পান-সুপারি), turmeric, flowers, and sweets. A lamp is circled, the conch shell — *shankha* (শঙ্খ) — is blown, and the air fills with *uludhwani* (উলুধ্বনি): the high, rolling trill that Bengali women make with the tongue at every auspicious moment, also written *ululudhwani*. It announces joy and wards off ill omen, and no goddess crosses a Bengali threshold without it.

A note of honesty here: the formal, scripture-adjacent rite called *boron* (বরণ) — married women feeding the goddess sweets, touching her face, bidding her farewell — properly belongs to Dashami, the day of parting. The welcome on arrival is boron's mirror image, done in the same spirit and with the same tray, because Bengal receives Durga exactly as it receives a daughter arriving home: at the gate, with lamp, conch, and trilling. Different paras do more or less of it; none skips it entirely.

### Bodhon — and the honest confusion about its evening

Now the ritual question this day is famous for. *Bodhon* (বোধন) means "awakening": the rite in which the goddess is roused and invited into the clay image and into a *bel* (বেল, wood-apple) branch, remembering Rama's out-of-season invocation — *akal bodhan* (অকালবোধন) — before his war with Ravana.

When does it happen? The almanacs and priestly manuals are clear: *kalparambha* (কল্পারম্ভ), the formal commencement, is performed on Shashthi morning, and bodhon — together with *amantran* (আমন্ত্রণ), the invitation, and *adhibas* (অধিবাস), the sanctifying residence — belongs to Shashthi's dusk, the sandhya hour being prescribed as ideal. Yet you will find plenty of articles, and plenty of real pujas, describing bodhon on Panchami evening. Both are reporting something true, and the discrepancy has honest roots:

- Tithis slide. When the Shashthi tithi begins on Panchami's calendar evening, the evening rites can land, to any observer, "on Panchami."
- Lineages differ. Family pujas — the *bonedi bari* (বনেদি বাড়ি) houses of old Kolkata, Howrah, and the districts — follow their own inherited manuals. In some houses a lamp is lit on Panchami that will burn unbroken until Dashami, and the puja is reckoned to have begun; several of the oldest houses are described as beginning their bodhon observances well before Devi Paksha altogether, by their own reckoning. Each house keeps its own rulebook, and none regards the neighbours' as binding.
- Community pujas simplify. A barowari pujo juggling priest availability and office-going members may fold rites together pragmatically.

So the fair summary for a learner is: the mainstream panjika tradition places bodhon on Shashthi evening; a visible minority of traditions, and the drift of tithis, put awakening rites on Panchami evening; and no one should be startled to see either. What no tradition does is treat Panchami as an ordinary day.

### Chokkhu daan — the giving of eyes

One more rite hovers around this threshold. *Chokkhu daan* (চক্ষুদান), "the gift of eyes," is the painting of the goddess's eyes — the last strokes that turn clay into gaze. In the workshops of Kumortuli this is classically done at dawn on Mahalaya by the most senior artisan, brush steady, often after prayer; photographs of that moment are among the most famous images of the festival. But not every image is finished in the workshop. Some pujas, especially ones that receive the protima early or commission the finishing on site, have the eyes painted at the pandal in the final days — around Panchami — or keep the protima draped and leave the unveiling of the finished face for Shashthi. Practice varies by puja and by artisan lineage; what does not vary is the meaning. Until the eyes are given, she is an image. After, she looks back.

### The pujo of lists — fordo, bajar, and the last logistics

Panchami is also the great day of lists, because everything that must be true by Shashthi morning becomes true today, or else. The *fordo* (ফর্দ) — the master list of every ritual requirement, from sindoor to 108 lotuses — gets its final check against the *purohit*'s specifications, and the materials are laid out stage by stage: the *ghot* (ঘট), the sarees for the goddess and the nabapatrika, the *naibedya* (নৈবেদ্য) bowls, the lamps set aside for Sandhi Puja. Flowers are ordered, fruit and ghee secured, the bel branch for tomorrow's bodhon arranged. Households, meanwhile, finish what Bengal calls *pujor bajar* (পুজোর বাজার) — the festival shopping, above all new clothes for every day of the festival, the best kept for Ashtami. And in the kitchens the karais come out, because the Mother's welcome requires naru — for many families, rolling the welcome sweets on Panchami evening is an observance in itself, told in full in its own chapter ([Anando Naru: The Sweets Made at Home for Ma](05-anando-naru.md)).

### Udbodhon — inaugurations, ribbons, and the switching-on of light

Then there is the modern ritual, nowhere in any scripture and now utterly entrenched: *udbodhon* (উদ্বোধন), the formal inauguration. The big Kolkata pujas invite dignitaries — film stars, ministers; the Chief Minister of West Bengal famously inaugurates dozens of pujas in person and hundreds more remotely, a marathon that in recent years begins from Mahalaya itself and crescendos through Chaturthi and Panchami, with conch shells, dhak, and chanting at each ribbon. The evening lighting is its own event: the lane-arching displays of Chandannagar's famed lighting artisans blaze on for the first time, theme pandals unveil the year's design and throw open their gates ahead of the rush, and awards juries begin their rounds. By Panchami night the city is effectively open, and lakhs of people are already walking it.

### The para wakes up — stalls, stages, and the smell of Pujo

Under the lights, the ordinary magic. The *dhakis* arrive from their districts, drums garlanded in white feathers, and give the year's first practice rolls. The food stalls that ring every large pandal open on Panchami — rolls, *phuchka* (ফুচকা), chowmein, biryani, jilipi — and will not bank their fires for five days. The para's cultural stage hosts its first evening: children's recitations rehearsed for weeks, a *Rabindra sangeet* set, a local band. Mikes are tested with the immortal "hello, hello, ek-dui-teen." Volunteers get their badges and their self-importance. New clothes appear. Somewhere the first *dhunuchi naach* (ধুনুচি নাচ) — the swaying dance with smoking incense braziers — breaks out unofficially, well before any aarti requires it. This is the pandal-para atmosphere the word "Pujo" actually conjures for most Bengalis: not a rite, but a neighbourhood transformed.

And late at night, when the crowds thin, there is a particular feeling regulars will name unprompted: the pandal lit and nearly empty, the protima perhaps still draped until her unveiling, the fordo checked one last time, the dhak resting. The goddess has arrived; she has not yet been woken. Tomorrow evening, under a bel tree, that changes.

## Why it matters

Panchami matters the way the last day of waiting always matters — it is the sweetest part of any arrival. Bengali culture is unusually honest about this: the anticipation of Pujo is treated as part of Pujo, and Panchami is anticipation's own feast day.

It is also the festival's great threshold, and thresholds are where Bengal locates meaning. The goddess has arrived but not yet awakened; the pandal is finished but not yet consecrated; the celebration has begun but the worship has not. Standing in a lit, incense-scented, still-unconsecrated pandal on Panchami night, you can feel the difference between a building and a temple — and feel it become the latter overnight. For children, Panchami teaches the festival's deepest lesson painlessly: that Durga comes as a daughter to her parents' home, and that the day before a daughter arrives — the airing of rooms, the cooking, the pacing by the gate — is itself an act of love.

Practically, too, Panchami is load-bearing. Everything that must be true by Shashthi morning — protima in place, priest arrived, ingredients bought, flowers ordered, stage tested — becomes true on Panchami, or else.

## The story behind it

The reason the "real" rites wait for Shashthi is a story every Bengali child learns. In the Krittibasi Ramayana's telling, Rama needed the blessing of the great goddess before facing Ravana — but it was autumn, and the gods were asleep; the customary time for her worship was spring, the season of the older *Basanti Puja*. So Rama performed *akal bodhan*, the untimely awakening, rousing Durga out of season under a bel tree. Bengal's entire autumn festival is that audacity institutionalised: we wake her because Rama dared to, and the awakening — bodhon — is therefore the true beginning, fixed to Shashthi's dusk. Everything before it, Panchami included, is preparation for that moment; the fortnight from Mahalaya is Devi Paksha, the goddess's own fortnight, when she is already travelling.

Alongside the epic runs the domestic story, and it is the one Panchami really belongs to: Uma, daughter of the mountain, married far away to the ascetic Shiva of Kailash, comes home to her parents Menaka and Giriraj for four days each autumn, children in tow. The songs of this homecoming — the *agomoni* (আগমনী) songs — are songs of a mother waiting, and Panchami is the last day of that waiting. On both sides of the border — *Epar Bangla* and *Opar Bangla*, West Bengal and erstwhile East Bengal, today's Bangladesh — this is the shared emotional grammar of the festival, and families with roots in Dhaka or Barishal keep it exactly as families from Hooghly do; the pujo of Bangladesh runs on the same tithis with the same rites. Where East Bengal-rooted families sometimes differ is in flavour, not structure — their own house customs, their own agomoni repertoire, and, famously, their own opinions at the fish market on Shashthi eve.

## In a probasi pujo

Transplant all this to a housing-society clubhouse in Magarpatta City, and Panchami changes costume but not character. There is no Kumortuli truck rolling through the night — our protima travels by tempo from a workshop across the city, or waits, carefully sheeted, from an earlier delivery — but the arrival is met the same way: lamp, conch, whatever uludhwani the assembled throats can raise, and phone cameras standing in for the para's thousand eyes.

Panchami here is above all the day of last logistics, and our own account books say so plainly: the 2022 ledgers show Panchami-day entries for grocery runs and for the *baran dala* purchases — the sindoor, paan, dhaan, and durba that no Pune supermarket aisle labels as such, hunted down in the old-city markets. It is the day the *purohit* (পুরোহিত) — the priest, often flown or driven in, sometimes shared with another pujo — is received, settled, and walked through the hall; the day the pujor bajar is triple-checked against his fordo (108 lotuses do not materialise in Pune on Ashtami morning without a Panchami phone call); the day flowers, fruit, ghee, and the bel branch for tomorrow's bodhon are secured. Committee members leave offices early; someone's dining table disappears under marigold garlands; in someone's kitchen the last batch of naru is being rolled for Ma's welcome ([Anando Naru: The Sweets Made at Home for Ma](05-anando-naru.md)).

And then, by evening, the other Panchami arrives even here: the sound check, the children's dress rehearsal dissolving into chaos, the first shift of the food counters, neighbours — Marathi, Punjabi, Tamil — drifting in to ask when the festival "actually starts," and receiving the only correct answer: tomorrow, officially. But stay — it has already begun.

## Words to know

- *Panchami* (পঞ্চমী) — the fifth lunar day; here, the fifth day of Devi Paksha, the pujo's eve. "Maha Panchami" in formal usage.
- *Tithi* (তিথি) — a lunar day, which slides across calendar days; the *panjika* (পঞ্জিকা) almanac adjudicates.
- *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ) — the goddess's fortnight, from Mahalaya to Kojagori purnima.
- *Protima* (প্রতিমা) / *murti* (মূর্তি) — the sculpted image of the goddess; *sthapan* (স্থাপন) is its installation; the *bedi* (বেদী) is the platform she stands on.
- *Kumortuli* (কুমোরটুলি) — Kolkata's hereditary potters' quarter, source of the most celebrated protimas.
- *Bodhon* (বোধন) — the "awakening" of the goddess; *akal bodhan* (অকালবোধন) is Rama's out-of-season invocation it commemorates. Mainstream practice sets it on Shashthi evening, with *amantran* (আমন্ত্রণ) and *adhibas* (অধিবাস); some lineages and sliding tithis place awakening rites on Panchami evening.
- *Kalparambha* (কল্পারম্ভ) — the formal commencement of the puja, on Shashthi morning.
- *Sri Panchami* (শ্রীপঞ্চমী) — a different Panchami entirely: Saraswati Puja day in the month of Magh. Not this day.
- *Chokkhu daan* (চক্ষুদান) — the painting of the goddess's eyes, classically at dawn on Mahalaya in the workshop, at the pandal in the final days for some pujas.
- *Boron* (বরণ) — the women's welcome/farewell rite with the *baran dala* (বরণডালা) tray of sindoor, dhaan, durba, paan, and sweets.
- *Uludhwani* (উলুধ্বনি) — the rolling auspicious trill made by women at sacred moments; also written *ululudhwani*.
- *Dhak* (ঢাক) — the great barrel drum of the pujo; its player is the *dhaki* (ঢাকি), arriving from the districts by Panchami, drum garlanded in white feathers.
- *Fordo* (ফর্দ) — the master list of every puja requirement, checked and re-checked on Panchami.
- *Pujor bajar* (পুজোর বাজার) — the festival shopping, above all new clothes for every day, the best kept for Ashtami.
- *Udbodhon* (উদ্বোধন) — the modern inauguration ceremony of a pandal, by Panchami a city-wide festival of its own.
- *Dhunuchi naach* (ধুনুচি নাচ) — the dance with smoking incense braziers, officially of the aarti, unofficially of any Panchami evening.
- *Pandal-hopping* — the competitive, joyous touring of pandals, now beginning on Panchami night.
- *Purohit* (পুরোহিত) — the priest who will conduct the rites from Shashthi; welcomed and settled on Panchami in most probasi pujos.
