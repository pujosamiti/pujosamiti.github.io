## What it is

Long before the lights go up, before the *dhak* (ঢাক) drummers arrive, before the goddess herself is anywhere near finished, Durga Puja begins with a single bamboo pole.

*Khunti puja* (খুঁটি পুজো) — sometimes spelled *khuti puja* — is the ceremony in which a puja committee worships and plants the first structural post of the year's *pandal* (প্যান্ডেল), the temporary pavilion in which Ma Durga will be housed and worshipped. The word *khunti* (খুঁটি) means simply a stake, peg or post. On the chosen day, a priest consecrates a bamboo pole, prayers are offered to the earth and to the goddess, and the pole is ceremonially set upright at the spot where the pandal will rise. From that moment the pujo is considered officially "on": construction can begin, the countdown has started, and everyone involved — organisers, artisans, priests, neighbours — understands that the season has opened.

If you grew up outside Bengal, the nearest familiar comparison is a *bhoomi pujan*, the ground-breaking rite performed before building a house: a ritual that asks the earth's permission and seeks blessings before construction begins. Khunti puja is exactly that, but for the goddess's temporary home — and over the last few decades it has also grown into something more: a festival kickoff event in its own right, complete with drummers, sweets, celebrity guests at the big Kolkata pujas, and the dramatic unveiling of the year's pandal theme.

It is fitting that this book turns to khunti puja so early, because this is genuinely where each year's pujo begins.

## When it happens

There is no single fixed date for khunti puja, and this is worth understanding properly.

The most popular and traditional occasion is *Rath Yatra* (রথযাত্রা), the chariot festival of Jagannath that falls in the Bengali month of Ashadh (June–July), roughly three months before Durga Puja. Many committees instead choose *Ulto Rath* (উল্টোরথ), the return chariot journey about a week later. Rath Yatra's connection to Durga Puja runs deep in Bengali custom: it is on this day that many *bonedi bari* (বনেদি বাড়ি) — the old aristocratic households of Bengal that have run family pujas for centuries — traditionally begin work on the wooden-and-bamboo frame of the Durga image itself, with rituals such as *kathamo puja* (কাঠামো পূজা), the worship of the idol's frame (told in full in [The Making of the Murti](03-the-making-of-the-murti.md)). Rath Yatra is counted among the most auspicious days of the Hindu calendar, so beginning the goddess's house on the same day that her idol's skeleton is begun feels natural and right.

That said, plenty of committees hold their khunti puja on other auspicious days — some on *Akshaya Tritiya*, some during the month of Shravan, some as late as Janmashtami (August–September), and some simply on a convenient weekend blessed by the *panjika* (পঞ্জিকা), the Bengali almanac that lists auspicious timings. In Kolkata, where the biggest committees compete to build astonishing themed pandals that take months of engineering, khunti pujas now cluster earlier and earlier in the year. The rule of thumb is simple: the bigger the pandal ambition, the earlier the pole goes into the ground. News reports from recent years show major clubs like Sreebhumi Sporting Club, Chetla Agrani and Dum Dum Park's committees holding their khunti pujas on Rath Yatra day itself, often with ministers, actors and huge crowds in attendance.

So the honest answer to "when is khunti puja?" is: on an auspicious day of the committee's choosing, most famously Rath Yatra, and always far enough ahead of Mahalaya (see [Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md)) to leave time for the pandal to be built.

## The rituals, step by step

Khunti puja is a short ceremony — usually under an hour of actual ritual — but it follows a recognisable sequence. Details vary from committee to committee and priest to priest; what follows is the broadly common form. Nothing here requires a temple: the whole thing happens outdoors, on the bare ground where the pandal will stand.

### Choosing the day and preparing the site

The committee consults the *panjika* or a priest to fix an auspicious day and time. The pandal site — a park, a street corner, a courtyard, a housing-society ground — is swept and cleaned. The exact spot where the first post will stand is often chosen with care: many committees plant it at or near the centre of the future structure, or where the goddess's platform will be. A small pit is dug for the pole. Women of the community may decorate the spot with *alpona* (আলপনা), the white rice-paste floor patterns drawn for auspicious occasions.

### Dressing the khunti

The bamboo pole itself is treated as an object of worship, not a mere building material. It is typically wrapped in new cloth — red and white being the favourite colours — smeared with *sindoor* (সিঁদুর, vermilion) and turmeric, and garlanded with flowers and mango leaves. Some committees tie a *gamchha* (গামছা), the traditional chequered cotton towel, around it. By the time the priest arrives, the humble bamboo stake looks like a guest of honour.

### The sankalpa

Every Hindu ritual of consequence opens with a *sankalpa* (সংকল্প) — a formal declaration of intent, spoken by the priest on behalf of the person or community undertaking the rite. In a household puja the head of the family takes the sankalpa; at a khunti puja it is usually the committee's president or secretary, or a group of senior members, who sit before the priest. The sankalpa states, in effect: *we, this community, in this place, in this year, resolve to celebrate the worship of Devi Durga, and we begin today.* It binds the whole season's effort — the fundraising, the construction, the five days of worship, the immersion — into a single vow made at this moment. This is why committee members take being present at the khunti puja seriously: the vow is made in their name.

### Worship of the earth and the pole

The priest — the *purohit* (পুরোহিত) — then performs the puja proper. A *ghot* (ঘট), the clay or metal pitcher that represents divine presence in Bengali ritual, is commonly installed: filled with water, topped with mango leaves and a green coconut, and marked with sindoor. The ground is purified by sprinkling *gangajal* (গঙ্গাজল), water of the Ganga, often using a mango-leaf sprig. Offerings of flowers, rice, sweets, incense and lamp-flame are made to Ganesha (who is always worshipped first, as remover of obstacles), to the earth, and to Durga, whose blessing is sought for the safety of everyone who will work on the structure and the success of the puja itself. Mantras are chanted; a coconut is a near-universal presence, whether atop the ghot, offered whole, or placed with the pole. (Committees differ on the specifics — some break a coconut, some bury one in the pit, some simply offer it — and no single "correct" version exists.)

In honouring the earth before digging into her, many priests reach for the old verse of apology to Bhumi, the Earth — the same one traditionally murmured on setting one's feet on the ground each morning:

> समुद्रवसने देवि पर्वतस्तनमण्डले ।
> विष्णुपत्नि नमस्तुभ्यं पादस्पर्शं क्षमस्व मे ॥
>
> সমুদ্রবসনে দেবি পর্বতস্তনমণ্ডলে ।
> বিষ্ণুপত্নি নমস্তুভ্যং পাদস্পর্শং ক্ষমস্ব মে ॥
>
> *samudra-vasane devi parvata-stana-maṇḍale,
> viṣṇu-patni namas tubhyaṁ pāda-sparśaṁ kṣamasva me.*

**In simple words:** O Goddess Earth, robed in the oceans, whose slopes are the mountains, consort of Vishnu — I bow to you; forgive me for touching you with my feet.

### Planting the first post

Then comes the moment the ceremony is named for: the consecrated khunti is lowered into the pit and set upright. Senior committee members, often together with the priest, do this by hand — and it is considered an honour to be one of the people steadying the pole as the earth is filled in around it. Conch shells (*shankh*, শঙ্খ) are blown, women give the *ululu* (উলু) — the rolling auspicious sound made with the tongue — and if the committee has hired *dhakis* (ঢাকি), the drummers strike up. The first post of Ma Durga's house is standing.

### Prasad, and increasingly, the theme reveal

The ritual closes the way Bengali rituals do: with *prasad* (প্রসাদ) — sweets and fruit distributed to everyone present. At the large Kolkata pujas, khunti puja day has additionally become the occasion for unveiling the year's pandal *theme* — the artistic concept the committee will spend crores bringing to life — announced with banners, posters and press coverage. The little bamboo pole thus doubles as a launch event. Smaller and household-scale pujas skip the showmanship, but the sweets are non-negotiable.

After the ceremony, the planted khunti is usually left standing and becomes, quite literally, part of the pandal: the bamboo skeleton of the structure is built up around and from it over the following weeks. There is a quiet poetry in this — the blessed first post ends up holding up the goddess's roof.

## Why it matters

On the surface, khunti puja blesses a construction project. Underneath, it carries several layers of meaning.

**Asking the earth's permission.** Like bhoomi pujan across India, the rite honours Bhumi, the earth, before disturbing her. The ground that will host the goddess for five days is treated as sacred from day one, not merely from the day the idol arrives. Digging into the earth to plant the pole is done apologetically and gratefully, with offerings.

**Inviting the goddess early.** Bengalis speak of Durga Puja as the annual homecoming of a daughter: Uma returning from Kailash to her parents' house (see [The Goddess and Her Story](01-the-goddess-and-her-story.md)). Khunti puja is the moment the family starts getting her room ready. The invitation, in a sense, is issued months in advance — the sankalpa formally announces to the goddess that this community is expecting her.

**Sanctifying labour.** Between khunti puja and the festival, dozens of people — bamboo-workers, electricians, decorators, artists — will labour on the site, often at height, often in monsoon rain. The ceremony asks protection for them. It also dignifies their work: pandal construction is not just carpentry, it is *seva*, service to the goddess, and it begins with a puja rather than a purchase order.

**Starting the clock, together.** Perhaps most importantly for a community, khunti puja converts an idea into a commitment. Funds may be half-raised and plans may still be sketchy, but once the pole is in the ground the pujo is *happening*, and everyone knows it. Organisers will tell you the mood of the whole committee changes on khunti puja day. For probasi communities especially, it is the day the countdown becomes real.

There is also a lovely symbolic reading that priests sometimes offer: the single upright khunti is like a spine or an axis — a still centre around which the enormous, chaotic, joyous machinery of Durga Puja will organise itself.

## The story behind it

### The mythological frame

Khunti puja has no single origin myth of its own; rather, it borrows its logic from two old ideas. The first is the pan-Indian principle that all construction begins with worship of the earth and of Ganesha — the same principle behind bhoomi pujan. The second is the Bengali custom tying the start of Durga Puja preparations to Rath Yatra: for generations, the aristocratic bonedi households have performed the first rites over their idol's frame on Rath Yatra day, treating Jagannath's chariot festival as the auspicious gateway to the Durga Puja season. When community pujas needed a ceremonial start for their pandals, the same day and the same instinct — sanctify the frame, sanctify the ground — carried over naturally from idol to pavilion.

### From the zamindar's courtyard to everyone's puja

To understand why there are pandals at all — and therefore why there is a khunti puja — you have to go back to a quarrel in a Hooghly village more than two centuries ago.

Until the late 1700s, Durga Puja in Bengal was overwhelmingly a private affair of wealth: *zamindars* (জমিদার, landlords) and aristocratic families held lavish pujas in the courtyards of their mansions, and ordinary people attended, if at all, as spectators at the gate. The famous story — told with minor variations across many sources — is set in Guptipara (গুপ্তিপাড়া), a village in Hooghly district. A group of young men, refused entry to (or slighted at) the household Durga Puja of a local aristocratic family, decided in their anger to do something unheard of: hold a puja of their own, funded not by one rich patron but by public subscription collected from the surrounding villages. Twelve friends formed the organising committee, and from them the new institution took its name: *barowari* (বারোয়ারি), from *baro* (বারো, twelve) and *yaar* (ইয়ার, friend) — "the twelve friends' puja."

How solid is this story historically? Reasonably solid, with honest caveats. The earliest documented reference is a report in the *Friend of India*, published from Serampore in 1820, describing how, about thirty years earlier, a number of Brahmins at Guptipara had formed an association to celebrate a puja independently, elected twelve men as a committee, and solicited subscriptions from all the surrounding villages. That "about thirty years earlier" is the basis of the commonly cited date of 1790, though some accounts place the beginning as early as 1760–61 — sources genuinely disagree on the year. A further wrinkle worth knowing: several careful tellings note that the deity first worshipped by the Guptipara barowari was not Durga herself but *Bindhyabasini* (বিন্ধ্যবাসিনী), a form of the goddess whom local tradition associates with *Jagaddhatri* (জগদ্ধাত্রী); the Guptipara barowari of Bindhyabasini Mata continues to this day. The retelling in which the twelve friends launched a Durga Puja specifically is the popular version; the documented kernel is that they invented the *format* — a goddess-puja run by a public committee on public money.

And it is the format that changed everything. The barowari idea spread through Bengal — on both sides of today's border — across the 1800s. It reached Kolkata surprisingly late: the first barowari Durga Puja in the city is generally credited to the Bhowanipore Sanatan Dharmotsahini Sabha, held on Balaram Bose Ghat Road in 1909 (some accounts say 1910). Others followed quickly — Shyampukur in 1911, and in 1919 the famous Bagbazar puja, which helped popularise a new word: *sarbojanin* (সর্বজনীন), "of all people." Where "barowari" described the committee, "sarbojanin" described the spirit — a puja belonging to everyone, regardless of caste, class or contribution. During the Swadeshi era and the freedom movement, these community pujas became platforms for nationalist feeling and social mixing, and after Independence they exploded: Kolkata alone now hosts over two and a half thousand of them, sarbojanin pujas thrive in every district town of West Bengal and across Bangladesh, and every Bengali community anywhere in the world that gathers to worship Durga in a rented hall or a neighbourhood ground is running, whether it knows it or not, a direct descendant of the Guptipara twelve.

Here is the connection back to our bamboo pole. A household pujo happens in a permanent *thakur dalan* (ঠাকুর দালান), the family's own worship hall, and needs no construction. A sarbojanin pujo, by definition, has no permanent home — it must build the goddess a house from scratch every single year. The pandal is the signature architecture of the people's puja, and khunti puja is the people's ground-breaking ceremony. In that sense this small ritual is a monument to a big idea: the goddess belongs to everyone, and so everyone, together, builds her house.

### The modern kickoff

As Kolkata's pujas grew into the gigantic artistic productions recognised by UNESCO (which inscribed Durga Puja in Kolkata on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2021), khunti puja grew with them. What was once a quiet rite has become the season's opening ceremony — covered by newspapers, attended by film stars and ministers at the marquee pujas, and used to announce themes and unveil logos. Purists occasionally grumble about the showbiz, but the core of the day survives intact underneath: a priest, a sankalpa, a garlanded bamboo pole, and a community promising itself a puja.

## In a probasi pujo

For *probasi* (প্রবাসী, expatriate) Bengali communities — in Pune, Bangalore, Delhi, London or New Jersey — khunti puja translates beautifully, precisely because its real subject is not bamboo but commitment.

Many probasi pujas happen in rented venues: community halls, school grounds, housing-society lawns. Often there is no three-month pandal build; sometimes there is no pandal at all in the Kolkata sense, just a decorated stage erected days before the festival. So the khunti puja adapts. Some communities perform the full traditional rite at their venue or society grounds on Rath Yatra or another auspicious day, planting a real bamboo post — even a symbolic one that will not structurally hold anything — with a priest, a ghot, gangajal and sweets. Others fold the spirit of it into a season-opening gathering: the first committee meeting after the date is fixed, a small puja, the announcement of the year's plans and budget, the formal "yes, we are doing this again."

Experienced organisers will tell you the khunti puja is one of the most valuable days of a probasi pujo's calendar, for reasons that have little to do with ritual technicality. It is the day new volunteers are welcomed and the old guard re-commits; the day sub-committees — food, cultural, decoration, puja — get their first push; the day children see that a festival is not something you attend but something you *make*. In a community where members come from different districts of Bengal (and increasingly, where non-Bengali friends and neighbours join in), the khunti puja is a low-stakes, high-warmth occasion to gather everyone before the pressure of the festival itself. The goddess's house — whether it is a bamboo cathedral in Kolkata or a decorated stage in a Pune community hall — gets built the same way it has since 1790: by a committee of friends who decided their puja belonged to everyone.

And when the first post goes up, or the first banner is tied, someone will inevitably say the words every Bengali feels in their bones at that moment: *pujo eshe gelo* — Pujo is almost here.

## Words to know

- *khunti* (খুঁটি) — a stake, peg or post; here, the first bamboo pole of the pandal.
- *pandal* (প্যান্ডেল) — the temporary pavilion built to house the goddess during the festival; called a *mandap* (মণ্ডপ) in its role as sacred space.
- *Rath Yatra* (রথযাত্রা) — the chariot festival of Jagannath (June–July); traditional start of Durga Puja preparations. *Ulto Rath* (উল্টোরথ) is the return journey about a week later.
- *sankalpa* (সংকল্প) — the formal vow or declaration of intent that opens a puja.
- *purohit* (পুরোহিত) — priest.
- *ghot* (ঘট) — the pitcher, topped with mango leaves and coconut, that embodies divine presence in Bengali ritual.
- *gangajal* (গঙ্গাজল) — water of the Ganga, used for purification.
- *sindoor* (সিঁদুর) — vermilion, the auspicious red powder.
- *alpona* (আলপনা) — auspicious floor art drawn with rice paste.
- *gamchha* (গামছা) — the traditional chequered cotton towel, often tied around the khunti.
- *dhak* (ঢাক) — the large barrel drum of Bengali festivals; its player is a *dhaki* (ঢাকি).
- *shankh* (শঙ্খ) — conch shell, blown at auspicious moments; *ululu* (উলু) is the rolling tongue-trill sound women make alongside it.
- *prasad* (প্রসাদ) — blessed food distributed after worship.
- *panjika* (পঞ্জিকা) — the Bengali almanac used to find auspicious dates and times.
- *zamindar* (জমিদার) — landlord; the aristocratic patrons of old household pujas.
- *bonedi bari* (বনেদি বাড়ি) — the old aristocratic households of Bengal, many of which still run centuries-old family pujas.
- *thakur dalan* (ঠাকুর দালান) — the permanent worship hall of a traditional Bengali mansion.
- *barowari* (বারোয়ারি) — "of twelve friends"; a community puja run by committee on public subscription, first organised at Guptipara.
- *sarbojanin* (সর্বজনীন) — "of all people"; the later, more inclusive name for community pujas.
- *probasi* (প্রবাসী) — expatriate; a Bengali living outside Bengal.
- *kathamo* (কাঠামো) — the bamboo-and-wood frame of the idol; its worship, *kathamo puja*, is covered in [The Making of the Murti](03-the-making-of-the-murti.md).
