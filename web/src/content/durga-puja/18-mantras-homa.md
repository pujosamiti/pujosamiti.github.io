## When the worship changes medium

On Mahanabami, after the day's puja and anjali (see [Nabami](10-nabami.md)), the worship changes medium. Everything so far has been offered to the goddess directly — flowers at her feet, food before her image. The *homa* (হোম), also called *yajna* (যজ্ঞ), offers through fire instead. A fire is kindled in a temporary altar-pit or platform, the *homa kunda*, usually built beside the mandap with bricks or banked sand, fed with *ghee* (ঘি) and wood — mango wood is the Bengali standard — and each offering is placed into the flames with a mantra ending in the word *svāhā*. Agni, the fire god of the oldest Vedic ritual, acts as the mouth of the gods: what the fire consumes, he carries to the deity. It is the most ancient layer of Hindu ritual embedded inside the pujo, and the smoke, the crackle and the rhythmic *svāhā… svāhā…* make it one of Nabami's most watchable hours.

One honest caveat before the texts. A homa is a priest's ritual through and through: its full sequence — preparing the ground, laying the fire, the long chain of preliminary oblations, the main offerings, the conclusion — is directed by the purohit's *paddhati* (পদ্ধতি), the manual of his tradition, and Bengali manuals such as the *Purohit Darpan* (পুরোহিত দর্পণ) differ in detail from South Indian or North Indian homa handbooks, and from each other. The complete Bengali Durga-homa liturgy is not verifiably published, so this chapter presents the framework and the core mantras that ARE well attested across sources — the Mahavyahritis, the Gayatri, the Durga and Navarna svaha formulas, and the purnahuti — and marks clearly where practice varies.

## Agni-sthapana — establishing the fire

The homa begins with *agni-sthapana* (অগ্নিস্থাপন), "establishing the fire": the kunda is purified, lines are drawn in it, kusha grass is laid, and fire — traditionally carried from a lamp already burning before the deity — is placed into the kunda while Agni is invoked by name and asked to be present, face the worshippers, and carry the offerings. Camphor and ghee-soaked wicks help the wood catch. In the concise printed vidhi we could verify, the kindling is accompanied by the Vyahritis themselves joined into a single utterance:

> ॐ भूर्भुवस्सुवरोम्।
>
> ওঁ ভূর্ভুবস্সুবরোম্।
>
> *oṁ bhūr bhuvas suvar om*

**In simple words:** Oṁ — earth, atmosphere, heaven — oṁ. The three worlds are named between two utterances of *oṁ* as the flame comes alive: before anything is offered into it, the fire is declared a meeting point of every level of the cosmos.

Notes:

- Fuller paddhatis precede this with rituals our sources describe but do not quote in full: consecrating the kunda, installing Agni with a dhyana of his own, and feeding the first sticks of wood (*samidh*). The invocation asks Agni to sit facing the worshipper as the *havya-vahana*, "carrier of offerings."
- The purohit performs all of this; the *tantradharak* (তন্ত্রধারক) prompts from the manual. The *yajman* (যজমান) — the sponsor of the rite, in a samiti pujo usually a designated member or couple — sits beside the priest.

## The Mahavyahriti mantras

Among the standard opening oblations, ghee is offered with the *Mahavyahritis* (মহাব্যাহৃতি) — the "great utterances" *bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, svaḥ*, the primal Vedic names of earth, atmosphere and heaven. They are offered separately (*vyasta*) and then together (*samasta*), a spoonful of ghee into the fire at each *svāhā*:

> ॐ भूः स्वाहा।
> ॐ भुवः स्वाहा।
> ॐ स्वः स्वाहा।
> ॐ भूर्भुवः स्वः स्वाहा॥
>
> ওঁ ভূঃ স্বাহা।
> ওঁ ভুবঃ স্বাহা।
> ওঁ স্বঃ স্বাহা।
> ওঁ ভূর্ভুবঃ স্বঃ স্বাহা॥
>
> *oṁ bhūḥ svāhā ·
> oṁ bhuvaḥ svāhā ·
> oṁ svaḥ svāhā ·
> oṁ bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ svāhā*

**In simple words:** To the earth — svaha! To the mid-air — svaha! To the heavens — svaha! To all three together — svaha! *Svāhā* is the untranslatable word of release, the exclamation with which every fire-offering is handed over — itself personified as Agni's wife, the one who delivers the oblation. The three worlds are fed one by one and then as a whole: before any particular deity is addressed, the whole cosmos is honoured through the fire.

Notes:

- These are among the oldest mantras still in daily Hindu use. Vedic texts prescribe vyahriti oblations precisely as the all-purpose repair and foundation of a sacrifice — offered to make good any defect in the rite. That is exactly their role in the Durga homa: a universal underwriting of everything that follows.
- Pronunciation varies regionally — *svaḥ* appears as *suvaḥ* in southern recitation (as in the kindling mantra above); some traditions extend the list with the further vyahritis *mahaḥ, janaḥ, tapaḥ, satyam*.

## The Gayatri in the homa

Sources on homa practice show the Gayatri mantra — the most revered verse of the Rig Veda (3.62.10), normally a silent prayer — used aloud as an oblation mantra, repeated for a series of ghee offerings with *svāhā* released into the fire at the end:

> ॐ भूर्भुवः स्वः तत्सवितुर्वरेण्यं भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि।
> धियो यो नः प्रचोदयात् (स्वाहा)॥
>
> ওঁ ভূর্ভুবঃ স্বঃ তৎসবিতুর্বরেণ্যং ভর্গো দেবস্য ধীমহি।
> ধিয়ো যো নঃ প্রচোদয়াৎ (স্বাহা)॥
>
> *oṁ bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ tat savitur vareṇyaṁ bhargo devasya dhīmahi ·
> dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt (svāhā)*

**In simple words:** Oṁ — earth, mid-air, heaven. Let us hold in our minds the supreme radiance of the divine Savitri, the Sun as inspirer; may he set our thoughts in motion. In homa use, *svāhā* is appended, and the light that is prayed to is fed with light: ghee poured into flame.

The goddess has her own form of the same sacred metre, the **Durga Gayatri**, heard in meditation at the fire in many Devi homas:

> ॐ महादेव्यै च विद्महे दुर्गायै धीमहि।
> तन्नो देवी प्रचोदयात्॥
>
> ওঁ মহাদেব্যৈ চ বিদ্মহে দুর্গায়ৈ ধীমহি।
> তন্নো দেবী প্রচোদয়াৎ॥
>
> *oṁ mahādevyai ca vidmahe durgāyai dhīmahi ·
> tan no devī pracodayāt*

**In simple words:** May we know the great Devi; on Durga we meditate; may that goddess set our minds in motion. Like every Gayatri it is a prayer for illumination rather than for things — the fire's first business is the worshipper's own mind.

Notes:

- Whether the Gayatri figures in a given Durga homa depends on the paddhati; the verifiable sources for its svaha-form are general homa manuals rather than specifically Bengali Durga-homa texts. Many goddess-focused homas move straight from the vyahritis to the Devi mantras below. We include it because it is standard in the broader homa tradition and commonly heard.

## The Durga ahuti — the svaha formulas of the main offering

Now the homa reaches its addressee. Each *ahuti* (আহুতি) — each ladle of ghee, each handful of the offering mixture — goes into the fire with the goddess's own mantra, converted into an oblation by the final *svāhā*. Verified printed vidhis give bija-mantra forms built on *duṁ*, the seed-syllable of Durga:

> ॐ दुं दुर्गायै नमः स्वाहा।
> ॐ श्रीं ह्रीं दुं दुर्गायै नमः स्वाहा॥
>
> ওঁ দুং দুর্গায়ৈ নমঃ স্বাহা।
> ওঁ শ্রীং হ্রীং দুং দুর্গায়ৈ নমঃ স্বাহা॥
>
> *oṁ duṁ durgāyai namaḥ svāhā ·
> oṁ śrīṁ hrīṁ duṁ durgāyai namaḥ svāhā*

The simplest attested form strips even the bijas away:

> ॐ दुर्गायै स्वाहा।
>
> ওঁ দুর্গায়ৈ স্বাহা।
>
> *oṁ durgāyai svāhā*

And in the Chandi tradition, the same conversion is applied to the nine-syllable *Navarna* (নবার্ণ) mantra — the root mantra of the whole Durga Saptashati (see [Sandhi Puja Mantras](17-mantras-sandhi-puja.md)):

> ॐ ऐं ह्रीं क्लीं चामुण्डायै विच्चे स्वाहा।
>
> ওঁ ঐং হ্রীং ক্লীং চামুণ্ডায়ৈ বিচ্চে স্বাহা।
>
> *oṁ aiṁ hrīṁ klīṁ cāmuṇḍāyai vicce svāhā*

**In simple words:** "Oṁ, duṁ — honour to Durga — svaha!" and, with the Navarna, "Oṁ, aiṁ hrīṁ klīṁ — to Chamunda, vicce — svaha!" The grammar of every homa formula is the same — the deity's mantra plus *svāhā*: "may Agni carry this to her." What the flame takes with *svāhā* is understood to reach the deity directly; each repetition is something being let go of.

Notes:

- All these patterns are genuinely attested and all are in use; which one a given pujo hears depends entirely on the purohit's paddhati. Counts of the main ahutis vary too — 28, 108, or more.
- In one common Bengali paddhati the *Jayanti mangala kali* pranam verse is itself turned into the oblation mantra, recited with *svāhā* in place of *namo'stu te* — the litany of her names fed to the fire, repetition by repetition, while the dhak keeps a low pulse and the pandal smells of ghee and mango-wood smoke.
- The grandest form of Devi homa, the *Chandi homa* (চণ্ডী হোম), goes further: the entire Devi Mahatmya is recited at the fire with *svāhā* added verse by verse — up to 700 oblations, one per shloka, over several hours. Full Chandi homas are more a temple and South Indian undertaking; a Bengali barowari Nabami homa is far shorter, but it belongs to the same family.

## The 108 bel leaves

The signature of the Bengali Nabami homa is the offering of 108 *bel* (বেল) leaves — the trefoil leaves of the wood-apple tree, sacred to Shiva and beloved of the goddess. Bengali accounts of Mahanabami consistently describe the homa as performed with 108 bel leaves, mango wood and ghee: each leaf, dipped in ghee, goes into the fire one by one, an ahuti mantra with each, until the count of 108 is complete.

Our sources document the practice — the count, the leaves, the ghee — rather than a distinct fixed mantra unique to the leaves. The oblations follow the ahuti formulas above (the Durga bija forms or the Navarna with *svāhā*), one repetition per leaf, in accordance with the purohit's paddhati.

Notes:

- 108 is the pujo's recurring sacred count — 108 lamps and 108 lotuses at sandhi puja (see [Sandhi Puja Mantras](17-mantras-sandhi-puja.md)), 108 names of the goddess — and here it structures the homa's central offering. A *japa mala* has 108 beads for the same reason: the number signifies a complete cycle.
- Some pujas keep a parallel custom of a garland of 108 bel leaves for the goddess; leaves for either purpose are ideally unbroken, three-lobed and whole — the offering must be as flawless as one can find.

## Purnahuti — the full and final oblation

The homa cannot simply stop; it must be completed. The *purnahuti* (পূর্ণাহুতি), "the full offering," is the last and greatest oblation: classically a whole dried coconut, wrapped in a red cloth and soaked in ghee, placed into the fire — often with betel, fruit, flowers or a bundled cloth of auspicious things, depending on tradition. Everyone present stands. The verified Durga-homa vidhi gives the purnahuti with the goddess's own formula:

> ॐ ह्रीं दुं दुर्गायै नमः स्वाहा।
>
> ওঁ হ্রীং দুং দুর্গায়ৈ নমঃ স্বাহা।
>
> *oṁ hrīṁ duṁ durgāyai namaḥ svāhā*

**In simple words:** Oṁ, hrīṁ, duṁ — honour to Durga — svaha! The last and largest offering, released with her own seed-syllables.

Many traditions join to the moment of completion the Upanishadic verse of fullness — the shanti mantra of the Isha Upanishad — whose subject, fullness that loses nothing by giving fully, is the purnahuti's exact meaning:

> ॐ पूर्णमदः पूर्णमिदं पूर्णात्पूर्णमुदच्यते।
> पूर्णस्य पूर्णमादाय पूर्णमेवावशिष्यते॥
> ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः॥
>
> ওঁ পূর্ণমদঃ পূর্ণমিদং পূর্ণাৎ পূর্ণমুদচ্যতে।
> পূর্ণস্য পূর্ণমাদায় পূর্ণমেবাবশিষ্যতে॥
> ওঁ শান্তিঃ শান্তিঃ শান্তিঃ॥
>
> *oṁ pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idaṁ pūrṇāt pūrṇam udacyate ·
> pūrṇasya pūrṇam ādāya pūrṇam evāvaśiṣyate ·
> oṁ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ*

**In simple words:** That is full; this is full; from fullness, fullness comes forth. Take fullness from fullness, and fullness alone remains. Oṁ — peace, peace, peace. The whole coconut is the worshipper's whole self, given entire, and the verse answers that a complete gift diminishes nothing. It is the Upanishad's most famous paradox placed at the moment of giving everything away — the pujo's economics stated as metaphysics, and the reason the purnahuti feels like an exhale.

Notes:

- Be clear about what is fixed and what is not: the act — a final, complete, standing oblation — is universal; the words vary. The vidhi we verified uses the Durga svaha formula above; the *pūrṇam adaḥ* verse is widely associated with completion and recited at the purnahuti in many traditions, but not every paddhati employs it there. Your purohit's manual decides.
- The unburnt remainder of the fire matters afterwards: its ash is taken as a blessing, touched to the forehead, and in household practice carried home.

## What the yajman and everyone else does

A homa looks like a spectator ritual, but it has defined roles for the non-priests, and this is where the audience joins in:

- **During the ahutis**, the yajman sits at the purohit's right, often holding kusha grass or touching the priest's arm or the ladle as offerings are poured, so that the oblations are formally made through him or her on behalf of everyone. In many pujas each repetition of *svāhā* is echoed by the assembly.
- **Holding a flower**: as at anjali, those present may be given a flower or bel leaf to hold during the closing oblations, offered into the fire or at the goddess's feet at the end, so that every person's offering rides with the purnahuti.
- **The standing purnahuti**: when the final oblation is announced, everyone stands. It is the homa's equivalent of the arati's climax — the one moment the whole gathering performs together.
- **Pradakshina and after**: where space allows, the fire or the deity is circumambulated — *pradakshina* (প্রদক্ষিণা), keeping the sacred on one's right — and the rite closes with *shanti* recitations and the sprinkling of *shanti jal* (শান্তি জল), the water of peace, over the assembly. The yajman receives the ash and the priest's blessing.

## After the fire

The flames are allowed to settle; the ash — now blessed — marks foreheads. The purohit receives **dakshina**, without which no rite is complete: tradition regards the honorarium as the yajna's final oblation. With that, the worship of the three great days stands, in the liturgy's accounting, delivered. What remains — Dashami's mirror, the sindoor, the river — is no longer asking; it is farewell.

For the watching crowd, the homa needs no translation — fire speaks for itself — but two of its words reward knowing. Listen for *svāhā*, returning like a bell-stroke a hundred and eight times: each one is something being handed over. And listen, at the end, for *pūrṇam* — "full" — sounding again and again through three lines: the tradition's last word, every year, on whether giving leaves you poorer.

And a final honest note, the same one this chapter began with: the sequence above is the well-attested skeleton. The flesh — which preliminary deities receive oblations, how many ahutis, which formula at the purnahuti, whether the Gayatri sounds at all — follows the purohit's paddhati, and two pujas in the same city can differ at every one of those points without either being wrong. When our own purohit's sequence differs from this chapter, his manual wins; this chapter exists so you know what you are hearing when the ghee hits the fire.
