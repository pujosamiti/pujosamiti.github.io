## What it is

Every Durga who presides over a pujo — in a Kolkata pandal, a London community hall or a Pune housing society — began as a bundle of bamboo, straw and river clay in somebody's workshop. This chapter is about that transformation: the craft of making the *protima* (প্রতিমা) — the worship image, also called a *murti* (মূর্তি) — and about the most famous place on earth where it happens.

*Kumortuli* (কুমোরটুলি, often anglicised as Kumartuli) is a dense warren of lanes beside the Hooghly river in north Kolkata. The name means "the potters' quarter": *kumor* (কুমোর) is the Bengali word for potter, and the *-tuli* suffix marked the neighbourhoods of colonial Calcutta where particular trades clustered. There were once many such quarters — for oil-pressers, painters, milkmen — but Kumortuli is the one that survives, and it survives spectacularly. Several hundred workshops line its alleys, and in the months before the festival they produce thousands of Durga images, from three feet tall to thirty, which travel to every district of the city and, in crates and containers, to Bengali communities on every continent.

Kumortuli is the capital of the craft, but not the whole country. Nearly every district town of West Bengal and Bangladesh has its own *para* of image-makers: Krishnanagar's Ghurni quarter is celebrated for the fineness of its modelling, and Dhaka's Shankhari Bazar keeps the eastern lineages alive. And Kumortuli is more than a factory district. Idol-making in Bengal is a hereditary sacred craft, passed down in families for generations — parent to child, master to apprentice — and it is threaded through with rituals of its own: the frame is worshipped before clay ever touches it, the clay itself is compounded according to old prescriptions, and the goddess's eyes are painted last, in a rite spoken of as the moment she comes alive. The artisan who does all this is called a *mritshilpi* (মৃৎশিল্পী) — literally, an artist in clay. Understanding what happens in those lanes between Rath Yatra and Mahalaya is understanding half of what Durga Puja is.

## When it happens

Idol-making has a calendar of its own, loosely synchronised with the ritual calendar of the pujo.

The season traditionally opens around *Rath Yatra* (রথযাত্রা), the chariot festival in June–July, roughly three months before the puja — the same auspicious gateway on which committees plant their first pandal post (see [Khunti Puja](02-khunti-puja.md)). On or around this day, many workshops and traditional households perform the first rites over the idol's wooden base and frame: some observe *pat puja* (পাটপূজা), the worship of the wooden base plank, on Rath Yatra itself, while others hold the *kathamo puja* (কাঠামো পূজা), the worship of the frame, on the day after Janmashtami in August, and some houses on Akshaya Tritiya. Practice varies by family and by workshop; what is common is the principle that construction begins with worship, on an auspicious day.

Through the monsoon months the frames are built, straw is bound, and clay goes on in stages — a slow business, because each layer must dry, and Kolkata's humid monsoon makes drying agonisingly slow. Painting and dressing happen through Bhadra and early Ashwin (September). The final act, the painting of the eyes — *chokkhu daan* (চক্ষুদান) — is by tradition performed on *Mahalaya* (মহালয়া), the day that opens *Debi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ), the goddess's fortnight (see [Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md) for that day's own rich story). In practice, with thousands of idols and hard delivery deadlines, many workshops now begin eye-painting well before Mahalaya — artisans are candid about this — but the day retains its ceremonial primacy, and Mahalaya morning in Kumortuli, with senior artisans moving from idol to idol giving each goddess her gaze, remains one of Kolkata's great sights.

Then the lanes fill with lorries. Between Mahalaya and Panchami, the goddesses leave home.

## The rituals, step by step

What follows traces the making of a traditional clay protima from bare frame to finished goddess, with the rituals embedded at each stage. The sequence described is the broad Kumortuli standard; individual workshops and family pujas have their own variations.

### Kathamo puja — worshipping the frame

Before anything else, there is the *kathamo* (কাঠামো): the skeleton of the idol, built from bamboo lengths lashed to a wooden base, one armature for each figure in the tableau — Durga and her lion, the demon Mahishasura, and the four children: Lakshmi with her owl, Saraswati with her swan, Ganesha with his mouse, Kartik with his peacock.

The kathamo is not treated as lumber. Before construction begins in earnest, it receives its own worship — *kathamo puja* — in which the frame is sanctified with flowers, sindoor and mantras, and the goddess is in effect invited into the structure that will bear her form. In many traditional households this happens on Rath Yatra or the day after Janmashtami (Akshaya Tritiya in some houses); several centuries-old family pujas time their entire calendar from this rite.

The most touching part of the tradition is reuse. In many old household pujas, after the idol is immersed at the end of the festival, the clay dissolves but the wooden-and-bamboo frame is retrieved from the water, dried, carried home and stored for the year — then worshipped again and rebuilt upon for the next pujo. The same kathamo may serve a family for generations, so that the "new" goddess literally stands each year on the bones of every goddess the family has ever worshipped. The symbolism is beautiful and explicit: the goddess departs, but something of her stays; her form is renewed each year on an unbroken foundation. (Most modern community pujas, by contrast, commission entirely new idols each year, and the frame goes into the river with the clay.)

### Gathering the soils — Ganga maati and punya maati

The flesh of the goddess is river clay. Workshops use two broad types, both associated with the Ganga: *entel maati* (এঁটেল মাটি), a sticky, plastic clay, and *bele maati* (বেলে মাটি), a sandier, finer-grained clay used for smooth finishing. Kumortuli sits on the Hooghly precisely because the river delivered both the clay and the transport for it; today much of the supply comes by boat from riverside sites such as Uluberia, upstream and downstream of the city, prized for the right texture. This river-borne earth is spoken of collectively and reverently as *Ganga maati* (গঙ্গামাটি) — Ganga's soil, taken with the river's blessing asked — and its use ties every idol physically to the sacred river into which it will eventually return.

Then there is the tradition every visitor to Kumortuli eventually hears about — the ingredient nobody expects — and it deserves an honest telling. Custom holds that the clay for Durga's image should include a small quantity of *punya maati* (পুণ্য মাটি) — "blessed soil" — collected from the doorstep of a courtesan's house, from the *nishiddho palli* (নিষিদ্ধ পল্লি), the "forbidden quarter," which in Kolkata means the red-light district of Sonagachi. In the traditional telling, the priest or artisan must go to the sex worker's door and *beg* for the soil — it cannot simply be taken — and it is given willingly, as a gift.

Why? Here the sources genuinely diverge, and it is worth laying the interpretations side by side rather than picking one:

- The most widely repeated folk explanation says that a man who crosses that threshold leaves his virtue at the door; the soil there is therefore heavy with accumulated *punya* (merit) — a poetic, if double-edged, idea.
- A second reading emphasises inclusion: the goddess's body must contain earth from the homes of those society shuns, because Durga Puja belongs to all women and all people, including the most stigmatised — because Shakti dwells in all women without exception. On this reading the ritual is a yearly, physical statement that no one stands outside the goddess's embrace.
- A third, historical reading recalls that before the colonial era hardened attitudes, courtesans in Bengal were bearers of music and dance, and the custom honoured their place in the culture.
- Scholars of ritual add a further layer: traditional prescriptions for Durga Puja's great bath (the *mahasnan*) call for water and soils from many symbolic sources — riverbanks, crossroads, earth turned by an elephant's tusk, the king's gateway, and the courtesan's door among them — as a way of gathering the whole world into the rite. The point is the same in every case: the Mother is made from everywhere, and no ground is beneath her. Some Tantric commentators have even argued that *veshya* in the old texts is a technical term of Tantra rather than a literal reference to prostitutes. None of these theories is settled; honest writers call the custom one of the festival's unresolved enigmas.

The inclusive reading has scripture on its side, in the verse from the Chandi that every pushpanjali crowd knows by heart:

> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु मातृरूपेण संस्थिता ।
> नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमो नमः ॥
>
> যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু মাতৃরূপেণ সংস্থিতা ।
> নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমো নমঃ ॥
>
> *yā devī sarva-bhūteṣu mātṛ-rūpeṇa saṁsthitā,
> namas tasyai namas tasyai namas tasyai namo namaḥ.*

**In simple words:** To the Goddess who dwells in all beings in the form of the Mother — we bow to her, we bow to her, we bow to her, again and again.

How literally is the custom practised today? Unevenly. Journalists who ask in Kumortuli find that some workshops and priests still ceremonially collect soil from Sonagachi each year, while for many others the punya maati is nominal or skipped altogether. In recent years the custom has also drawn criticism from sex workers' collectives, who point out the bitter irony of a society that wants their soil for its goddess but not their presence at its festivals — some have organised Durga Pujas of their own. A children's takeaway that stays truthful: tradition says the goddess's clay should include earth from the doorsteps of women society excludes, people disagree about exactly why, and the disagreement itself is part of what the tradition makes us think about.

### Khar and bichali — the straw body

With the frame blessed and the clay gathered, the figure is roughed out in straw. Bundles of *khar* (খড়) — rice straw, also called *bichali* (বিচালি) — are bound tightly to the bamboo armature with jute twine, twisted and shaped into torsos, limbs and heads, the lion, the buffalo demon. This is astonishingly skilled work: the straw bundle determines every proportion the clay will follow, and a good straw-binder produces a figure with correct gesture before a fingerful of clay is applied — masters can famously "see" the finished goddess in the straw. The straw core keeps the idol relatively light, gives the clay something to grip, and — come immersion — is what lets the whole form break down harmlessly in water.

### Ek mete — the first clay

Now the clay goes on, in the stage called *ek mete* (এক মেটে), "first coat." Coarse, sticky entel maati, kneaded — often by foot — with rice husk and chopped straw for binding strength, is slapped and pressed over the straw body and worked into the form of muscles and features. Then the figure must dry, propped in whatever sun the monsoon allows. As it dries the clay cracks; the cracks are filled, strips of old cloth or muslin are laid over joints and weak points with slip, and more clay is applied. The cycle of coating, drying and mending repeats — weeks of monsoon-defying patience — until the surface is sound.

### Do mete — the second clay and the finish

The finishing stage is *do mete* (দো মেটে), "second coat": a layer of fine, sieved bele maati slip that dries into the figure's smooth, luminous skin. Detailed parts — the face above all, but also fingers, ornaments-in-clay and the lion's mane — are usually cast separately in moulds or modelled by the most senior hands, then joined to the body with soft clay. The face of Durga is the workshop's signature: regular customers can recognise a particular Pal family's goddess across a crowded pandal by her face alone.

After final drying comes priming — traditionally a wash of white made with tamarind-seed glue and chalk — and then colour. The goddess's skin is most often painted a brilliant yellow-gold — Bengalis reach for comparisons like the *shiuli* flower, turmeric, the *atasi* (flax) blossom or ripe wheat — though studios now range from deep gold to the luminous pinks of the Krishnanagar style. Mahishasura is green in many workshops' convention; the lion white or gold. Spray guns have joined brushes in modern workshops, but the last details are always brushwork.

### Saaj — dressing the goddess: daaker saaj and sholar saaj

Dressed only in paint, the goddess is not yet the resplendent figure of the pandal. The dressing — *saaj* (সাজ) — is a craft, and often a separate hereditary trade, of its own: hair of jute, sarees and dhotis for the figures, beaten-metal and *zari* ornaments, and the weapons — trident, sword, discus, bow, conch and the rest — fitted into the ten hands. Two classic ornament styles matter to know:

*Sholar saaj* (শোলার সাজ) is ornament carved from *shola* (শোলা) — Indian cork, the snow-white pith of a marsh reed that grows in Bengal's wetlands. Shola carvers (*malakar*s, মালাকার) cut the feather-light pith into filigree crowns, breastplates and backdrops of astonishing delicacy. An idol dressed entirely in white shola has a serene, moonlit beauty, and several famous old pujas insist on it.

*Daaker saaj* (ডাকের সাজ) is the glittering alternative: ornament built up from silver and metallic foil — *rangta* (রাংতা) — with sequins, beaten-metal sheets and wire. The name has a story that delights everyone who hears it: *daak* (ডাক) means "post" in Bengali, and the accepted explanation is that in the nineteenth century the German silver foil used for this work could not be sourced in India and was imported — arriving by parcel post, famously from Germany. Ornament that came by daak became daaker saaj: "the decoration that came in the mail." Under the oil lamps of old Calcutta, and later under electric light, the foil-clad goddess shimmered; wealthy household pujas competed in its extravagance, and the white-and-silver style remains the default image of a traditional Durga. (Today's foils and zari are made in India, but the name has stuck for a century and a half.)

Modern theme pujas add a third, anything-goes category — goddesses dressed in terracotta, jute, brass, even recycled materials — but sholar and daaker saaj remain the classical vocabulary.

### Chokkhu daan — the gift of eyes

Last of all, the eyes. Through every earlier stage the goddess's eye-sockets stay blank or veiled; painting the eyes is reserved for the end, and it is a rite as much as a brushstroke — the final act of the making and the first act of the goddess's presence. *Chokkhu daan* (চক্ষুদান) — literally "the gift of eyes" — is by custom performed by the seniormost artisan of the workshop, and only by him, in many families after a fast or purifying bath, traditionally on Mahalaya. In a few unhesitating strokes of the fine brush he draws the three eyes — the two sweeping, elongated, fish-shaped eyes that make a Bengali Durga unmistakable, and the flame-shaped third eye on the forehead — and with them, in the craft's own understanding, the image stops being clay and becomes the goddess. Artisans describe holding their breath for it; a trembling hand at this moment can mar a month's work. Until the eyes are painted, the studios say, it is an image; after, you lower your voice around her. It is the mritshilpi's supreme act, the moment the maker gives his creation sight — and then, having given her eyes, he gives her away. The murti is ready to travel — by lorry, by boat, by air-freight crate — to whichever pandal, mansion or probasi hall has been building her a house since khunti puja. (For why Mahalaya is the fitting day for the goddess to open her eyes, see [Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md).)

## Why it matters

The making of the murti compresses the deepest ideas of Durga Puja into physical form.

**The goddess is made, welcomed, and let go.** Unlike a permanent temple deity, the clay Durga is created new (or renewed) each year, worshipped for five days, and returned to the river. The entire arc of the festival — welcome and farewell, form and formlessness — is built into the idol's own life cycle. The clay comes from the Ganga and goes back to the Ganga; the theology is in the material.

**Divinity out of humble things.** Bamboo, rice straw, river mud, reed pith, foil: nothing in a traditional protima is precious in itself. The tradition insists that the highest beauty is made from the commonest materials by human skill and devotion — and, through punya maati, that the goddess's very body must include earth from society's margins.

**Craft as worship.** For the Pal families, making the goddess is not a job adjacent to religion; it is religious practice, hedged with fasts, auspicious days and inherited discipline. The frame is worshipped before it is built upon; the eyes are painted as a rite. When UNESCO inscribed Kolkata's Durga Puja on its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2021, the citation's heart was precisely this fusion of artisanship and devotion.

**A living economy of tradition.** Idol-making supports an ecosystem — clay boatmen, straw binders, shola carvers, ornament makers, painters, transporters — and its pressures (seasonal income, cramped workshops, rising material costs, sons and daughters weighing other careers) are part of the festival's real story too. Notably, Kumortuli now has celebrated women mritshilpis, such as China Pal and Mala Pal, running some of its best-known studios in what was long an exclusively male hereditary line.

## The story behind it

### How the potters came to the river

Kumortuli's story begins outside Kolkata, in the Nadia district town of *Krishnanagar* (কৃষ্ণনগর) — and before that, by many family accounts, in eastern Bengal (Dhaka, Bikrampur, Faridpur), from where potter families migrated westward generations earlier. Under the celebrated eighteenth-century ruler Maharaja Krishnachandra Ray of Nadia, a great patron of the arts who did much to shape Durga Puja's modern form, clay artists flourished in and around Krishnanagar — the suburb of *Ghurni* (ঘূর্ণি) remains a famous centre of clay modelling to this day.

Calcutta's pull began in the mid-1700s. As the East India Company's city boomed after the Battle of Plassey (1757), its new Bengali elite began holding lavish household Durga Pujas — the most storied being that of Raja Nabakrishna Deb of Shobhabazar, whose grand 1757 puja, held in celebration of the Plassey victory and attended by Robert Clive himself, is a landmark (and a morally complicated one) in the festival's history. These *bonedi* households needed idols, and they brought kumors from Krishnanagar to make them. The artisans settled where the work and the raw material met: on the river bank, in the quarter the colonial city assigned to their caste and trade. Deeds and records suggest the quarter took shape through the eighteenth century; the Pal surname (পাল) — shared by the great majority of Kumortuli's artisan families, with Rudra Pal lineages tracing to East Bengal — became, and remains, almost a synonym for the craft.

For its first century and a half, the trade was seasonal and aristocratic: a limited number of household pujas, each with its hereditary idol-maker. What transformed Kumortuli into a mass workshop was the democratisation of the festival itself — the rise of the *barowari* (বারোয়ারি) and *sarbojanin* (সর্বজনীন) community pujas whose story is told in [Khunti Puja](02-khunti-puja.md). When Kolkata's first community Durga Puja was held at Balaram Bose Ghat Road in 1909–10 and hundreds more followed, demand for idols exploded, drawing fresh waves of artisans from Nadia and, after Partition, from East Bengal. Today Kumortuli's workshops number in the hundreds and its goddesses ship worldwide.

### One frame or many: ek-chala and after

The classical Bengali Durga is an *ek-chala* (একচালা) image: Durga and all four children arranged against a single unified backdrop — one arched frame, one family, one composition. The bonedi household pujas overwhelmingly keep this form, partly from ritual conservatism and partly for a practical reason: a single compact unit can be lifted onto shoulders and carried to the river for immersion.

The break with ek-chala has a name and (roughly) a date. Gopeshwar Pal — G. Paul, the celebrated Kumortuli sculptor who had trained in Western academic techniques and stunned onlookers with his speed-modelling at the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in the 1920s — is credited with first splitting the tableau into separate figures, each on its own base, in the late 1930s. A popular Kumortuli story attaches the innovation to an emergency: when fire destroyed the Kumortuli Sarbojanin committee's idol around 1937–38, Gopeshwar is said to have produced replacement images at impossible speed, abandoning the single frame in the process. Whatever the exact trigger — accounts vary, and the fire story is best labelled local legend — the separated, naturalistic style he pioneered was a sensation, and it opened the door to everything the modern theme puja does with the image: sculptural realism, giant scale, unconventional materials. The two styles now coexist: ek-chala for tradition-bound pujas, free composition for the theme era — and however far a theme puja reimagines the figures, the iconography underneath stays readable: Durga serene at the centre, the lion mid-leap, Mahishasura emerging from the buffalo, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Kartik and Ganesha in attendance (see [The Goddess and Her Story](01-the-goddess-and-her-story.md)).

## In a probasi pujo

Distance reshapes every part of this story, starting with a simple question: where does the goddess come from?

Some overseas and out-of-state pujas import a Kumortuli protima — the potters' quarter has a long-established export trade, crating idols (often lightweight shola-and-fibre constructions built to survive the journey) to communities across the world, and many probasi associations treasure their "Kumortuli thakur." But a great many probasi pujas, including those in Pune, source their idols closer to home. Maharashtra has its own deep murti-making tradition, centred on Ganeshotsav, and its cities host workshops — including Bengali artisan families who have settled outside Bengal — that take Durga commissions each autumn. A locally made protima has real advantages: no freight, no breakage risk, the ability to visit the workshop with your children and watch the goddess take shape, and a relationship with an artisan family that deepens year on year — a probasi echo of the old bonedi households and their hereditary kumors.

Then comes the material question, and with it a genuine debate. Many probasi (and Indian metro) pujas use fibreglass or similar durable idols that are *not* immersed but stored and reused for years — economical, transport-friendly, and immersion-free. Traditionalists reply that the annual cycle of clay-to-river is not incidental but essential: a reused goddess, they argue, misses the festival's central lesson of welcome and letting go. Communities resolve this differently, and honestly there is no single right answer; some keep a durable display idol but immerse a small clay protima, or perform a symbolic immersion of the *ghot* or a reflection ritual instead.

Ecology has become the deciding factor in many cities. Immersion of painted idols — especially plaster-of-Paris idols with chemical paints — measurably harms rivers and lakes, and courts and municipal bodies across India have tightened the rules; Pune, with its enormous Ganeshotsav experience, is actually ahead of most of Bengal here, with artificial immersion tanks provided by the municipal corporation, campaigns for natural-clay idols and lead-free paints, and initiatives such as Pune-based eCoexist's Punaravartan project, which collects immersed clay and returns it to artisans for reuse. A probasi Durga Puja in Pune thus often practises what Bengal is still moving toward: a natural-clay protima, immersed in a tank rather than a river, her clay recovered rather than lost. The theology survives the adaptation intact — the goddess still comes, is loved, and is let go; the river is simply spared.

And every probasi child who visits a local workshop in September, and watches an artisan draw two long eyes on a golden face, is standing inside the same tradition as a child in a Kumortuli lane: watching clay become the Mother.

## Words to know

- *Kumortuli* (কুমোরটুলি) — the potters' quarter of north Kolkata; from *kumor* (কুমোর), potter. Ghurni (Krishnanagar) and Shankhari Bazar (Dhaka) are its great counterparts.
- *protima* (প্রতিমা) / *murti* (মূর্তি) — the worship image of a deity.
- *mritshilpi* (মৃৎশিল্পী) — clay artist; the professional idol-maker.
- *kathamo* (কাঠামো) — the bamboo-and-wood frame of the idol; *kathamo puja* is its ritual worship before building begins.
- *pat puja* (পাটপূজা) — worship of the idol's wooden base plank, observed by some on Rath Yatra day.
- *khar* (খড়) / *bichali* (বিচালি) — rice straw, bound over the frame to form the idol's body.
- *entel maati* (এঁটেল মাটি) — sticky clay used for the first coat; *bele maati* (বেলে মাটি) — fine sandy clay for the finish.
- *ek mete* (এক মেটে) / *do mete* (দো মেটে) — the first and second clay coats.
- *Ganga maati* (গঙ্গামাটি) — clay of the Ganga, the idol's principal material.
- *punya maati* (পুণ্য মাটি) — "blessed soil" from a courtesan's doorstep, traditionally begged for and mixed into the clay; from the *nishiddho palli* (নিষিদ্ধ পল্লি), the "forbidden quarter."
- *saaj* (সাজ) — the goddess's ornamentation and dress.
- *sholar saaj* (শোলার সাজ) — ornament carved from *shola* (শোলা), white marsh-reed pith, by *malakar* (মালাকার) craftsmen.
- *daaker saaj* (ডাকের সাজ) — foil-and-sequin ornament; named from *daak* (ডাক), "post," because its German silver foil once arrived by parcel post. *Rangta* (রাংতা) is the metallic foil itself.
- *chokkhu daan* (চক্ষুদান) — "the gift of eyes"; the ritual painting of the goddess's eyes, traditionally on Mahalaya.
- *ek-chala* (একচালা) — the traditional composition with Durga and her children against a single backdrop frame.
- *bonedi bari* (বনেদি বাড়ি) — Kolkata's old aristocratic households, the original patrons of Kumortuli.
- *Mahalaya* (মহালয়া) — the day opening *Debi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ), the goddess's fortnight; see [Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md).
- *bisorjon* (বিসর্জন) — the immersion of the idol at the festival's end (also *visarjan*).
