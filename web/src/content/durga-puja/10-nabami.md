## What it is

*Maha Nabami* (মহানবমী) is the ninth day of Durga Puja and the last full day on which the goddess is worshipped — the last full day she is home, and the pujo knows it. If Saptami is the day the Devi arrives in her image and Ashtami is the day of her highest glory, Nabami is the day the community pours out everything it has left. Everything on Nabami runs at maximum: the fire, the food, the drumming, the dancing, the length of the evening — because everyone knows what tomorrow brings. On Dashami the goddess departs.

That knowledge gives Nabami its unmistakable double register. On the surface it is the most festive day of the pujo: the fire ritual crackles through the morning, the *bhog* (ভোগ) is at its most lavish, and the evening belongs to dance competitions and cultural programmes that run late into the night. But underneath the celebration runs a current of ache. An old Bengali song pleads with the ninth night itself not to end — *Nabami nishi* (নবমী নিশি), the Nabami night, is begged to linger, because when it passes, the daughter of the house must leave. The phrase needs no translation once it has been felt. Bengalis will tell you that on Nabami evening they laugh the hardest and feel the saddest, often in the same breath. Children sense it too: the pandal is at its brightest, and the grown-ups keep saying, "kal to Dashami" — tomorrow is already the tenth.

Ritually, Nabami is a day of completion. The worship that began with *bodhon* (বোধন), the awakening, is formally brought to a close with the great fire offering called the *homa* (হোম) or *yajna* (যজ্ঞ), sealed with a final offering called the *purnahuti* (পূর্ণাহুতি) — literally, the "complete oblation". Whatever remained to be given is given today, and what is given to the flame is understood to reach the goddess directly. The whole pujo's asking is folded into smoke.

## When it happens

Nabami falls on the ninth lunar day (*tithi*, তিথি) of the bright fortnight of the month of Ashwin — usually late September or October — the day after Maha Ashtami and the day before Bijoya Dashami. Its boundary with Ashtami is itself sacred: the 48-minute juncture where Ashtami ends and Nabami begins is the *sandhikkhan* (সন্ধিক্ষণ), when the fierce Sandhi Puja is performed. So Nabami technically begins in the middle of that dramatic transition, often late at night or before dawn.

The morning brings the Nabami puja proper, and the homa is traditionally performed in daylight hours after it — ritual manuals hold that a homa performed at night does not bear fruit, so priests take care to complete it before the day wanes. Bhog is offered around midday, distributed through the afternoon, and the evening is given over to *aarti* (আরতি), *dhunuchi naach* (ধুনুচি নাচ), and the cultural programme's biggest night. Because the lunar calendar does not align neatly with the solar one, in some years Nabami and Dashami share a calendar day, compressing the schedule — one reason the exact timings printed in the pujo's *nirghonto* (নির্ঘণ্ট), the ritual timetable, matter so much.

## The rituals, step by step

### The Nabami morning puja

The day opens as the others do: the priest, the *purohit* (পুরোহিত), bathes, performs his preparatory rites, and then worships the Devi once more with the full sixteen-fold hospitality — flowers, incense, lamp, food, water, cloth — accompanied by recitation from the *Chandi* (চণ্ডী), the Devi Mahatmya, whose seven hundred verses narrate the goddess's battles. On Nabami many traditions turn to the Chandi's later chapters — the slaying of the demons Shumbha and Nishumbha — as the epic of the goddess's wars is read toward its close. The Devi is worshipped along with her full retinue, and in many lineages she is honoured especially in her aspect as destroyer of the buffalo demon, for reasons the story section below makes clear. What sets the morning apart is that everyone knows this is the last full worship: regulars who have drifted in and out all week make a point of sitting through the Nabami *pushpanjali* (পুষ্পাঞ্জলি), the flower offering made with folded hands, often given in three rounds after the priest.

### The Nabami homa — the fire ritual

The centrepiece of Nabami is the *homa* (হোম), also called the *yajna* (যজ্ঞ) or simply *hom*: the ancient Vedic act of offering into fire, which carries gifts to the deity through Agni, the fire god, as messenger. Fire is the oldest carrier of Indian worship, and the homa is the ritual signature of the day. Watching it is one of the great experiences of the pujo — the leaping flames, the rhythmic Sanskrit, the sweet-sharp smell of burning ghee and wood. (The homa's own mantras have their chapter on the mantra shelf: see [the homa mantras](18-mantras-homa.md).)

**The kund.** A fire altar is prepared — the *homa kunda* (হোম কুণ্ড), typically a square pit or bed, or a portable copper vessel set on sanctified ground near the deity, its boundary marked out and consecrated with mantras. Sand or earth is laid down, and the site is purified with water and flowers before any fire is kindled.

**The fire and the fuel.** The fire is lit ceremonially, with the invocation of Agni, and treated as a divine presence in its own right. In Bengali tradition the preferred fuel is wood of the *bel* (বেল) tree — the wood-apple or bilva, sacred to both Shiva and the Devi — though in practice pujas use whatever clean, dry sacrificial wood (*samidh*, সমিধ) they can source. Into the fire the priest offers streams of clarified butter, *ghee* (ঘি), spooned from a ladle; sesame seeds, *til* (তিল); rice, grains and sweets; and other prescribed ingredients, each offering released with the exclamation *swaha* (স্বাহা) — the fire's "take this", the word that dedicates the oblation and delivers it.

**The 108 bel leaves.** The most beloved element, in many family and community pujas of Bengal, is the offering of 108 *bel pata* (বেলপাতা) — leaves of the bel tree, each one traditionally dipped or smeared in ghee and offered into the flames one by one, often with a name of the Devi recited for each. The number 108 recurs throughout the pujo — 108 lamps and 108 lotuses at Sandhi Puja — and carries its ancient sense of completeness and auspiciousness. Practice varies by lineage: some houses offer 108 leaves, some offer other counts or substitute lotuses, and the exact list of oblations differs from one priestly manual to another; but the picture of a priest patiently feeding glossy trefoil leaves into a fire is one of the enduring images of Nabami. In many pujas the homa is accompanied by recitation from the Durga Saptashati, with an oblation offered at each verse — a full performance of which can take hours, which is why the homa dominates the Nabami daytime.

**Purnahuti.** The homa ends with the *purnahuti* (পূর্ণাহুতি), the "complete offering" — a final, culminating oblation that declares the yajna, and with it the worship of the season, fulfilled. Depending on tradition this may be a whole fruit such as a coconut, a bundle of auspicious items wrapped in red cloth, or a last unbroken stream of ghee, offered as the mantras rise to a close. The symbolism is surrender without remainder: everything, finally, is given to the goddess. The priest then marks his forehead — and often the foreheads of the gathered devotees — with the cooled ash of the sacred fire, a blessing carried home from the flames. Monastic centres observe this rite with particular solemnity; the Nabami homa at Belur Math, the Ramakrishna Order's headquarters, is famous and draws large crowds.

### Balidan — sacrifice and its substitutes

Durga is a warrior goddess, and her worship historically included *balidan* (বলিদান) — animal sacrifice, most commonly of a goat — performed at Sandhi Puja and again on Nabami in the houses and temples that kept the custom; in those that keep the old ways, Nabami's balidan traditions were the richest of the festival. This is a part of the tradition that must be described honestly: it was once widespread, it survives today in a small number of old *bonedi* (বনেদি) aristocratic households and Shakta temples, and it has largely disappeared everywhere else.

The great transformation came with the spread of Vaishnav devotional culture in Bengal, which promoted non-violence and turned most pujas vegetarian in their offerings. In place of an animal, the priest today sacrifices vegetables and fruits with the same ritual — a *chalkumro* (চালকুমড়ো, ash gourd) split with a single stroke of the sacrificial blade is the most common, along with sugarcane, *aakh* (আখ), banana, and cucumber, *shosha* (শসা). The substitute is treated exactly as the animal was: consecrated, marked with vermilion, dispatched in one clean cut. The inner meaning, priests explain, was never really about the animal at all — the true sacrifice is of the beast within, the ego and its appetites, offered up before the Devi. Families differ, sometimes sharply, in how they feel about the old custom, and a pujo article should say plainly: both the memory and the reform are part of the story.

### The grand Nabami bhog

Nabami's *bhog* — the cooked food offered to the goddess and then shared by all as *prasad* (প্রসাদ) — is by tradition the most sumptuous of the festival: the year's feast. Where Saptami's bhog might be simple and Ashtami's pure and austere, Nabami's is often described as *raja-bhog* — food fit for royalty. The classic spread is *niramish* (নিরামিষ), vegetarian and cooked without onion or garlic: fragrant *polao* (পোলাও) or the beloved *khichuri* (খিচুড়ি) of rice and roasted moong dal; *labra* (লাবড়া), the great mixed-vegetable mash; fried delights like *beguni* (বেগুনি), fried eggplant and even lotus stem where kitchens are ambitious; rich preparations of cottage cheese such as *chanar dalna* (ছানার ডালনা); a sweet-tart *chatni* (চাটনি); *payesh* (পায়েস), rice pudding; and sweets in ranked rows. Community sittings run all afternoon, and the elders have a saying about them: the measure of a pujo's soul is whether the last server eats as well as the first guest.

But some lineages carry a different Nabami tradition, descended directly from the sacrificial past: *bhoger mangsho* (ভোগের মাংস), meat offered to the goddess. In houses that once performed balidan, the meat of the sacrificed goat was cooked — always without onion or garlic, since those are excluded from offerings — and served as *mahaprasad*. The dish survives even where the sacrifice does not, under the wonderfully paradoxical name *niramish mangsho* (নিরামিষ মাংস), "vegetarian mutton": a goat curry made in the ritual style, whose contradiction every Bengali understands without explanation, prepared in some families on Nabami to this day — especially in lineages of East Bengal descent, for whom Nabami is *the* day of mangsho. A few pujas offer fish. None of this is contradiction so much as history on a plate — Bengal's Shakta tradition never equated devotion with vegetarianism, and each family's Nabami menu preserves its own past. Whatever the menu, the logic of bhog is unchanged: the Devi eats first, and what she has touched becomes blessing, distributed to every person present without distinction.

### Sandhya aarti and the language of the dhak

As dusk falls comes the evening *aarti* (আরতি), the waving of lights before the deity — on Nabami, usually the biggest of the festival. The priest offers, in sequence, the five-flamed lamp (*panchapradip*, পঞ্চপ্রদীপ), burning camphor, incense smoke, cloth, flowers, and the yak-tail whisk (*chamor*, চামর), circling each before the Devi's face while the gong (*kanshor*, কাঁসর) clangs, women trill the *uludhwani* (উলুধ্বনি), conch shells blow, and above it all the *dhak* (ঢাক) thunders.

The dhak — the huge barrel drum slung from the shoulder and beaten with two thin sticks (*kathi*, কাঠি) — deserves its own paragraph, because it is not mere accompaniment; it is a language. A skilled *dhaki* (ঢাকি) plays distinct *bols* (বোল) — rhythmic phrases — for each moment of the pujo: one pattern for bodhon and the goddess's welcome, another for the bathing of the *nabapatrika*, another for Sandhi Puja, a rolling ecstatic beat for aarti, and a slow, heavy cadence for the immersion. Bengali ears "hear" words inside these rhythms; the most famous folk rendering sets the bisarjan beat to the words *ṭhakur thakbe koto-khon, thakur jabe bisorjon* — "how much longer will the deity stay? the deity goes to immersion" — which is why the mere change of a dhak rhythm on Dashami can bring tears. On Nabami evening the dhakis play their most exuberant repertoire, often two or three drummers interlocking, feathers nodding on their drums — tomorrow's farewell already in their wrists, they play the ninth night as if to hold the tithi open by force — and it is entirely normal for the whole pandal to end up dancing.

### Dhunuchi naach — the incense dance

Out of the aarti grows Nabami's signature spectacle: *dhunuchi naach* (ধুনুচি নাচ), the incense-burner dance. The *dhunuchi* (ধুনুচি) is a flared, goblet-shaped, stemmed censer of baked clay — clay insulates, which is what makes the dance possible at all. Into it goes a bed of dried coconut husk (and often a few embers of coal), lit and coaxed into glowing, and onto that is sprinkled *dhuno* (ধুনো) — the fragrant resin of the sal tree, Bengal's frankincense — with camphor sometimes added. The resin hitting the embers sends up thick, sweet, billowing white smoke until the vessel pours like a chimney.

The dancer takes up the smoking dhunuchi — one in each hand for the ambitious, and the true virtuosos grip a third by its stem between their teeth — and dances before the goddess to the dhak's fastest, most driving patterns: swaying, dipping, sweeping the censers in arcs so that smoke ribbons and wreathes the whole space, balancing on their knees, sometimes lying back with the burners still held aloft, the crowd clapping the rhythm in. Sparks fly; nobody minds. It is devotion as athletics — the body offered as much as the flame — and every para, every neighbourhood, has its legendary dancer. The dance is understood as an offering of thanks to the Devi — smoke, skill, breath and sweat given as worship — and it is traditionally linked to the celebration of her victory over Mahishasura. Once an affair of men, dhunuchi naach today belongs to everyone; women and children compete with as much fire as anyone. And compete they do: most community pujas, on Nabami night, hold formal dhunuchi naach competitions, with heats, cheering sections, and prizes, and it is often the single most photographed hour of the entire festival. (A word to young dancers: the clay stays cool-ish, but the embers are real — sarees and dupattas are pinned back, and someone sensible stands by with water.)

### The close of worship — and the last night

Late on Nabami, in many traditions, the priest performs a quiet rite that visitors rarely notice: having received everything, the Devi's active worship is concluded, and the deity is, in effect, given leave to prepare for departure. The lamps still burn and the pandal still roars with the cultural programme outside, but ritually the long liturgy that began with bodhon has been completed. What remains for [Dashami](11-dashami.md) is farewell.

And then, after the aarti, nobody leaves. Nabami night is the pujo's long goodbye party — the *adda* (আড্ডা), that long, artful Bengali conversation, at its deepest; the pandal-hopping at its most determined, with the queues at the famous pandals running till dawn; the cultural programme's final act; the kitchen's last payesh. Somewhere past midnight the knowledge settles that tomorrow the ghot will be shifted, the mirror shown, the river waiting. The tradition does not hide this; it is the point. And already the phrase that will carry everyone through the year is being said, half consolation and half promise: *asche bochor abar hobe* (আসছে বছর আবার হবে) — next year, it will all happen again.

## Why it matters

Nabami teaches, more clearly than any other day, that celebration and impermanence are not opposites. The festival's whole architecture builds to this day — and the day itself is shadowed by its own ending. For Bengalis this is not a design flaw but the entire point: the Devi comes as a daughter comes home, and daughters go back. Loving something fully while knowing it will end is, Nabami suggests, the only way anything is ever loved. The pujo teaches, each year, that the fullest joy and the nearness of ending are the same lesson.

The homa adds a second lesson: completion. A pujo is not allowed to simply trail off; it is finished, deliberately, with fire and a final offering that holds nothing back. The purnahuti is a ritual enactment of wholeheartedness — a yearly reminder that things worth doing are worth completing utterly. And the day's social texture — thousands fed from one kitchen, dancers of every age before one goddess, the drummer's ancient vocabulary understood by all — is community-making of the most practical kind. For children growing up far from Bengal, Nabami night is often the memory that lasts: the smoke, the drums, the crowd, the feeling of belonging to something old and alive.

## The story behind it

The mythic frame of Nabami comes from the *Devi Mahatmya*: the gods, humiliated by the buffalo demon *Mahishasura* (মহিষাসুর) — who held a boon that no man or god could kill him — pooled their energies into a blazing female form, armed her with their own weapons, and sent her into battle. The war raged for nine days and nights, the demon shifting shapes — buffalo, lion, elephant, man — until the Devi pinned him and severed his head. Tradition places the climax of that battle at the edge of Nabami: many hold that the slaying occurred at the *sandhikkhan*, the Ashtami–Nabami juncture, when the goddess as Chamunda destroyed the demons Chanda and Munda and then finished Mahishasura himself, with Dashami dawning as the day of accomplished victory — *Vijaya* (বিজয়া). Regional tellings differ on the precise day, and honest tellers say so; what all agree on is that Nabami is the ninth night of the war, the night the tide of the cosmos turned.

Bengal adds its own layer through the Ramayana as told by Krittibas: Rama, before his final battle with Ravana, performed the *akal bodhan* (অকালবোধন) — the "untimely awakening" of the Devi in autumn, which is why Bengal's pujo happens in Ashwin at all — worshipping her with 108 blue lotuses and, when one went missing, offering his own eye before the goddess stayed his hand. His worship won her blessing; Ravana fell, by the common reckoning, on the tenth day. Nabami thus sits at the hinge of two victories at once — the Devi's over Mahishasura and Rama's over Ravana — which is why the day hums with the word *joy* (জয়), victory, even as the household mood turns toward parting.

## In a probasi pujo

In a *probasi* (প্রবাসী) — expatriate — pujo like ours in Magarpatta, Nabami is unmistakably the big night. Working families who could only glimpse Saptami and Ashtami on weekday evenings pour in; the cultural programme peaks with its most anticipated performances; the dhunuchi naach competition — with children's rounds, and smoke alarms prudently consulted beforehand — fills the floor; and the pandal stays loud and bright later than on any other night, because everyone is postponing tomorrow. Nabami dinner is the community's banquet night, the one meal everyone attends.

The bhog operation on Nabami is community logistics at full stretch. Cooking begins before dawn: volunteer teams and hired cooks working over huge pots of khichuri and labra, chatni stirred in vessels the size of bathtubs, payesh thickening slowly, serving lines organised with coupons or tokens so that many hundreds of plates move smoothly through the afternoon. Sourcing is its own adventure — bel leaves for the homa, dhuno and clay dhunuchis, a dhaki booked months in advance — and in a city like Pune much of it travels from Bengali grocers or arrives in suitcases from Kolkata. The homa itself is adapted sensibly to the venue: fire-safety rules mean a contained copper kunda — a modest fire-bed in a metal tray on a protected floor has closed many a rented-hall pujo — a cleared perimeter, and an extinguisher standing discreetly behind the purohit; some communities, where venue rules leave no choice, reduce the rite to a symbolic lamp-offering, and the purohit knows the permitted substitutions. None of this diminishes the rite; if anything, watching a community conjure a full Nabami — fire, feast, drums and dance — in a Maharashtrian housing society is its own small proof of what the day celebrates: that the goddess goes wherever her people carry her. The last-night ache is, if anything, sharper away from Bengal: the hall must be returned tomorrow, and the year is long.

## Words to know

- *Maha Nabami* (মহানবমী) — the great ninth day; the last full day of the Devi's worship.
- *Homa / yajna* (হোম / যজ্ঞ) — the Vedic fire ritual; offerings are placed in a consecrated fire (its mantras: [the homa chapter](18-mantras-homa.md)).
- *Homa kunda* (হোম কুণ্ড) — the fire pit or altar in which the homa fire burns.
- *Bel / bel pata* (বেল / বেলপাতা) — the wood-apple (bilva) tree and its sacred trefoil leaves; 108 ghee-smeared leaves are offered into the Nabami fire in many traditions.
- *Ghee* (ঘি) and *til* (তিল) — clarified butter and sesame seeds, staple oblations of the homa.
- *Swaha* (স্বাহা) — the word spoken as each offering enters the fire; the fire's "take this".
- *Purnahuti* (পূর্ণাহুতি) — the "complete offering" that concludes the homa and the season's worship.
- *Balidan* (বলিদান) — ritual sacrifice; today almost everywhere performed on substitutes such as the *chalkumro* (চালকুমড়ো, ash gourd), sugarcane and banana.
- *Bhog* (ভোগ) — cooked food offered to the deity and shared as *prasad* (প্রসাদ).
- *Bhoger mangsho / niramish mangsho* (ভোগের মাংস / নিরামিষ মাংস) — the no-onion-no-garlic goat curry some lineages offer on Nabami, a survival of the sacrificial past.
- *Dhunuchi* (ধুনুচি) — the flared clay incense burner; *dhuno* (ধুনো) is the sal-tree resin burned in it over coconut husk.
- *Dhunuchi naach* (ধুনুচি নাচ) — the incense-burner dance performed to dhak rhythms, often as a competition.
- *Dhak* (ঢাক) — the great barrel drum of the pujo; a *dhaki* (ঢাকি) plays distinct *bols* (বোল), rhythmic phrases, for each ritual moment.
- *Aarti* (আরতি) — the waving of lamps and other offerings before the deity.
- *Sandhikkhan* (সন্ধিক্ষণ) — the sacred juncture where Ashtami turns to Nabami.
- *Nabami nishi* (নবমী নিশি) — "the ninth night", begged in song not to end; shorthand for Nabami's bittersweetness.
- *Adda* (আড্ডা) — the long, artful Bengali conversation; Nabami night is its festival.
- *Asche bochor abar hobe* (আসছে বছর আবার হবে) — "next year, it will happen again"; consolation and promise, first said on Nabami night.
