## What it is

Every Bengali festival has a beginning, but Durga Puja has something rarer: a beginning that is itself beloved. *Mahalaya* (মহালয়া) is the new-moon day that closes the fortnight of the ancestors and opens the fortnight of the Goddess. It is not yet Pujo — the pandals are unfinished, the idols may still be drying — yet ask any Bengali which single morning of the year feels most charged, and many will name the pre-dawn dark of Mahalaya, when a conch sounds on the radio and a voice that has been dead since 1991 begins to recite Sanskrit, and suddenly the festival is no longer weeks away. It has begun.

The word itself is usually explained as *maha* (মহা), "great," joined to *alaya* (আলয়), "abode" or "dwelling" — the great abode. Whose abode is meant is read two ways, and both are true to the day. Some take it as the great dwelling of the ancestors, the *pitris* (পিতৃ), because Mahalaya is the culmination of *Pitri Paksha* (পিতৃপক্ষ), the ancestral fortnight, and the most important day of the year for offering them water and remembrance. Others take it as the abode of the Goddess, because tradition holds that on this day Durga is invoked and begins her descent — in the Bengali imagination, her journey from her husband Shiva's home on Mount Kailash back to her parents' house on earth. The day faces both directions at once: backward toward those we have lost, forward toward the daughter who is coming home. In a single dawn Bengal turns from its dead to its Mother, and the day belongs equally to both — one asks the blessing of one's own departed before asking anything of the Goddess.

Because of that double character, even the greeting exchanged on this day is gently contested. Millions of WhatsApp messages say *"Subho Mahalaya"* — auspicious Mahalaya — every year, and millions of others point out, every year, that a day of rites for the dead is solemn, not celebratory, and should not be called *subho* at all. Neither side ever wins, which is itself very Bengali; this chapter will try to show why both instincts are right.

## When it happens

Mahalaya falls on the *amavasya* (অমাবস্যা) — the new-moon day — at the end of the dark fortnight of the lunar month of Ashwin (in some years, by regional calendars, late Bhadra), which lands in September or October of the English calendar. In the wider Hindu calendar this day is known as *Sarva Pitri Amavasya* or Mahalaya Amavasya: the last and most powerful day of Pitri Paksha, the roughly sixteen-day period during which the spirits of ancestors are believed to draw near to their descendants and receive offerings.

The moment the amavasya ends, the bright fortnight begins — and this particular bright fortnight is *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ), the fortnight of the Goddess, which runs from Mahalaya all the way to the full moon of Kojagari Purnima, the night of Lakshmi Puja. Shashthi, Saptami, Ashtami, Navami and Vijaya Dashami, the five great days of Durga Puja, all fall within it, with Dashami on the tenth lunar day. In an ordinary year, therefore, Mahalaya arrives six or seven days before Shashthi, and works as the community's countdown clock: once you have heard the Mahalaya broadcast, Pujo is one week away.

Occasionally the calendar plays a trick. In 2020, an intercalary month (*mala mash*, a "leap month" that keeps the lunar and solar calendars aligned) fell between the two fortnights, and Mahalaya arrived more than a month before Shashthi — a reminder that Mahalaya is fixed by the lunar calendar, not by its usual one-week distance from Pujo.

One almanac note belongs to this day too, because it is on Mahalaya that panjika-readers begin debating it in earnest: the Goddess's yearly *arrival and departure vehicles*. Each year she is said to come and to leave by elephant, horse, boat or palanquin, the vehicles read from the weekdays on which the pujo's key days fall, and each carries a traditional omen for the year's fortunes — the elephant promising plenty, the horse unrest, the boat both flood and harvest, the palanquin epidemic and upheaval. Whether taken as forecast or as folklore, the annual reading of the omens is one of Mahalaya's small, relished rituals of conversation.

## The rituals, step by step

Mahalaya's observances are unusual among festival days in that the most important ones happen before sunrise, and one of them is performed not in a temple or at home but through a loudspeaker.

### Tarpan — water for the ancestors

*Tarpan* (তর্পণ) comes from a Sanskrit root meaning "to satisfy" or "to satiate." It is the offering of water — usually mixed with black sesame — to the departed, and on Mahalaya morning it is performed on a scale seen on no other day of the year. In Kolkata, tens of thousands descend the ghats of the Hooghly at first light — Babughat, Ahiritola, Bagbazar — standing waist-deep in the river in wet cotton, palms cupped, murmuring names, while police barricade the steps and rescue boats patrol the water. The same scene repeats at the ghats of the Buriganga in Dhaka, at ponds and tanks in every district town, even at buckets on apartment terraces. The sight of a whole city waist-deep in its river, remembering its dead, never stops being extraordinary.

The procedure, as classically prescribed, goes like this. The offerer bathes first, because the rite must be performed in a state of purity, and wears simple, traditionally unstitched or white cloth. He faces south — the direction of Yama and of the ancestors (gods are honoured facing east; the pitris, facing south). Those who wear the sacred thread shift it from the left shoulder to the right, a reversed position called *apasavya* used only for ancestral rites. On the ring finger goes a small ring woven of *kusha* grass (কুশ) — a sacred grass, botanically *Desmostachya bipinnata*, which tradition regards as a purifying conductor of offerings; texts on shraddha rites say the ancestors are invoked upon it. Then, with black sesame seeds (*til*, তিল) held in the cupped palms along with water — Ganga water, *gangajal* (গঙ্গাজল), where it can be had; any clean water consecrated by intention where it cannot — the offerer lets the water fall from the part of the palm between thumb and forefinger, which the ritual manuals call the *pitri-tirtha*, the ancestors' ford. The pouring is repeated while reciting the names and lineage of one's father, grandfather and great-grandfather, then the maternal line, and then — this is the special generosity of Mahalaya — for all departed souls, known and unknown, including those who have no one left to remember them. Fuller observances add *pinda daan*, balls of cooked rice mixed with til and ghee, and the feeding of Brahmins, crows, cows and dogs, each regarded as a messenger or carrier of the offering.

The offering of sesame-water — *tilodaka* (তিলোদক) — closes with a verse that widens the circle to everyone who has ever lived:

> ॐ आब्रह्मभुवनाल्लोका देवर्षिपितृमानवाः।
> तृप्यन्तु पितरः सर्वे मातृमातामहादयः॥
> अतीतकुलकोटीनां सप्तद्वीपनिवासिनाम्।
> आब्रह्मभुवनाल्लोकादिदमस्तु तिलोदकम्॥
>
> ওঁ আব্রহ্মভুবনাল্লোকা দেবর্ষিপিতৃমানবাঃ।
> তৃপ্যন্তু পিতরঃ সর্বে মাতৃমাতামহাদয়ঃ॥
> অতীতকুলকোটীনাং সপ্তদ্বীপনিবাসিনাম্।
> আব্রহ্মভুবনাল্লোকাদিদমস্তু তিলোদকম্॥
>
> *om ābrahma-bhuvanāl lokā devarṣi-pitṛ-mānavāḥ ·
> tṛpyantu pitaraḥ sarve mātṛ-mātāmahādayaḥ ·
> atīta-kula-koṭīnāṁ sapta-dvīpa-nivāsinām ·
> ābrahma-bhuvanāl lokād idam astu tilodakam*

**In simple words:** From Brahma's realm down to this world — gods, sages, ancestors, human beings — may all the departed be satisfied: mothers, mothers' fathers, everyone. To the countless generations gone before, dwellers of all the seven continents, may this water and sesame reach them all.

Who performs it? Classically, tarpan is the duty of descendants whose father has died — most commonly sons, which is why the crowds at the ghats are overwhelmingly male. But practice is wider and getting wider: many families hold that anyone may offer water to the ancestors on Sarva Pitri Amavasya, daughters increasingly perform tarpan for their parents, and priests at the ghats guide anyone who asks through the Sanskrit. There is no single all-India rulebook here; family custom governs, and it is honest to say that traditions differ from house to house.

One thing tarpan is not: sad. Watch the ghats and you will see something closer to a family reunion across the boundary of death — an annual appointment kept, a debt of gratitude settled in water and sesame. The story of why this fortnight belongs to the ancestors — the story of Karna — is told below in "The story behind it."

### Mahishasuramardini — the 4 a.m. radio ritual

No scripture prescribes it, no priest conducts it, and yet the second great ritual of Mahalaya is observed with more unanimity than almost any rite in Bengali life: at 4 a.m., you wake up, and you listen.

*Mahishasuramardini* (মহিষাসুরমর্দিনী) — "the slayer of the buffalo demon" — is a ninety-minute programme of All India Radio, Calcutta: an interweaving of Sanskrit recitation from the Devi Mahatmya, Bengali devotional songs, and orchestral music, narrating the creation of the Goddess and her war with Mahishasura. It opens with the sound of the conch and the choral hymn "Ya Chandi," and closes, ninety minutes later, with the song "Shanti Dile Bhori." In between, the recitation of Birendra Krishna Bhadra rises from a murmur to a storm — his delivery of the Chandi's *Ya devi sarvabhuteshu* litany, the hymn to the Goddess who abides in all beings, is for most Bengalis the sound of the festival itself. The songs between the Sanskrit passages are classics known by heart across generations: Dwijen Mukhopadhyay's "Jago Durga, Jago Dashapraharanadharini" ("Awake, Durga, wielder of the ten weapons") and Supriti Ghosh's "Bajlo Tomar Alor Benu" ("Your flute of light has sounded") among the most loved. Families who agree on nothing else agree on this recording.

The "procedure," such as it is, has hardened into genuine ritual over nine decades. The alarm is set for a few minutes before four. Someone — traditionally the eldest — turns on the radio (now, as often, a phone). Lights stay low; tea may appear; nobody talks over Bhadra. Many families bathe first, treating the listening itself as an act of worship. When the programme ends, dawn is breaking, and the day of tarpan and Pujo preparations begins. The programme's history — its disputed birth year, the caste controversy around its narrator, the famous 1976 attempt to replace him — is told below, because it is one of the best-documented stories in Indian broadcasting; and the broadcast itself, movement by movement, song by song, with the Sanskrit it recites, has a companion chapter of its own: [The Mahalaya Broadcast](21-the-mahalaya-broadcast.md).

A word on the songs' theme, because it frames the whole festival. Many songs of the season belong to the genre called *agomoni* (আগমনী), "songs of arrival," and their subject is startlingly domestic: not a war goddess but a married daughter. Uma — the Bengali household name for Parvati/Durga — lives far away in Kailash with her ascetic husband Shiva. Her mother Menoka, in the Himalayan palace, aches for her; in the songs she scolds her husband Giriraj (the Himalaya personified) into fetching their daughter home for a visit of just a few days. Agomoni songs are Menoka's longing set to music — the dreams, the reproaches, the counting of days. This is the emotional key of the entire festival: Durga Puja as *the daughter's annual homecoming*, Saptami to Dashami her brief stay, Vijaya Dashami the tearful send-off. Mahalaya, in this frame, is the morning the journey home begins — which is why a fortnight that starts with rites for the dead can end its first morning with songs of almost unbearable happiness.

### Chandipath — reciting the Devi Mahatmya

Underneath the radio programme lies the scripture it dramatises. *Chandipath* (চণ্ডীপাঠ) means the recitation of the *Chandi* — the Bengali name for the *Devi Mahatmya* (দেবীমাহাত্ম্যম্‌, "the glory of the Goddess"), also called the *Durga Saptashati*, "the seven hundred verses." It is a Sanskrit text of thirteen chapters embedded in the Markandeya Purana, composed, scholars generally hold, somewhere between the fifth and seventh centuries CE — an inscription of 608 CE already quotes it. It is the foundational scripture of Goddess worship in Hinduism, and its middle episode, chapters two to four, is the Mahishasura story that gives the radio programme its name. The scripture itself — its structure, its great hymns, its litany in full — has its own chapter on the mantra shelf: [Mantras: The Chandi](20-mantras-the-chandi.md).

On Mahalaya, recitation of the Chandi — whole or in part — is undertaken by priests and devout householders as the formal invocation of the Devi Paksha; some households also make their ritual resolutions (*sankalpa*) for the coming Pujo, or set up a small *ghot sthapana* (a consecrated water-vessel) to mark the fortnight's opening, though the main *bodhan* (awakening) rite belongs to Shashthi. Who performs chandipath? Anyone who can — there is no restriction in most modern practice, though classically it was the province of trained Brahmin reciters, a point that made the radio programme's history genuinely dramatic, as we shall see. Items needed are minimal: the text, a lamp, perhaps flowers and incense before an image. What matters is the sound — the Chandi is believed to act through recitation itself, heard as much as understood.

### Chokkhu daan — giving the Goddess her eyes

While households listen and ghats fill with water-bearers, a quieter ritual happens in the workshops of the idol-makers. Through the monsoon months the artisans — most famously those of Kumortuli (কুমোরটুলি), the three-hundred-year-old potters' quarter of north Kolkata, which sends thousands of images across India and the world each year, and their counterparts from Krishnanagar to Dhaka — have built the Durga image up from bamboo, straw, and layer upon layer of river clay, then painted it. But by tradition the face is finished last, and the eyes last of all. The painting of the eyes is *chokkhu daan* (চক্ষুদান), literally "the gift of eyes," and by long custom it is done on Mahalaya.

The rite belongs to the master artisan — the senior-most hand in the workshop, often the head of a family that has made images for generations. Accounts from Kumortuli describe the painter approaching the task in a state of ritual care, sometimes after fasting, choosing an auspicious moment, and drawing the eyes — including the third eye on the forehead — with fine brushes in steady, practised strokes. It is regarded as the moment the image stops being clay: with sight, the murti becomes a dwelling the Goddess can enter — she is given eyes so that she can see us, on the very day she is said to set out toward us.

Honesty requires a footnote here. With workshops producing thousands of idols on tight delivery schedules, many images today receive their eyes well before Mahalaya; artisans themselves say the strict Mahalaya timing survives as an ideal, observed for some images and compressed for others. And some family pujas follow a different convention, painting the eyes during *bodhan* or on Saptami. The Mahalaya chokkhu daan is a strong and genuinely old Kumortuli tradition — but it is a craft tradition flexed by real-world deadlines, not a fixed scriptural injunction.

## Why it matters

Mahalaya matters, first, because it is the hinge of the sacred year. Hindu ritual time swings between obligations to the dead and celebrations of the divine, and this one dawn contains the pivot: the last water poured for the ancestors, the first songs sung for the Goddess. A child can grasp the idea easily — before you throw a party for the mother of the universe, you first sit quietly and thank the mothers and fathers who came before you.

Second, it matters because it demonstrates something remarkable: a community can create a ritual. Tarpan is ancient; the 4 a.m. radio vigil was invented by a scriptwriter, a composer and a narrator in a colonial-era studio, yet is now observed with the devotion of scripture. Sacredness, it turns out, is not only inherited but made — by repetition, love, and shared experience. For children growing up far from Bengal this is worth knowing: the traditions you keep in a Pune apartment complex are not lesser copies of "real" ones; they are the same process happening again.

Third, Mahalaya carries the festival's emotional thesis. The theology of Durga is cosmic — the combined power of all the gods, the destroyer of evil. But Mahalaya's songs insist she is also somebody's daughter, missed terribly, coming home for four days. Holding those two truths together — the invincible and the intimate — is the particular genius of the Bengali festival, and on Mahalaya morning the two are first braided.

## The story behind it

### Karna and the fortnight of the ancestors

Why does a fortnight belong to the dead? The story most often told comes from the Mahabharata tradition. Karna — the tragic hero famed above all for *daan*, giving — died in the Kurukshetra war and rose to heaven, where he was served food: gold. Plates of gold, jewels for grain. When he protested that he could not eat treasure, he was told the reason — in most tellings by Indra; in some versions by Yama, lord of death. All his life Karna had given away gold without limit, but never once food and water to his ancestors, for he had grown up not knowing who his ancestors were; and what one gives in life is what one receives after it. Moved by the unfairness — Karna's omission was ignorance, not neglect — the gods granted him leave to return to earth for a fortnight, during which he fed the hungry and offered water in his forebears' memory, and was received back satisfied. That fortnight became *Pitri Paksha*, when offerings reach the departed directly; its final amavasya, Mahalaya, gathers up all of it — the day when even those forgotten by everyone are remembered by someone.

### Mahishasura, and how the Goddess was made

The story the Chandi tells — the story Bengal wakes to hear — is the founding story of the whole festival, and its full telling, with the Goddess's theology and iconography, belongs to the opening chapter of this book: [The Goddess and Her Story](01-the-goddess-and-her-story.md). Here is the arc as the dawn broadcast carries it.

Mahishasura, "the buffalo demon," son of the asura Rambha, performed austerities so severe that Brahma appeared and offered him a boon. Mahishasura asked for immortality; refused (all that is born must die), he crafted what he believed was the perfect loophole: let no god, demon, or man be able to kill him — let death come, if it must, only from a woman. To an asura who had never met a woman who fought, this was immortality with extra steps. Boon secured, he did what boon-holders in these stories always do: made war on heaven, threw Indra off his throne, scattered the gods, and set himself up as lord of the three worlds.

The dispossessed gods went to Vishnu and Shiva, and as the tale of the demon's insolence was told, anger blazed out of the assembled gods as light — *tej* or *tejas* (তেজ), fiery radiance, is the text's word. From Vishnu's face, from Shiva's, from Brahma's, from the bodies of Indra and all the devas, streams of brilliance poured out, flowed together into a single mountain of flame, and condensed into the form of a woman: her face from Shiva's light, her hair from Yama's, her arms from Vishnu's, her waist from Indra's, her eyes from Agni's — the Devi Mahatmya builds her limb by limb from the powers of the entire pantheon. It is the festival's central theological image: the Goddess is not a junior member of the pantheon but what all the gods amount to when their power is gathered into one — and also, the text insists, the primordial Shakti who existed before them all; the tej merely revealed her.

Then each god armed her from his own attribute. As the Devi Mahatmya's second chapter lists them (tellings vary slightly in detail): Shiva drew a trident from his trident; Vishnu spun a discus from his discus; Varuna gave a conch and his noose; Agni a spear; Vayu a bow and quivers that never emptied; Indra a thunderbolt from his own vajra, and a bell from his elephant Airavata; Yama a staff from his rod of death; Brahma his water-pot and rosary; Surya filled her pores with his rays; Kala — Time itself — gave a sword and shining shield; the ocean of milk gave garments and jewels; Vishwakarma, architect of the gods, an axe and armour and ornaments; Kubera a cup of wine; the serpent-king Shesha a garland of snakes; and Himavat, lord of mountains — her grandfather, in the Bengali telling — gave her a lion to ride. Ten arms (in Bengal she is *Dashabhuja*, the ten-armed), each holding a gift, so that when she rides into battle, every power in the universe rides with her. Then she laughed — a laugh that shook the three worlds — and the war began.

Mahishasura's generals came first and fell in their thousands. When the demon king took the field himself, he fought as his nature allowed: shifting shape. A buffalo, storming her armies, lashing mountains apart with his tail; cornered, a lion; struck, a man with a sword; then a great elephant, dragging her lion with his trunk; then the buffalo again. The Devi, the text says, paused to drink from Kubera's cup, her eyes reddening, and told him — laugh, fool, while you can. Then she leapt, pinned the buffalo under her foot, and drove her spear (in the familiar iconography, the trident) into his neck; and as Mahishasura strained to emerge from the buffalo's mouth in his true form — half-out, sword in hand, caught forever in the moment of transformation, exactly as every Bengali idol shows him — she beheaded him. The gods rained flowers and hymns, and the Goddess promised to return whenever she is remembered in distress. The recitation of that promise, before dawn on Mahalaya, is the community formally remembering her.

### The radio programme: a documented history

Now the history — and it is genuine history, with dates, files, and one spectacular public-relations disaster.

In the early 1930s, the Indian Broadcasting Company's Calcutta station (later All India Radio, Akashvani) housed an extraordinary cluster of talent: the writer Baidyanath Bhattacharya, who wrote as *Bani Kumar*; the composer and singer Pankaj Kumar Mullick; music director Raichand Boral; and a staff artist named Birendra Krishna Bhadra (1905–1991), a playwright, actor and announcer with a voice of unusual dramatic range. From a brainstorming among them came a programme blending Sanskrit hymns from the Devi Mahatmya with newly composed Bengali songs — Bani Kumar's script, Mullick's music, Bhadra's recitation.

The programme's exact birth is honestly disputed. The most widely repeated account — followed by Wikipedia and much of the press — says it was first broadcast at dawn on Mahalaya 1931 and was an instant success. But broadcast historians and several researched accounts tell a more evolutionary story: a precursor programme, *Basanteshwari*, aired in the spring of 1932 for the vernal Navaratri; the format migrated to the autumn festival over the following seasons and acquired the name *Mahishasuramardini* around 1936–37. Both versions agree on the essentials — the same three creators, the same station, the early 1930s — and the discrepancy is a good lesson in how living traditions blur their own origins. For decades the programme was performed live: musicians, chorus and narrator assembling at the studio in the small hours of Mahalaya, on air as the city slept. Accounts of those sessions describe Bhadra arriving bathed and in traditional dress, treating the microphone as an altar; the performance was finally committed to record, and since 1966 AIR has broadcast the definitive pre-recorded version — the one heard to this day, running one hour and twenty-nine minutes.

Two controversies bracket its history, and both ended the same way: the public chose Bhadra.

The first was about caste. Bhadra was a Kayastha, not a Brahmin, and in the 1930s the idea of a non-Brahmin reciting the Chandi — scripture recitation being traditionally a Brahmin preserve — drew objections from orthodox quarters, reportedly including a senior colleague at the station itself. Bani Kumar and the production team simply refused to yield, and Bhadra went on air. The story goes that Bhadra answered the objection in one sentence: *"Ami path kori na, ami pujo kori"* — "I don't merely recite; I worship." The anecdote is widely told and may be polished by retelling, but the documented outcome is beyond dispute: within a few seasons, the community had decided that this non-Brahmin's voice was the sound of the Goddess's arrival, and no orthodoxy has ever managed to argue with that.

The second controversy has a date: 1976. All India Radio, judging the old programme dated, commissioned a glossy replacement called *Durga Durgatiharini* — narrated by Uttam Kumar, the biggest Bengali film star of the century, with music by Hemanta Mukherjee and songs by Lata Mangeshkar and Asha Bhosle. On paper, an unbeatable line-up. On Mahalaya morning, Bengal woke, heard the wrong voice, and erupted. Protests, furious letters, demonstrations at the AIR offices; contemporary accounts describe outrage "verging on state-wide rebellion." The point, listeners insisted, was not quality — it was that Mahalaya *was* Bhadra's voice; replacing it was like repainting a deity. The backlash reached Delhi; Information and Broadcasting Minister L. K. Advani was drawn into the retreat, and AIR capitulated — by most accounts rebroadcasting Bhadra's version within days (on Shashthi that same season) and restoring it permanently to Mahalaya from 1977. Uttam Kumar, blameless and reportedly deeply hurt, was gracious about it. The episode is now retold every year as a parable: the community, not the broadcaster, owns the ritual. Bhadra died in 1991; his recording has outlived him by three and a half decades and shows no sign of stopping.

## In a probasi pujo

For a probasi community — in Magarpatta City, Pune, as in New Jersey or Frankfurt — Mahalaya is in some ways the easiest observance to keep and in others the most poignant.

The 4 a.m. vigil travels perfectly, because its medium always was technology. Where a grandmother once turned a radio dial, a family now opens YouTube or a music app, and the same 1966 recording pours out of a phone speaker in the pre-dawn dark of a Pune flat — or streams across time zones to households keeping Bengali hours in other hemispheres. Many probasi families keep the discipline strictly — alarm at 3:50, lights low, no talking over the chandipath — precisely because, far from Bengal, the recording is the strongest single thread back. Some communities make the listening collective, gathering with tea and rewinding favourite passages. Children who cannot follow the Sanskrit still absorb what matters: once a year the whole household wakes in the dark to listen together, and afterwards the elders' phones flood with *Subho Mahalaya* messages and agomoni song clips on WhatsApp — the twenty-first-century descendant of the songs Menoka's neighbours once sang to each other. Bengali television channels air dramatised Mahishasuramardini programmes at dawn too; the purists hold out for audio only, eyes closed, exactly as it came through the radio. And in the week that follows, cultural evenings titled *Agomoni* fill the community calendar — the probasi para's own songs of arrival.

Tarpan requires more improvisation. Kolkata has the Ganga at the end of every street; Pune's rivers are a drive away, and no probasi para has a Babughat. Communities solve this the way diaspora always has: some organise a collective tarpan with the puja's priest at whatever clean water is within reach; others perform the rite at home, facing south with a large vessel of water standing in for the river — the manuals themselves allow this, holding that gangajal sprinkled with intention consecrates ordinary water. Kusha grass and black til are easy finds in any Indian city; where even they are missing, the tradition's own principle applies — the remembering is the rite. Elders often guide younger members through the names of three generations, with an unplanned benefit: probasi children end up learning their great-grandparents' names, some for the first time.

And chokkhu daan arrives by courier. Most probasi pujas worship an image made in Kumortuli or by artisans elsewhere, its eyes long since painted by a master hand in a workshop the community will never see; some reuse a fibreglass murti and mark Mahalaya instead with an unveiling or a fresh adornment of the Goddess's face. Either way, somewhere between the ancestors' water at dawn and the first agomoni song shared on the family group, the turn happens in a Pune apartment exactly as on a Kolkata ghat: the year pivots, the pujo stops being a plan and becomes a countdown, and the daughter is on her way to this home too.

## Words to know

*Mahalaya* (মহালয়া) — the new-moon day ending Pitri Paksha and opening Devi Paksha; usually explained as *maha* + *alaya*, "the great abode."

*Amavasya* (অমাবস্যা) — the new-moon day of the lunar calendar; *Mahalaya amavasya* or *Sarva Pitri Amavasya* is this day's pan-Indian name.

*Paksha* (পক্ষ) — a lunar fortnight; *Pitri Paksha* (পিতৃপক্ষ) is the dark fortnight of the ancestors, *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ) the bright fortnight of the Goddess that contains Durga Puja and runs to Kojagari Purnima.

*Tarpan* (তর্পণ) — the offering of water (with sesame) to satisfy the ancestors; from a root meaning "to satiate."

*Tilodaka* (তিলোদক) — sesame-water, the tarpan offering itself.

*Pitri* (পিতৃ) — the ancestors, the departed forefathers and foremothers.

*Shraddha* (শ্রাদ্ধ) — the fuller ceremony of rites for the dead, of which tarpan is a part.

*Kusha* (কুশ) — sacred grass worn as a ring and used as a seat in ancestral rites.

*Til* (তিল) — sesame; black sesame seeds are the essential ingredient of tarpan water.

*Gangajal* (গঙ্গাজল) — Ganga water, used to consecrate offerings.

*Chandipath* (চণ্ডীপাঠ) — recitation of the Chandi, i.e. the *Devi Mahatmya* / *Durga Saptashati*, the 700-verse scripture of the Goddess.

*Mahishasuramardini* (মহিষাসুরমর্দিনী) — "slayer of the buffalo demon," an epithet of Durga and the name of the 4 a.m. Mahalaya radio programme.

*Tej / tejas* (তেজ) — fiery radiance or energy; the combined tej of all the gods took form as the Devi.

*Dashabhuja* (দশভুজা) — "the ten-armed," Bengal's name for Durga bearing the gods' ten gifts.

*Agomoni* (আগমনী) — "songs of arrival"; the song genre in which Menoka longs for her daughter Uma to come home.

*Uma* (উমা) — the Bengali household name for Parvati/Durga as the beloved daughter; *Menoka* (মেনকা) is her mother, *Giriraj* (গিরিরাজ) her father, the Himalaya.

*Chokkhu daan* (চক্ষুদান) — "the gift of eyes"; the master artisan's painting of the idol's eyes, traditionally on Mahalaya, bringing the image to life.

*Kumortuli* (কুমোরটুলি) — the historic potters' quarter of north Kolkata whose workshops supply Durga images to pujas worldwide.

*Sankalpa* (সংকল্প) — the formal ritual resolution or vow that begins an observance.

*Ghot sthapana* (ঘট স্থাপনা) — the installing of a consecrated water-vessel, performed by some households at the fortnight's opening.
