## What it is

*Bijoya Dashami* (বিজয়া দশমী) is the tenth day of Durga Puja and its last: the day of the goddess's departure. The name holds both halves of the day's meaning. *Dashami* is simply "the tenth day"; *Bijoya* comes from *vijaya* (বিজয়া), victory — for this is the day the Devi's triumph over the demon Mahishasura is complete, and, in the pan-Indian telling, the day Rama felled Ravana. Across most of India the tenth day is Vijayadashami or Dussehra, a festival of unambiguous triumph, with effigies of Ravana going up in flames. In Bengal the same day wears a different face. Here Durga is *Uma* (উমা), the daughter of the land, home for four short days from her husband Shiva's house on Mount Kailash — and Dashami is the morning the daughter packs to leave. The victory is real, but the tears are real too. Bengal has never chosen between triumph and grief on this day, and does not intend to.

So Dashami is a day of paradoxical texture, and Bengalis navigate it with a sequence of tender rituals: the goddess is bid farewell first in a mirror; married women adorn her one last time as they would a departing bride, feeding her sweets and whispering invitations to return; vermilion is smeared on her face and then on each other's in the crimson riot of *sindoor khela* (সিঁদুর খেলা); and finally the image is carried in procession to the water and immersed — *bisorjon* (বিসর্জন) — the clay returning to the river it came from. Then, remarkably, grief pivots to warmth: from the moment the goddess is gone, the season of *Bijoya* greetings begins — embraces, touched feet, plates of homemade sweets, and the words *Shubho Bijoya* (শুভ বিজয়া) exchanged for weeks afterwards.

## When it happens

Dashami is the tenth lunar day of the bright fortnight of Ashwin, falling the day after [Nabami](10-nabami.md), usually in late September or October, and coinciding with Dussehra celebrated across India. The ritual work of the day happens early: the farewell puja and *darpan bisorjon* are performed in the morning, within the tithi's prescribed window, and pujo calendars are careful about this because in some years the tithi ends by mid-morning. Certain moments in the almanac are avoided for immersion, and each pujo's *nirghonto* (নির্ঘণ্ট) states its timings precisely. The aparajita rite described below is traditionally kept in the afternoon, the *aparahna* period.

The immersion itself may happen the same afternoon or evening — in big cities it is spread over several days by police schedule — and the social season it opens has a long tail: by custom, Bijoya greetings may be exchanged, and Bijoya sweets served, from Dashami until Kali Puja and Diwali a few weeks later, and many families extend the courtesy to the end of the Bengali year's festive stretch.

## The rituals, step by step

### The morning dashami puja and darpan bisorjon

The final worship is brief and unbearably tender. The *purohit* (পুরোহিত) performs the dashami puja — the last offerings of flowers, incense and food, closing the worship begun at bodhon — and then formally requests the goddess to depart, with mantras that ask her to return to her own abode and to come again, and that the worship, with all its inevitable imperfections, be accepted; many traditions add an explicit plea for forgiveness, the *kshama prarthana*, for whatever was done wrongly or left incomplete.

Then comes the rite children find most magical, a rite of quiet genius: *darpan bisorjon* (দর্পণ বিসর্জন), "immersion in the mirror". A vessel of water is set before the deity, and a small mirror, a *darpan*, is placed so that the goddess's face is caught in its reflection — the reflection appearing to float on the water in the vessel. It is this reflection that receives the farewell: the priest immerses the mirror-image in the water, and with that act the divine presence, the *prana* (প্রাণ) that was invoked into the clay at the start of the pujo, is understood to have left the image. There is a deep ritual logic here — throughout the pujo, whenever the Devi is "bathed", it is her reflection that is bathed, never the painted image itself — and the mirror-immersion completes that grammar: the goddess departs through her reflection before her image ever touches the river. Over the mirror the priest speaks the most quoted farewell in Bengali ritual — the verse that is also an invitation back:

> ॐ गच्छ गच्छ परं स्थानं स्वस्थानं परमेश्वरि ।
> संवत्सरव्यतीते तु पुनरागमनाय च ॥
>
> ওঁ গচ্ছ গচ্ছ পরং স্থানং স্বস্থানং পরমেশ্বরি ।
> সংবৎসরব্যতীতে তু পুনরাগমনায় চ ॥
>
> *oṃ gaccha gaccha paraṃ sthānaṃ svasthānaṃ parameśvari |*
> *saṃvatsara-vyatīte tu punar āgamanāya ca ||*

**In simple words:** "Go, go now to the highest place — to your own home, O Supreme Lady; and when a year has passed, come again." The doubled *gaccha gaccha* is tender, not brusque — the way a host finally releases a guest who must leave — and the last phrase, *punar āgamanāya ca*, "and for coming again", carries the whole weight of the day: the farewell is never goodbye; it is a booking. Old Bengali sources record variants that name the destination outright — "go where the god Maheshvara is" — which is exactly how Bengal imagines the departure: Uma going back to her husband's house on Kailash. This verse *is* the immersion-day chant "asche bochor abar hobe" in Sanskrit, a thousand years older. A companion verse releases the whole invited company — Ganesh, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Kartik, the nabapatrika, the attendant deities of every corner of the ritual space — asking all the hosts of gods to depart "taking my worship with them… and to come again". (Both texts, with their variants, are in [the visarjan and shanti mantra chapter](19-mantras-visarjan-and-shanti.md).)

In older times, when women did not go to the ghats for the immersion, the darpan bisorjon in the house courtyard was their farewell, and the moment retains that intimacy; some houses whisper to her at this point, as to a departing daughter, the message for Kailash — *tell them you were loved here*. What is carried to the water afterwards is, strictly speaking, no longer the goddess — only her portrait in clay. Knowing this is what lets Bengalis submerge the image they have worshipped for days: she is not drowned; she has already gone home.

### Aparajita puja

On Dashami, tradition prescribes the worship of *Aparajita* (অপরাজিতা) — "she who is undefeated" — honoured in the form of the aparajita creeper, the butterfly-pea vine (*Clitoria ternatea*) with its vivid blue flowers, which Bengalis consider especially dear to the Devi. The blue bloom is treated as a form of the unconquerable goddess of victory herself, worshipped with flowers and mantras after the dashami puja; in many traditions a strand of the creeper, blessed in the rite, is then tied on the arm or wrist as a *raksha* — a protective token carrying the day's victory onto the wearer's body for the year ahead. The invocation asks precisely that:

> हारेण तु विचित्रेण भास्वत्कनकमेखला ।
> अपराजिता भद्ररता करोतु विजयं मम ॥
>
> হারেণ তু বিচিত্রেণ ভাস্বৎকনকমেখলা ।
> অপরাজিতা ভদ্ররতা করোতু বিজয়ং মম ॥
>
> *hāreṇa tu vicitreṇa bhāsvat-kanaka-mekhalā |*
> *aparājitā bhadraratā karotu vijayaṃ mama ||*

**In simple words:** "May Aparajita — wearing her wondrous necklace and her belt of gleaming gold, she who delights in the good of her devotees — grant *me* victory." Spoken on the day of Durga's triumph, it asks that her victory be lent to the worshipper — the same logic by which kings once began their campaigns on Vijayadashami.

The rite is old — Bengal's medieval smriti digests prescribe aparajita worship on Dashami — and it ties the Bengali day to the pan-Indian one: Vijayadashami has for centuries been the day kings, and later ordinary households, sought the blessing of victory for ventures beginning anew. The same plant appears earlier in the pujo too: on Saptami the *nabapatrika*'s nine plants are bound with an aparajita creeper. Practice varies — many community pujas today perform the aparajita puja briefly or fold it into the dashami rites, and some omit it — but where it survives it is a lovely, quiet counterpoint to the day's louder emotions, the warrior tradition of the day preserved in a blue flower.

### Ghot bisorjon

Alongside the image, the pujo has all along had a second seat of the goddess: the *ghot* (ঘট), the consecrated water-filled pitcher established at the very start, its water standing for the Devi's presence through every day of worship. On Dashami it too is released — *ghot bisorjon* (ঘট বিসর্জন). The priest ritually "moves" or shifts the ghot, dismantling its consecration and closing the *kalpa*, the ritual undertaking; its water is not discarded but becomes blessing, sprinkled on the deity, the pandal and the devotees. In household pujas this shaken ghot is the definitive sign that the pujo is over; the coconut that crowned it is often kept or offered, and the mango-leaf sprig has done its work.

### Boron — the farewell adornment

Now the women of the community take over, and the pandal's mood turns into something between a wedding farewell and a funeral's tenderness. The *boron* (বরণ) — the ceremonial send-off — is performed by married women, traditionally the *eyo* or *sumangali* (সুমঙ্গলী), women whose husbands are living, dressed most often in the classic white saree with red border.

Each woman carries or shares a *barandala* (বরণডালা) — the farewell tray, usually a winnowing fan or a plate arranged with the auspicious things of a married woman's world: *sindoor* (সিঁদুর, vermilion), *paan* (পান, betel leaf), *mishti* (মিষ্টি, sweets), unhusked rice, *dhaan* (ধান), and blades of *durba* (দূর্বা) grass, with a small lamp and water. One by one the women climb to the deity. Each touches the auspicious items to the Devi's forehead, cheeks and feet; touches a paan leaf to her face — many will tell you they are wiping her tears, or shading her eyes for the journey; dabs sindoor at her parting and her feet, some houses touching it even to her feet's reflection; and feeds her sweets, pressing a sandesh or a naru gently to the painted lips — food for the road. The same courtesies are then paid to Lakshmi, Saraswati, and often to Ganesh's and Kartik's foreheads too — the whole family is leaving. And then, leaning close to the goddess's ear, each woman whispers the sentence on which the entire Bengali year turns: *asche bochor abar esho* (আসছে বছর আবার এসো) — "come again next year." It is said through smiles, and very often through tears, exactly as one speaks to a daughter leaving for her husband's house after a too-short visit. In her last hour in the pandal, fed and adorned and whispered to, the protima has never looked more like family. In many houses the women then complete the boron by ceremonially sharing the paan and sweets among themselves.

### Sindoor khela — the vermilion play

Out of the boron flows Dashami's most photographed ritual: *sindoor khela* (সিঁদুর খেলা), the "vermilion play". Having adorned the goddess with sindoor, the married women turn to one another — first a respectful touch of vermilion to the parting of the hair and the *shankha-pala-noa* bangles (শাঁখা, পলা, নোয়া — conch, coral and iron, the marks of a married Bengali woman), then to the forehead, and then all restraint dissolves: sindoor is smeared across cheeks and chins, sweets are pressed into mouths, embraces and laughter and ululation fill the pandal, and by the end every face is laughing through a joyful crimson dust-storm. The traditional meaning is a collective prayer of married women for the long life of their husbands and the wellbeing of their families — sindoor being the visible sign of marriage — and an auspicious send-off for the goddess herself, who departs as a vermilion-adorned married woman returning to her husband's home.

Its history should be told honestly. Popular accounts trace sindoor khela back some 400 years, to the era when Durga Puja took its modern shape; another common account places its origin around 200 years ago in the *zamindar* (জমিদার) mansions — the *bonedi bari* pujas — as a bonding ritual among the women of large households. The truth is that the exact origin is not known, and scholars caution against confident dating; what is certain is that it grew up inside the world of the aristocratic house pujas and spread outward with the community pujo until it became universal.

And one more honest thing. As traditionally practised, sindoor khela excluded as many women as it embraced: widows, unmarried women, and anyone outside the charmed circle of *sumangali* status stood at the edges and watched — an exclusion that could be quietly cruel in a culture where widows were once stripped of colour altogether. In recent years this has been challenged openly. Campaigns — most famously a Calcutta Times initiative — and a growing number of pujo committees have reframed sindoor khela as a celebration of womanhood itself, inviting widows, unmarried women, transgender women and others to play sindoor together, and many pandals, in Bengal and abroad, now practise it inclusively as a matter of course — an evolution most communities have simply absorbed. Others keep the older custom. Communities are still negotiating this, and a child watching should know both truths: the ritual's beauty, and the conversation about who gets to belong to it.

### The procession and bisorjon — the immersion

Then the goddess is lifted from the *bedi*, her altar — always a gasp in the crowd at that moment, after days of her immovability. Strong shoulders — traditionally an honour fiercely contested — carry the image out of the pandal, often swaying it three times or circling before departure, and the procession forms: the *dhak* (ঢাক) beating the slow, heavy immersion *bol*, the gong and conch, dancers, the *kola bou* carried alongside her, the truck-borne protima in the big processions swaying above the crowd, and the cry that punctuates the whole route — *bolo Durga mai-ki jai!* (বলো দুর্গা মাই কি জয়, "say victory to Mother Durga!") answered by the roar *jai!* — along with *asche bochor abar hobe* (আসছে বছর আবার হবে), "it will happen again next year," chanted like a promise the crowd is making to itself.

At the water's edge — the Hooghly ghats in Kolkata, where processions run past midnight; the Buriganga in Dhaka, which receives its thousands; a lake, tank or shoreline elsewhere — a final *aarti* (আরতি) is offered, lamps circling the Devi's face one last time as the dhak plays. In many customs the image is circled over the water — seven times, some houses hold — then turned to face it and immersed: *bisorjon*, also called *bhashan* (ভাসান), "the floating away". Made of river clay, straw and bamboo, the image is designed to dissolve; the goddess formed from the river returns to the river. Watchers stand on the ghat steps in silence or in tears while the painted face goes under, and many scoop a little water toward themselves as she goes.

Two practical notes belong here. First, the *kathamo* (কাঠামো) — the bamboo-and-straw skeleton of the image — is traditionally retrieved from the water after immersion; in many old family pujas the same kathamo is worshipped and reused year after year, and Kumortuli's artisans have always recycled frames, so retrieval is itself tradition, not merely cleanup. Second, ecology: unfired clay dissolves harmlessly, but chemical paints (historically lead-laden), synthetic decorations and the sheer scale of modern pujo have made mass immersion a real pollution problem for rivers and lakes. Responses now shape the ritual everywhere — rules requiring eco-friendly, lead-free paints and biodegradable ornament, municipal cranes lifting the frames out of the Hooghly within minutes, designated artificial immersion tanks, symbolic immersion where the river cannot take the load, and "wash and melt" immersions where the image is dissolved with water jets and the clay collected for reuse; even some venerable bonedi houses have moved to tank immersion. The theology accommodates all of this without strain — the goddess, remember, left in the mirror this morning; the river receives only the year's vessel, and caring for the river she returns to is entirely in her spirit.

### Shanti jal — the water of peace

When the party returns from the ghat, one rite remains — the quietest moment of the whole festival. Water brought back from the immersion site — or from the sanctified ghot — is the *shanti jal* (শান্তি জল), the water of peace; in the old custom a swallow of the immersion water, or a smear of its clay, was carried home as the festival's residue turned blessing. The purohit dips a mango leaf, bel leaf or flower into it and sprinkles it over the bowed heads of everyone gathered, chanting the great peace-chant of the Shukla Yajurveda — a verse that asks peace of the entire cosmos, element by element:

> ॐ द्यौः शान्तिरन्तरिक्षं शान्तिः पृथिवी शान्तिरापः शान्तिरोषधयः शान्तिः ।
> वनस्पतयः शान्तिर्विश्वेदेवाः शान्तिर्ब्रह्म शान्तिः सर्वं शान्तिः
> शान्तिरेव शान्तिः सा मा शान्तिरेधि ॥
> ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः ॥
>
> ওঁ দ্যৌঃ শান্তিরন্তরিক্ষং শান্তিঃ পৃথিবী শান্তিরাপঃ শান্তিরোষধয়ঃ শান্তিঃ ।
> বনস্পতয়ঃ শান্তির্বিশ্বেদেবাঃ শান্তির্ব্রহ্ম শান্তিঃ সর্বং শান্তিঃ
> শান্তিরেব শান্তিঃ সা মা শান্তিরেধি ॥
> ওঁ শান্তিঃ শান্তিঃ শান্তিঃ ॥
>
> *oṃ dyauḥ śāntir antarikṣaṃ śāntiḥ pṛthivī śāntir āpaḥ śāntir oṣadhayaḥ śāntiḥ |*
> *vanaspatayaḥ śāntir viśvedevāḥ śāntir brahma śāntiḥ sarvaṃ śāntiḥ*
> *śāntir eva śāntiḥ sā mā śāntir edhi ||*
> *oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ ||*

**In simple words:** "Peace be the sky; peace the air between; peace the earth, the waters, the plants and the great trees; peace all the gods; peace the Absolute; peace everything; peace, peace itself — and may that very peace come to me. Om, peace, peace, peace." It is less a request than a roll-call: every layer of the world is named and declared at peace, and then that universal peace is asked to settle on the people standing there with wet foreheads and an empty pandal behind them. The threefold closing is traditionally understood to quiet the three sources of disturbance — from oneself, from other beings, and from the gods and fate. (The full text, with the svasti benedictions that often accompany it, is in [the visarjan and shanti mantra chapter](19-mantras-visarjan-and-shanti.md).)

The sprinkle is a benediction closing the festival, distributing to every person a share of all the pujo's accumulated merit and calm. Those who could not go to the water make a point of being present for this. With the last drops, the pujo is over — and Bijoya begins.

### Bijoya — greetings, sweets, and the long goodbye

The moment the goddess is gone, Bengalis do a very Bengali thing: they turn to each other, and convert grief into courtesy. Juniors bend to touch the feet of elders in *pranam* (প্রণাম) and receive blessings; equals embrace in the *kolakuli* (কোলাকুলি) — the formal Bijoya embrace, chest to chest, traditionally alternating shoulders three times; and everyone says *Shubho Bijoya* — "an auspicious Bijoya to you." Quarrels are traditionally expected to end here; rivals reconcile, the embrace conveniently wordless — Bijoya is the year's amnesty. Then come the sweets, because no Bengali reconciliation, celebration or consolation is complete without them. Bijoya has its own canon, largely homemade: *narkel naru* (নারকেল নাড়ু), coconut balls rolled in jaggery or sugar; crisp diamond-shaped *nimki* (নিমকি); *kucho gaja* (কুচো গজা), sugar-glazed fried pastry bits; sandesh and chandrapuli where households are ambitious; often savoury *ghugni* alongside. For weeks afterwards, visiting anyone means being fed these, and visiting is nearly compulsory: Bijoya is the season for calling on relatives, mending strained relationships, and gathering the scattered clan.

Distance created its own tradition. For generations, probasi Bengalis and far-flung relatives exchanged Bijoya letters and postcards — formal, affectionate notes of pranam and blessing that could arrive weeks late and still count. The telephone call shortened the distance; today the WhatsApp Bijoya message, forwarded ornate graphics and all, has replaced the postcard almost entirely — grandparents video-called at the pandal gate, family groups flooded with *Shubho Bijoya* stickers by evening. The medium has changed completely; the obligation — reach out to everyone you belong to — has not changed at all.

Meanwhile the pandal comes down pole by pole, back toward the first bamboo *khunti* it began from; the dhak plays its emptying-out rhythm; and somewhere a child asks how many days until next year's Mahalaya. *Asche bochor abar hobe* — it will all happen again.

## Why it matters

Dashami carries two meanings at once, and refuses to choose. As Vijayadashami it is the festival of victory: good defeats evil, the Devi defeats Mahishasura, Rama defeats Ravana, and every household claims a share of that triumph for the year ahead. As Bijoya it is the festival of parting: the daughter goes back, the house empties, and the community practises — annually, together, in ritual form — the hardest human skill, which is letting go gracefully. The genius of the day is that it makes the two the same lesson: the immersion is not a defeat, and the departure is not an ending. Clay returns to river, daughter returns to Kailash, and the very chant of the procession — "it will happen again next year" — converts grief into a calendar. For children of the diaspora, Dashami is also the day the festival's social machinery shows itself plainly: the feet touched, the embraces given, the sweets carried to neighbours' doors are how a community re-knots itself for the year.

## The story behind it

Two great stories meet on this day. In the *Devi Mahatmya*, the tenth day is *Vijaya* — the victory accomplished: Mahishasura is dead, the gods are restored, and the goddess, her work done, withdraws, promising to return whenever she is remembered. In the Ramayana as Bengal tells it — through Krittibas — Rama's autumn worship of the Devi, the *akal bodhan*, won him her favour, and on the tenth day Ravana fell; north India burns the demon-king in effigy on Dussehra for exactly this reason, while Mysore parades the goddess Chamundeshwari, and much of India treats the day as auspicious for new beginnings, weapons, tools and journeys — the old rites of kings marching out after the rains.

But Bengal grafted onto this cosmic frame a domestic story, and it is the one that makes Dashami weep. Through the *agomoni* (আগমনী) and *bijoya* songs of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Durga became Uma, daughter of the mountain king *Giriraj* (গিরিরাজ) and his queen *Menoka* (মেনকা), married off to the ash-smeared ascetic Shiva of Kailash. The agomoni songs sing Menoka's longing and her insistence that Giriraj bring their daughter home; the pujo's four days are Uma's annual visit to her parents, with her children in tow; and the bijoya songs sing the other side — the mother begging the tenth day not to come, the farewell at the door. Dashami is agomoni's mirror image: the same love, reversed. Every ritual of the day — the boron's whispered "come again next year," the sweets pressed to her lips for the road, the women's tears at the ghat — makes sense only inside this story: Bengal is not disposing of an idol; it is seeing its daughter off to her in-laws, counting already the days until next Ashwin.

## In a probasi pujo

A *probasi* (প্রবাসী) pujo must manage Dashami's emotions and its logistics at once. The boron and sindoor khela translate intact — in Magarpatta as anywhere, Dashami morning fills with red-bordered sarees, barandalas assembled from local markets, and increasingly the inclusive spirit: many probasi pujas explicitly welcome every woman, married or not, to the sindoor khela, which itself needs only sindoor and courage for the dry-cleaning.

The immersion is the probasi pujo's hardest logistics and its tenderest improvisation. There is no Hooghly; there are municipal rules. Immersion happens at approved lakes, ghats or — very commonly in Pune, a city whose Ganeshotsav culture pioneered them — artificial immersion tanks, coordinated in advance with the housing society, the municipal corporation and the police: routes agreed, timings fixed, sound limits observed, volunteers marshalling the procession, and the clay and kathamo recovered afterwards per the rules. Where even that is not possible, communities improvise honourably — a rented tank, an inflatable pool, a symbolic bucket-immersion of the darpan; some immerse only the ghot and preserve the protima across several years, performing the visarjan through the mirror alone. All of it is liturgically sound, since the departure happened in the glass. Many probasi pujas take genuine pride in a fully eco-friendly bisorjon — natural-clay idols, herbal colours, tank immersion with the residue collected — finding that the constraint, embraced, feels less like compromise than like keeping faith with the river-goddess properly. And the emptied hall on Dashami night — folding chairs, swept petals, a lingering smell of dhuno — is where probasi communities annually rediscover why they will do it all again next year.

And because a working city cannot hold a month of house-to-house Bijoya visits, the diaspora concentrated the tradition into an institution: the *Bijoya Sammilani* (বিজয়া সম্মিলনী), a community get-together held in the weeks after Dashami — an evening of kolakuli and pranam, cultural performances, adda and a proper feast, where the whole Bijoya sweets circuit happens under one roof, and Bijoya greetings become the diaspora's great phone-call home. Our samiti holds its own Bijoya Sammelani each year a few weeks after the pujo; watch the events page for it, and come — it is the festival's true last chapter, the one where, having sent the goddess home, the community confirms that it, at least, is staying together.

## Words to know

- *Bijoya Dashami* (বিজয়া দশমী) — the tenth day; "the victorious tenth", day of the Devi's departure.
- *Bisorjon / bhashan* (বিসর্জন / ভাসান) — the immersion of the image in water; also the procession to it.
- *Darpan bisorjon* (দর্পণ বিসর্জন) — the "mirror immersion": the goddess's reflection is immersed first, releasing her presence from the image.
- *Punar agamanaya cha* (পুনরাগমনায় চ) — "and for coming again": the farewell verse's promise (full text in [the visarjan chapter](19-mantras-visarjan-and-shanti.md)).
- *Aparajita* (অপরাজিতা) — "the undefeated"; the blue butterfly-pea flower worshipped on Dashami as the goddess of victory, its creeper tied at the wrist.
- *Ghot bisorjon* (ঘট বিসর্জন) — the release of the consecrated pitcher that held the Devi's presence through the pujo.
- *Boron* (বরণ) — the farewell adornment of the goddess by married women.
- *Barandala* (বরণডালা) — the farewell tray of sindoor, paan, sweets, dhaan and durba.
- *Asche bochor abar esho* (আসছে বছর আবার এসো) — "come again next year", whispered to the departing goddess.
- *Sindoor khela* (সিঁদুর খেলা) — the vermilion play among women after the boron.
- *Shankha, pala, noa* (শাঁখা, পলা, নোয়া) — the conch, coral and iron bangles of a married Bengali woman.
- *Kathamo* (কাঠামো) — the bamboo-and-straw frame of the image, retrieved after immersion and often reused.
- *Shanti jal* (শান্তি জল) — the "water of peace" sprinkled by the priest after the immersion.
- *Pranam* (প্রণাম) — touching the feet of elders in respect; the junior's Bijoya greeting and its first duty.
- *Kolakuli* (কোলাকুলি) — the threefold Bijoya embrace exchanged between equals.
- *Shubho Bijoya* (শুভ বিজয়া) — the greeting of the season: "an auspicious Bijoya."
- *Naru, nimki, kucho gaja* (নাড়ু, নিমকি, কুচো গজা) — the classic homemade Bijoya sweets and savouries.
- *Agomoni* (আগমনী) — the songs of Uma's homecoming, whose mirror image is Dashami's farewell.
- *Bijoya Sammilani* (বিজয়া সম্মিলনী) — the post-Dashami community gathering; our samiti holds one every year.
