## What it is

*Maha Shashthi* (মহাষষ্ঠী) is the hinge of the festival — the day Durga Puja stops being something we are getting ready for and becomes something that is happening. For weeks the pandal has been rising, the idol travelling from the artisan's studio, the lists being ticked off. On Shashthi evening every one of those preparations converges on a single, quiet, astonishing idea: the goddess is asleep, and tonight we are going to wake her.

The ritual that defines the day is *bodhon* (বোধন) — literally "awakening." A belief running through the Puranas holds that the six months when the sun moves southward — *Dakshinayana* (দক্ষিণায়ন) — are the night of the gods, and that the deities sleep through it as we sleep through our own nights. Autumn falls squarely inside that divine night. To worship Durga in Ashwin, then, is to rouse her out of season — which is why this awakening bears its famous name, *akal bodhon* (অকালবোধন), the "untimely awakening." It is, in Bengali tradition, the very same untimely awakening that Rama performed on the eve of his war with Ravana, and every Bengali pujo — whether a three-hundred-year-old family courtyard in Kolkata or a community hall in Pune — re-enacts Rama's act of devotional impatience on this one evening. (Rama's full story, and the goddess's own, are told in the opening chapter — see [The Goddess and Her Story](01-the-goddess-and-her-story.md).)

There is something quietly radical in where the evening's worship takes place: not before the magnificent clay image but beside a tree. Before the goddess ever faces her protima, she is woken, invited, and lodged in a *bel* branch — a reminder that she lived in groves and on riverbanks long before she lived in pandals. The bel tree is, in a sense, the protima's wilder, older sister.

Shashthi is also the day the festival turns public. In many pujos the idol's face, kept veiled until now, is unveiled this evening — the *mukh unmochan* (মুখ উন্মোচন); the *dhak* (ঢাক) drummers arrive in earnest and find their full voice; the pandal formally opens for *darshan* (দর্শন) — the act of seeing, and being seen by, the deity. And threaded through all of it runs a gentler, older observance: Shashthi is a mothers' day of fasting and prayer for children, tying Durga Puja back to Bengal's ancient folk goddess of childhood, Ma Shashthi. One day, three meanings braided together: an awakening, a welcome, and a blessing.

## When it happens

Shashthi is the sixth *tithi* (তিথি — lunar day) of the bright fortnight of Ashwin — the fortnight called *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ), which opens at Mahalaya. On the Gregorian calendar it usually lands in late September or October, four days ahead of Vijaya Dashami.

The day has a clear arc. Morning brings *kalparambha* (কল্পারম্ভ), the formal vow that "starts the clock" of the puja — though, as we will see, different traditions count the start from different days, and some regard the principal kalpa as opening only on Saptami. The morning may also include a first, preliminary worship of Durga and Chandi at a water-filled vessel.

But the ritual heart of Maha Shashthi is the evening — traditionally the twilight hour called *godhuli* (গোধূলি), the "cow-dust hour," when the herds come home and the light turns gold. There is a simple, beautiful logic to holding bodhon then: you wake someone at the border of night. The sequence runs bodhon, then *amantran* (আমন্ত্রণ — the invitation), then *adhibas* (অধিবাস — the welcome-residence, or consecration), each flowing into the next at the foot of a bel tree or beside a cut bel branch. Priests consult the *panjika* (পঞ্জিকা — the Bengali almanac) afresh every year for the exact windows, because tithi timings drift; occasionally Shashthi and Saptami squeeze up against each other and the sequence has to be adjusted.

By the time adhibas is complete and the evening *arati* (আরতি) has been offered, the goddess is present, welcomed, and at rest — ready to be installed in the idol on Maha Saptami morning ([Saptami](08-saptami.md)).

## The rituals, step by step

### Kalparambha — the vow that starts the puja

*Kalparambha* (কল্পারম্ভ) means "the beginning of the kalpa" — a resolved programme of worship that declares exactly which days the puja will cover and by which scriptural procedure it will be performed. It is the moment when intention hardens into obligation.

At its centre stands the *sankalpa* (সংকল্প), the ritual vow. The *purohit* (পুরোহিত — priest) and the *yajaman* (যজমান — the puja's sponsor: the head of the household in a family pujo; in a community pujo, often the president or secretary vowing on everyone's behalf) sit down together. Facing north, holding sacred water in a small copper vessel called a *kosha* (কোশা), the yajaman repeats after the priest a declaration that names the place, the date by the Hindu calendar, the worshipper, and the promise itself: to perform the autumnal worship of Bhagavati Durga over the coming days according to the family's scriptural tradition. In the classic nineteenth-century account of Bengali practice, the vow cites the Brihannandikeshwara Purana as the authority for the autumn puja, and a second vow is commonly taken alongside — to recite the *Devi Mahatmya* (the Chandi) daily for the duration of the festival. A water-filled vessel is then installed, and Durga and Chandi are worshipped at it.

Here the traditions genuinely part ways, and it is worth understanding why. The old manuals describe several valid kalpas of different lengths. The longest opens on Pratipada, the very first day of Devi Paksha — the Bengali counterpart of north Indian Navaratri's *ghatasthapana* — and some houses that follow it keep a full fortnight of daily Chandi recitation. The most widespread modern practice is the kalpa that begins on Shashthi morning, which is why most panjikas and nearly all community pujos place kalparambha on this day. But certain lineages follow a Saptami-adi kalpa, counting the principal worship from Saptami — for them, Shashthi evening's bodhon–amantran–adhibas is the preliminary welcome, and the "real" puja opens at dawn the next day. And a few venerable family pujos run the other direction entirely, performing bodhon early on *Krishna Navami* — the ninth day of the dark fortnight *before* Mahalaya — and keeping the goddess "awake" with daily worship all the way to Shashthi. None of these is an error; they are different *paddhati* (পদ্ধতি — lineage ritual manuals). If you visit two pujos and are given two confident answers to "when does the puja really begin?", both answers are right.

### Ghot sthapana — establishing the sacred vessel

Everything that follows needs an anchor, and the anchor is the *ghot* (ঘট) — the sacred vessel, established in the rite of *ghot sthapana* (ঘটস্থাপনা). Look at the base of any Durga idol, or under the bel tree on Shashthi evening, and you will find it: a rounded earthen or brass pot that serves, for the length of the puja, as a seat of the goddess in its own right. The idol is for our eyes; the ghot is for the ritual — a surprising share of the priest's mantras and offerings are actually addressed to it.

Its assembly is exact. A low altar of earth is laid down, traditionally with grains scattered into it for abundance. The pot is set upon it and filled with holy water — Ganga water, or clean water into which Ganga water has been mixed. The pot's belly is then marked with auspicious designs in *sindoor* (সিঁদুর — vermilion): most commonly a little stick-figure motif or a dotted swastika, painted with vermilion mixed into ghee or oil. So adorned, it becomes the *mangal ghot* (মঙ্গল ঘট), the "auspicious vessel." Into its mouth goes the *aam pallab* (আম পল্লব), a sprig of five mango leaves — the count varies by paddhati. Resting on the leaves sits a *sasheesh daab* (সশীষ ডাব): a green coconut with its tuft of stalk still attached — "with its topknot," as the Bengali phrase has it. A new *gamchha* (গামছা) — Bengal's red-bordered cotton towel — or a piece of new cloth is tied around coconut and pot together. Betel leaves, *durba* grass, flowers, and a little rice complete the arrangement in most manuals.

The symbolism deserves telling aloud, especially to children, because it is one of Hinduism's loveliest compressed images: the pot is the earth, or the womb; the water is life; the leaves are nature in flourish; the coconut is the seed of consciousness; the cloth is the honour in which a guest is dressed. An entire cosmos, assembled on a handful of soil. The priest performs the sthapana with mantras, and from that moment the ghot must not be moved until the puja ends. Many pujos use two vessels across the day — one at the morning kalparambha and one beneath the bel tree for the evening rites; small pujos often manage everything with a single ghot.

### Bodhon — waking the goddess

Evening. Conch shells sound, the lamp is lit, and the priest takes his seat before the bel tree — or, where no tree grows, before a *bel daal* (বেল ডাল), a cut bel branch with good leaves and, if possible, a pair of its fruits, set upright beside the ghot. Why bel? The bilva is Shiva's tree, and the Kalika Purana itself directs that the goddess be awakened "on a branch of the bel tree and on a pair of its fruits." Bengali folk imagination adds its own tender gloss: Durga is the married daughter coming home, and Shiva, the son-in-law, travels with her but waits outside beneath his own tree — so the welcome begins at the bel, at the edge of the courtyard, exactly the way one walks out to the gate to receive a daughter.

*Bodhon* (বোধন) itself is the act of awakening. The priest worships the tree and the ghot with the standard sequence of offerings — water, flowers, sandal paste, incense, lamp, food — and then recites the bodhon mantras, calling on the goddess to rise from her sleep through the gods' night. The mantras name the untimeliness openly: the worshipper, standing where Rama once stood, asks the Devi to wake regardless — for the destruction of obstacles and the protection of the world. The most widely cited of these verses does something remarkable: rather than simply waking her, it cites precedent, reminding the goddess (and everyone listening) that this out-of-season summons has been made before, in the oldest emergency of all.

> ॐ ऐं रावणस्य वधार्थाय रामस्यानुग्रहाय च ।
> अकाले ब्रह्मणा बोधो देव्यास्त्वयि कृतः पुरा ॥
>
> ওঁ ঐং রাবণস্য বধার্থায় রামস্যানুগ্রহায় চ ।
> অকালে ব্রহ্মণা বোধো দেব্যাস্ত্বয়ি কৃতঃ পুরা ॥
>
> *oṃ aiṃ rāvaṇasya vadhārthāya rāmasyānugrahāya ca |*
> *akāle brahmaṇā bodho devyās tvayi kṛtaḥ purā ||*

**In simple words:** Om. Aim. Long ago, O Goddess, Brahma woke you at the wrong hour of the year — so that Ravana might be brought down, and so that Rama might receive your grace.

The seed-syllable *aiṃ* at the head is a *bija* (বীজ) of the goddess. Two lines, and inside them the whole charter of the autumn festival: the pujo of Ashwin is *akal* — out of season, inside the gods' sleeping half of the year — and it is legitimate precisely because it repeats the awakening performed before Rama's war. A well-attested variant recasts the second line into the first person and the present tense — "out of season, O gracious one, this awakening of you, Goddess, is made *by me*" — the priest stepping directly into Rama's role; the two readings mirror the two tellings of the story, one in which Brahma wakes the goddess on Rama's behalf and one in which Rama does so himself. Bengali sources also record a short performative formula spoken to the goddess in the tree itself — *aiṃ śrīvṛkṣe bodhayāmi tvāṃ yāvat pūjāṃ karomy aham* (ঐং শ্রীবৃক্ষে বোধয়ামি ত্বাং যাবৎ পূজাং করোম্যহম্): "in this shri-tree I awaken you, for as long as I perform your worship." Exact wording and sequence differ between paddhatis; the full texts, variants, and the companion verses of the evening are gathered in the mantra chapter ([Mantras: Bodhon and Nabapatrika](16-mantras-bodhon-and-nabapatrika.md)) — and the best commentary of all is simply to sit near the priest on Shashthi evening and listen.

In many pujos bodhon is also the moment of unveiling: the cloth over the idol's face — the drape that has kept her hidden through the days of setup — is lifted away, to a swell of dhak and conch and *ululudhwani* (উলুধ্বনি — the rolling tongue-trill made at auspicious moments). The goddess opens her eyes to her festival. Some pujos unveil earlier, some later; custom varies. The purohit leads and recites, the yajaman joins for the sankalpa portions, but bodhon is a community moment through and through — this is the evening the whole neighbourhood gathers, and everyone present is part of the welcome.

### Amantran — the invitation

Waking someone is not yet inviting them. *Amantran* (আমন্ত্রণ) — in some manuals *nimantran*, or the lovely compound *bilva nimantran*, "the invitation at the bel tree" — is the formal request that the newly awakened goddess come and accept worship. It follows bodhon in the same sitting, usually flowing so seamlessly out of it that onlookers cannot tell where one rite ends and the next begins.

The priest, flowers and sandal paste in hand, addresses the goddess as present in the bel branch and asks her, in effect: tomorrow, on Saptami, come and be worshipped; accept our hospitality; stay with us through the festival. An offering is made to the branch, and protective rites are performed alongside it — the manuals are candid that an awakening at an unusual hour calls for extra care, so the invitation carries prayers warding off obstacles from the days ahead; in some regional descriptions a thread of welcome is wound around the ghot as part of this protection, a drawn boundary of care around the goddess's lodging.

Children ask exactly the right question at this point: if Durga is everywhere, why does she need an invitation? Because the invitation is for us, not for her. Hospitality is how human beings make love visible. You do not merely leave the door open for an honoured guest; you go out, invite, escort, seat, adorn. Amantran is the going-out-to-invite.

### Adhibas — the welcome-residence

*Adhibas* (অধিবাস) is the most textured ritual of the evening and the hardest to translate. The word carries the sense of "dwelling," "abiding": having been woken and invited, the goddess is now ceremonially settled — consecrated into residence — in the bel branch and the ghot, where she rests overnight before entering the idol on Saptami morning. Bengalis know the word from another setting entirely: on the night before a Bengali wedding there is also an adhibas, when the bride is blessed and touched with auspicious things. Shashthi's adhibas is the same gesture performed at divine scale.

Its centrepiece is the *adhibas dala* (অধিবাস ডালা) — a large plate or winnowing tray arranged with a prescribed set of auspicious articles. One by one the priest touches each article to the bel branch (and, in many pujos, to the ghot and then to the idol's forehead), reciting the consecration formulas as he goes. Each touch settles one more strand of auspiciousness onto the goddess — a blessing pronounced through things rather than words.

The list of articles is old and specific, though — true to form — the paddhatis differ on the exact roster and count. A classical list, recorded in the tantric and Bengali ritual literature, runs: earth (*mati* — মাটি), sandal or perfume (*gandha* — গন্ধ), a stone (*shila* — শিলা), unhusked rice (*dhan* — ধান), *durba* grass (দূর্বা), flowers, fruit, curd (*doi* — দই), ghee (*ghrita* — ঘি), the *swastika* mark, vermilion (*sindoor* — সিঁদুর), a conch shell (*shankha* — শঙ্খ), collyrium (*kajal* — কাজল), *rochana* (রোচনা — a yellow sacred pigment), white mustard seed (*shwet sarisha* — শ্বেত সরিষা), gold, silver, copper, a lamp (*pradip* — প্রদীপ), and a mirror (*darpan/arshi* — দর্পণ/আরশি). Many Bengali lists go further and add turmeric (*halud* — হলুদ), the yak-tail fly-whisk (*chamor* — চামর), *alta* (আলতা — the red lac dye for a married woman's feet), a garland or *chandmala* (চাঁদমালা), iron, and — in some householder traditions — a fish-symbol. You will hear the set described as twenty articles at one pujo and twenty-six at another; both are following genuine manuals. What matters is the grammar of the list: earth and stone for steadfastness, grain and grass for abundance, curd and ghee for nourishment, the metals for permanence, sindoor and alta and mirror and kajal for the adornment of the married daughter, the lamp for light, the mustard seed for warding off evil, the chamor for royalty. It is everything a mother's household would gather to bless a bride.

Alongside the touching of the dala, adhibas carries its own sankalpa, its own offerings, and the sprinkling of scented water; when it is complete, a lamp is often left burning beside the branch and the ghot through the night. The goddess is now in residence. At the next dawn the bel branch joins the *nabapatrika* (নবপত্রিকা) — the bundle of nine plants, popularly the "kola bou" — to be bathed, dressed, and installed beside Ganesha; but that is the next chapter's story ([Saptami](08-saptami.md)).

### Bel boron — welcoming her like a bride

Interlaced with adhibas — and in many pujos honoured as its own named moment — comes the *boron* (বরণ) of the bel branch: *bel boron* (বেল বরণ). Boron is the quintessential Bengali gesture of ceremonial welcome, the same one offered to a bride at the threshold of her new home, and to the goddess herself on Dashami before immersion. A *boron dala* (বরণ ডালা) is prepared — typically a lamp, sindoor, betel leaf, sweets, durba, and paddy — and the branch that now carries the goddess is formally received: the dala's articles are shown and touched to her, the lamp is circled before her, the conches sound. Where Dashami's boron is the farewell of a departing daughter, Shashthi's is the welcome of an arriving one; the whole festival is bracketed between the two. In most community pujos the priest performs the Shashthi boron within the adhibas sequence, while married women lead the one on Dashami — though family customs differ, and in some houses the women's role begins already on Shashthi evening.

### Durga Shashthi — the mothers' fast

Running quietly beneath the temple-facing rituals is a domestic one — for many Bengali families the most personal observance of the entire festival. Shashthi, the sixth tithi of any month, belongs to *Ma Shashthi* (মা ষষ্ঠী), Bengal's folk goddess of children, fertility, and safe motherhood, whose vahana is a cat and whose worship is as old as anything in rural Bengal. The Devi Bhagavata tradition honours her as a portion of Prakriti herself, protectress of the young; village Bengal keeps a Shashthi observance in nearly every season — *Jamai Shashthi*, *Ashoka Shashthi*, *Aranya Shashthi*, *Sheetal Shashthi*, and more — each a mothers' *vrata* (ব্রত — votive observance) for the welfare of children and family.

Durga Puja's Shashthi folds this old tradition into the great festival as *Durga Shashthi* (দুর্গাষষ্ঠী). On this day mothers keep a fast for their children's long life, health, and protection. How strictly varies by family: some take nothing at all until the evening bodhon; some keep *niramish* (vegetarian) restraint; very commonly the rule is simply no rice or cooked grain — fruits, milk, sweets, and in some households luchi, are permitted. In the evening, after bodhon, the mothers offer their own worship — often under or facing the bel tree, with fruits, sweets, durba, and paddy — pray to the goddess (Durga and Ma Shashthi shading into each other, as folk and classical deities so often do in Bengal), and break the fast. No priest is needed; this is women's ritual knowledge, handed from mother to daughter. And it makes Shashthi the day the cosmic and the intimate meet: while the priest outside wakes the Mother of the universe, inside every home a mother has been fasting for her own child.

### Sandhya arati, dhak, and the opening of the pandal

With adhibas complete, Shashthi evening opens outward into festivity. The *sandhya arati* (সন্ধ্যা আরতি — evening lamp ceremony) is offered before the now-unveiled idol: the priest circles the *panchapradip* (পঞ্চপ্রদীপ — the five-flamed lamp), then incense, camphor flame, cloth, flower, and chamor in slow figures before the goddess, to the accelerating dhak, the clang of the *kanshor* (কাঁসর — brass gong), the conch, and ululudhwani. This is the first full arati of the festival — and for many people it is the moment Pujo arrives in the body: dhuno smoke, drummers leaning hard into their rhythm, the crowd pressed close.

The *dhakis* (ঢাকি) — hereditary drummers who travel to pujos with their great feather-plumed barrel drums — traditionally arrive by Shashthi, and from this evening on the dhak plays at every ritual junction through Dashami. The pandal formally opens for darshan; in Bengal's cities the crowds begin in earnest, and families step out in the first of their new Pujo clothes. The four busiest days of the Bengali year have begun.

## Why it matters

Shashthi matters first for what it says about presence. Hindu ritual takes seriously the idea that divinity does not simply sit inside an image; it must be invited, welcomed, and established — and, when the festival ends, released. Bodhon–amantran–adhibas is the front half of that arc (immersion is the back half), and it is what turns the idol from a magnificent sculpture into a worshipped presence. Everything done on Saptami, Ashtami, and Navami rests on what is done this evening.

It matters, second, for what it says about welcome. The whole evening is structured as hospitality toward a guest who is, specifically, a daughter — home for four days from her husband's house in the mountains. Children who grow up outside Bengal sometimes ask why the goddess of war is greeted with a mirror and kajal and alta; the answer is that on Shashthi she is not primarily the warrior. She is Uma — somebody's girl, arriving home. And the fact that her welcome begins under a tree, before she ever faces her own clay image, keeps the theology honest and domestic at once: a worship that starts in the open air remembers the goddess's older, wilder homes, and what Bengal does on Shashthi night is what any household does when the long-awaited daughter finally turns into the lane.

Third, akal bodhon carries a quietly radical teaching: the right time to call on the divine is when you need to. Rama could not wait for spring; Bengal, following him, decided once and for all that devotion outranks the calendar. A worship that begins by *waking* its deity admits openly that it is out of season — urgent, borrowed against spring, summoned for a battle rather than scheduled for a court — and goes ahead anyway.

And finally, the mothers' vrata grounds the whole spectacle. Durga Puja can look, from the outside, like pure pageantry; Shashthi's fast keeps its oldest purpose in plain sight — parents asking protection for their children, the same prayer that has been made under bel and banyan trees in Bengal for as long as anyone can trace.

## The story behind it

**The mythology of the untimely awakening.** In the Bengali telling — established by Krittibas Ojha's fifteenth-century Bengali Ramayana, the *Krittibasi Ramayan*, and absent from Valmiki's Sanskrit original — Rama stands on the shore facing Lanka, needing the blessing of the supreme Shakti before he can defeat Ravana. But it is autumn: Dakshinayana, the gods' night. The goddess is asleep, and her proper worship belongs to spring. So Rama performs the akal bodhon — he wakes her out of season and worships her with 108 blue lotuses, *neel padma* (নীলপদ্ম). In the best-loved episode, the goddess tests him by hiding one lotus; counting only 107, Rama — whose eyes had been praised as lotus-like — prepares to offer one of his own eyes to complete the number. The Devi appears, stays his hand, and grants him the boon of victory; Ravana falls, and the triumph is celebrated on Dashami. When Bengal worships Durga in Ashwin, it is deliberately repeating Rama's out-of-season awakening — which is why the autumn festival's first great ritual is a bodhon at all. (The fuller telling is in [The Goddess and Her Story](01-the-goddess-and-her-story.md).)

**The original spring worship, and the first worshipper.** Behind the "untimely" autumn rite stands the "timely" spring one, and its story comes from the frame-tale of the *Devi Mahatmya* (the Chandi), the chapters embedded in the Markandeya Purana. King *Suratha* (সুরথ), a good ruler defeated by his enemies and betrayed by his own ministers, wanders grieving into a forest. There he meets *Samadhi* (সমাধি), a merchant cast out by the very wife and sons he had enriched — and who, to his own bewilderment, still loves and worries about them. The two ask the forest sage *Medhas* (মেধস — Medha muni) why the mind clings to what has wounded it. The sage answers that all beings are held fast by Mahamaya, the great enchantment of the Goddess — who is also, when pleased, the giver of liberation — and he recounts to them her three great deeds: the slayings of Madhu and Kaitabha, of Mahishasura, and of Shumbha and Nishumbha. Those narrations are the Devi Mahatmya itself, the text chanted throughout Durga Puja. Instructed by Medhas, king and merchant worship the Devi on a riverbank with an earthen image; she appears and grants each his wish — Suratha his kingdom, Samadhi the knowledge that frees. Bengali tradition remembers Suratha as the first performer of Durga Puja — in spring. That spring worship survives to this day as *Basanti Puja* (বাসন্তী পূজা), held in Chaitra with the same rituals; it is the elder sister of the autumn festival, still kept in some family homes and temples, though the autumn *Sharadiya* (শারদীয়া) puja — Rama's season — long ago became the great public celebration of Bengal.

**The history.** As documented history, the grand autumn Durga Puja rose to prominence in early modern Bengal. Tradition credits the landholding houses — most famously Raja Kangshanarayan of Taherpur in the late sixteenth century — with the first lavish Sharadiya pujas in the familiar form; the zamindar houses of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (the Sabarna Roy Choudhury family of Barisha, Shobhabazar Rajbari after 1757) made the festival a spectacle of standing and hospitality, and some of these ancient family pujos still preserve the older, longer kalpas — bodhon on Krishna Navami, worship stretched across a fortnight. The democratic turn came with the *baroyari* (বারোয়ারি — "of twelve friends") puja, begun around 1790 by twelve Brahmins of Guptipara in Hooghly who, excluded from a household puja, pooled subscriptions and held their own; and then with the twentieth century's *sarbojanin* (সার্বজনীন — "of everyone") community puja, whose model — a committee, subscriptions, a pandal, a priest engaged for five days — is precisely what every probasi samiti, including ours, follows today. Through all these social transformations, the rituals of Shashthi evening have come down essentially intact: the ghot, the bel branch, the dala of auspicious things, the awakening at dusk.

## In a probasi pujo

In Bengal, Shashthi's rites unfold at leisure — kalparambha in the morning, the whole evening free for the long bodhon sequence. A probasi samiti rarely enjoys that luxury. Shashthi often falls on a working day, the hall may only become available from the evening, and the priest — frequently serving more than one community — has a schedule of his own. So the near-universal probasi solution is compression: kalparambha, ghot sthapana, bodhon, amantran, and adhibas are performed as one continuous sequence after office hours, the priest moving through the sankalpas and the dala in a single sitting of an hour or two. Some weekend-compressed pujos go further still, folding Shashthi's rites into Saptami morning, or holding a brief bodhon on whichever Friday evening the hall opens. The panjika's ideal windows are honoured in spirit where they cannot be in letter; every priest who serves expatriate communities knows the adjustments, and the paddhatis — with their multiple valid kalpas — turn out to be surprisingly accommodating of real life.

The bel tree is the other puzzle. A ritual built around waking the goddess at the base of a bilva assumes there is a bilva — no small assumption in a gated township in Pune. The standard solutions: a potted bel plant brought to the pandal for the evening, or a cut bel branch (bel daal) with good leaves — ideally with fruit — standing in for the tree, exactly as the manuals themselves permit. Our samiti does this the proper way: each year we source banana trees (for the nabapatrika that takes over on Saptami morning) and fresh bel — branches, leaves, and fruit — along with the rest of the Shashthi list: the earthen mangal ghot, aam pallab, the sasheesh daab green coconut, a new gamchha, and the makings of the adhibas dala from sindoor and kajal through to the chamor and the mirror. If you have ever wondered why the shopping spreadsheet carries a line reading "green coconut WITH stalk," Shashthi evening is the answer.

Two notes for families. First, this is the best evening of the whole festival to bring children close to the ritual: the objects are wonderfully tangible (a mirror! a whisk! a pot painted with a little vermilion figure!), and every item has a one-sentence story — let them watch the dala being touched to the branch, article by article. Second, mothers in our community do keep the Durga Shashthi fast in whatever form weekday life allows — many simply skip rice until evening, come to the pandal for bodhon, and break the fast after praying for their children. In Magarpatta as in Murshidabad, that quiet vrata, more than any decoration, is what makes Shashthi feel like the festival's true beginning: the community wakes the Mother together, and goes home having asked her to watch over the youngest among us.

## Words to know

- *tithi* (তিথি) — a lunar day, the unit the puja calendar runs on.
- *Devi Paksha* (দেবীপক্ষ) — the Goddess's fortnight, Mahalaya to Purnima.
- *bodhon* (বোধন) — "awakening"; the evening rite that rouses the goddess.
- *akal bodhon* (অকালবোধন) — the "untimely awakening," after Rama's out-of-season invocation.
- *Dakshinayana* (দক্ষিণায়ন) — the sun's southward half-year, the night of the gods.
- *kalparambha* (কল্পারম্ভ) — the vow-taking that begins the puja's programme (kalpa).
- *sankalpa* (সংকল্প) — the ritual vow naming worshipper, place, time, and procedure.
- *ghot* / *mangal ghot* (ঘট / মঙ্গল ঘট) — the vermilion-painted sacred vessel seating the goddess; installed in *ghot sthapana* (ঘটস্থাপনা).
- *aam pallab* (আম পল্লব) — the sprig of mango leaves in the ghot's mouth.
- *sasheesh daab* (সশীষ ডাব) — a green coconut with stalk attached, set atop the ghot.
- *gamchha* (গামছা) — the red-bordered Bengali cotton towel dressing the ghot.
- *bel / bilva* (বেল) — Shiva's wood-apple tree, seat of the Shashthi rites; a cut branch is a *bel daal* (বেল ডাল).
- *amantran* (আমন্ত্রণ) — the formal invitation; also called *bilva nimantran*.
- *adhibas* (অধিবাস) — the "welcome-residence" consecration; also the pre-wedding blessing ceremony.
- *adhibas dala* (অধিবাস ডালা) — the tray of auspicious articles touched to the goddess.
- *boron* (বরণ) — ceremonial welcome with a lamp-and-sindoor tray; for the bel branch on Shashthi, and in farewell on Dashami.
- *mukh unmochan* (মুখ উন্মোচন) — the unveiling of the protima's face, in many pujos done at bodhon.
- *chamor* (চামর) — the yak-tail fly-whisk waved before royalty and deities.
- *vrata* (ব্রত) — a votive observance, usually with fasting.
- *Ma Shashthi* (মা ষষ্ঠী) — Bengal's folk goddess of children and motherhood; her vahana is a cat.
- *paddhati* (পদ্ধতি) — a lineage's ritual manual; the reason equally correct pujos differ in details.
- *purohit* (পুরোহিত) — the priest; *yajaman* (যজমান) — the puja's sponsor.
- *panjika* (পঞ্জিকা) — the Bengali almanac of ritual timings.
- *dhak* (ঢাক) — Bengal's great barrel drum, played by hereditary *dhakis* (ঢাকি).
- *darshan* (দর্শন) — beholding the deity.
- *ululudhwani* (উলুধ্বনি) — the trilling sound made at auspicious moments.
- *Basanti Puja* (বাসন্তী পূজা) — the original springtime Durga Puja of King Suratha's story.
