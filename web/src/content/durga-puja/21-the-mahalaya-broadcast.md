## Is the "Mahalaya mantra" different from the Chandipath?

Every year someone asks, and the question deserves a straight answer: is what Birendra Krishna Bhadra recites at 4 a.m. on Mahalaya a separate scripture — a "Mahalaya mantra" — or is it the *chandipath* (চণ্ডীপাঠ)?

It is the Chandipath. There is no independent "Mahalaya scripture." The radio programme *Mahishasuramardini* (মহিষাসুরমর্দিনী) — "she who crushes the buffalo demon" — is a curated selection **from** the Devi Mahatmya (the Chandi, or Durga Saptashati) and its traditional liturgical companions: the prefatory *dhyana* (ধ্যান) verses, the Argala Stotram, the *Ya devi sarvabhuteshu* litany of chapter five, and the Rig-Vedic Devi Suktam that reciters traditionally append to a full Chandi reading. Around and between these ancient Sanskrit passages, the programme's creators wove three things of their own: a Bengali prose narration (*bhashya*, ভাষ্য) scripted by Bani Kumar, some two dozen newly composed Bengali devotional songs, and Pankaj Mullick's orchestral score. Everything Sanskrit in the broadcast is scripture you can find in a printed Chandi; everything Bengali in it was written for the radio in the 1930s.

Why, then, does it *feel* like its own text? Three reasons. First, selection: a full Chandipath of thirteen chapters takes hours; the programme compresses the whole arc — the gods' distress, the Goddess's creation from their combined radiance, the battle, the slaying, the hymns of praise — into about ninety minutes, keeping the most luminous verses and letting the narration carry the story between them. Second, Bhadra's voice: his recitation is not the level drone of a priest at a *ghat* but a dramatist's instrument, rising from murmur to storm, and generations have learned the verses with his exact cadences attached. Third, the montage itself: no temple recitation alternates scripture with orchestral interludes and songs. The result is that most Bengalis met the Chandi *through* this programme — so the frame became inseparable from the picture.

If you want the scripture itself — all thirteen chapters, the Kavacham, the complete litany — that is the chapter [Mantras: The Chandi](20-mantras-the-chandi.md). This chapter maps the broadcast onto it.

## The programme, movement by movement

Some fixed points first, all well documented. The programme runs one hour and twenty-nine minutes. It was performed live at the Calcutta station through its first decades; the definitive pre-recorded version — the one broadcast to this day and released on record — dates from 1966. Script: Bani Kumar. Music: Pankaj Kumar Mullick. Recitation and narration: Birendra Krishna Bhadra. It opens with the long-drawn sound of the conch, and the opening music is set in raga Malkosh — the deep pre-dawn raga — before the chorus enters.

The published recording is credited in nineteen movements. Bhadra's chandipath and Bengali bhashya are not separate tracks; they run between and beneath these pieces, so what the listener actually hears is a continuous braid: narration → song → Sanskrit → narration again. A note on the songs, and on why none of their words appear in this chapter: unlike the Sanskrit, which is ancient scripture, the songs are copyrighted musical works — lyrics by Bani Kumar and his collaborators, tunes by Pankaj Mullick, from the 1930s — and we do not reproduce their lines here. What can be said about them is how they function. They are *agomoni* songs in the classic Bengali mode: songs of the daughter's homecoming, addressed to Durga not as a distant deity but as Uma returning to her parents. "Bajlo Tomar Alor Benu" — "your flute of light has sounded" is the sense of its title — greets the autumn dawn as the first note of her arrival, and Supriti Ghosh's rendition of it has become, for most listeners, the single most recognizable song of Mahalaya morning. Dwijen Mukhopadhyay's "Jago Tumi Jago" — often called by its fuller refrain-title "Jago Durga, Jago Dashapraharanadharini," the address to the wielder of the ten weapons — is the broadcast's great summons, the human voice calling the Goddess awake exactly as the ritual of *bodhon* will do a week later. Manabendra Mukhopadhyay's "Tabo Achintya" carries a Sanskritic title that deliberately echoes the Argala Stotram's address to her as the one "whose form and deeds are beyond thought." The middle songs ride the battle's rising energy; Sumitra Sen's "Mago Tabo Bine" and Tarun Bandopadhyay's "He Chinmoyi" turn inward and devotional; and Utpala Sen's "Shanti Dile Bhori" lets the whole ninety minutes settle, after the war is won, into peace — followed by the conch again, and dawn.

The listener should also know what threads between these tracks. Bani Kumar's Bengali narration — itself a copyrighted literary work, so described here rather than quoted — opens the programme with its famous evocation of the autumn morning of Ashwin, an incipit so familiar that its first few words are, for Bengalis, as instantly identifying as any mantra. The narration returns throughout to carry the story: the gods humiliated by Mahishasura, the blazing energy pouring from Vishnu, Shiva and all the devas and condensing into the goddess, each god arming her, the lion's charge, the demon's shape-shifting, the final pinning and beheading. Between narration and song, Bhadra recites the Chandi itself — extended passages from the second and third chapters (the Goddess's creation and the slaying of Mahishasura), the chapter-five litany, the great hymns of chapters four and eleven — in full-throated Sanskrit. That alternation, not any single element, is the programme.

## The recited Sanskrit, in full where it is scripture

Everything below is ancient, public-domain scripture, given in Devanagari, in Bengali script, and in roman transliteration, with a plain-English sense of the meaning. These are the passages the broadcast is famous for.

### The opening: "Ya Chandi" — the Goddess named by her victories

After the conch, the chorus sings the verse that traditional Saptashati editions place as a *dhyana* before the Argala Stotram — a one-verse summary of the entire Chandi, naming the Goddess by each demon she destroyed:

> या चण्डी मधुकैटभादिदैत्यदलनी या माहिषोन्मूलिनी
> या धूम्रेक्षणचण्डमुण्डमथनी या रक्तबीजाशनी ।
> शक्तिः शुम्भनिशुम्भदैत्यदलनी या सिद्धिदात्री परा
> सा देवी नवकोटिमूर्तिसहिता मां पातु विश्वेश्वरी ॥
>
> যা চণ্ডী মধুকৈটভাদিদৈত্যদলনী যা মাহিষোন্মূলিনী
> যা ধূম্রেক্ষণচণ্ডমুণ্ডমথনী যা রক্তবীজাশনী।
> শক্তিঃ শুম্ভনিশুম্ভদৈত্যদলনী যা সিদ্ধিদাত্রী পরা
> সা দেবী নবকোটিমূর্তিসহিতা মাং পাতু বিশ্বেশ্বরী॥
>
> *yā caṇḍī madhu-kaiṭabhādi-daitya-dalanī yā māhiṣonmūlinī
> yā dhūmrekṣaṇa-caṇḍa-muṇḍa-mathanī yā raktabījāśanī |
> śaktiḥ śumbha-niśumbha-daitya-dalanī yā siddhi-dātrī parā
> sā devī nava-koṭi-mūrti-sahitā māṁ pātu viśveśvarī ||*

**In simple words:** She who is Chandi, crusher of Madhu, Kaitabha and the demon hosts; she who uprooted Mahisha; she who ground down Dhumralochana, Chanda and Munda; she who devoured Raktabija; the Power that broke Shumbha and Nishumbha; she who grants attainment, the Supreme — may that Goddess, sovereign of the universe, present in her nine crore forms, protect me. It is a table of contents for everything the next ninety minutes will narrate — which is exactly why the programme's makers put it first.

### "Simhastha Shashishekhara" — Durga on her lion

The second choral piece is a classical dhyana of Durga used in her formal worship:

> सिंहस्था शशिशेखरा मरकतप्रख्यैश्चतुर्भिर्भुजैः
> शङ्खं चक्रधनुःशरांश्च दधती नेत्रैस्त्रिभिः शोभिता ।
> आमुक्ताङ्गदहारकङ्कणरणत्काञ्चीरणन्नूपुरा
> दुर्गा दुर्गतिहारिणी भवतु नो रत्नोल्लसत्कुण्डला ॥
>
> সিংহস্থা শশিশেখরা মরকতপ্রখ্যৈশ্চতুর্ভির্ভুজৈঃ
> শঙ্খং চক্রধনুঃশরাংশ্চ দধতী নেত্রৈস্ত্রিভিঃ শোভিতা।
> আমুক্তাঙ্গদহারকঙ্কণরণৎকাঞ্চীরণন্নূপুরা
> দুর্গা দুর্গতিহারিণী ভবতু নো রত্নোল্লসৎকুণ্ডলা॥
>
> *siṁhasthā śaśi-śekharā marakata-prakhyaiś caturbhir bhujaiḥ
> śaṅkhaṁ cakra-dhanuḥ-śarāṁś ca dadhatī netrais tribhiḥ śobhitā |
> āmuktāṅgada-hāra-kaṅkaṇa-raṇat-kāñcī-raṇan-nūpurā
> durgā durgati-hāriṇī bhavatu no ratnollasat-kuṇḍalā ||*

**In simple words:** Mounted on the lion, moon-crested, gleaming like emerald, holding in four arms the conch, discus, bow and arrows, resplendent with three eyes; adorned with armlets, necklaces and bangles, her girdle and anklets chiming, her earrings flashing with gems — may Durga, the remover of misfortune, be gracious to us. (That epithet *durgati-harini* — "remover of misfortune" — is the phrase All India Radio reached for in 1976 when it titled its ill-fated replacement programme; the story of that episode is told in the [Mahalaya chapter](04-mahalaya.md).)

### "Ya devi sarvabhuteshu" — the litany

The broadcast's emotional centre of gravity is Bhadra's recitation of the chapter-five litany, the *Aparajita Stuti* — the hymn the gods sing to the unconquerable Goddess, naming her as the one power standing in all beings under different names. The frame verse:

> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु विष्णुमायेति शब्दिता ।
> नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमो नमः ॥
>
> যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু বিষ্ণুমায়েতি শব্দিতা।
> নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমো নমঃ॥
>
> *yā devī sarva-bhūteṣu viṣṇu-māyeti śabditā |
> namas tasyai namas tasyai namas tasyai namo namaḥ ||*

**In simple words:** To the Goddess who abides in all beings, spoken of as the māyā of Vishnu — salutation to her, salutation to her, salutation to her, again and again.

The litany then repeats this structure through a long roll of her forms; the broadcast carries the sequence substantially as the Chandi gives it. Among the forms every listener comes to know:

> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु **शक्तिरूपेण** संस्थिता … · যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু **শক্তিরূপেণ** সংস্থিতা … — *who abides in all beings **as power** (śakti)*
>
> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु **बुद्धिरूपेण** संस्थिता … · যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু **বুদ্ধিরূপেণ** সংস্থিতা … — *…**as intelligence** (buddhi)*
>
> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु **मातृरूपेण** संस्थिता … · যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু **মাতৃরূপেণ** সংস্থিতা … — *…**as mother** (mātṛ)*
>
> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु **शान्तिरूपेण** संस्थिता … · যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু **শান্তিরূপেণ** সংস্থিতা … — *…**as peace** (śānti)*

— each followed by the same triple *namas tasyai*. The full litany, with all its forms — consciousness, sleep, hunger, shadow, thirst, forbearance, modesty, faith, loveliness, fortune, memory, compassion, contentment, error and the rest — is given complete in [Mantras: The Chandi](20-mantras-the-chandi.md); we do not duplicate it here. What matters for Mahalaya is the delivery: Bhadra takes the litany as a rising tide, each *namas tasyai namas tasyai* louder and more incantatory than the last, and for most Bengalis this crescendo, more than any single verse, *is* the sound of Devi Paksha opening.

### The Devi Suktam — "Aham rudrebhir vasubhish charami"

Midway through the programme the chorus sings, and the recitation takes up, the Devi Suktam — Rig Veda 10.125, the hymn of the sage Vak, daughter of Ambhrina, in which the divine feminine speaks in the first person as the power upholding gods, worlds and speech itself. Tradition appends this hymn to a full Chandipath, which is how it enters the broadcast. It is eight verses, and since the broadcast made its opening words famous in every Bengali home, here it is substantially in full:

> अहं रुद्रेभिर्वसुभिश्चराम्यहमादित्यैरुत विश्वदेवैः ।
> अहं मित्रावरुणोभा बिभर्म्यहमिन्द्राग्नी अहमश्विनोभा ॥ १ ॥
>
> অহং রুদ্রেভির্বসুভিশ্চরাম্যহমাদিত্যৈরুত বিশ্বদেবৈঃ।
> অহং মিত্রাবরুণোভা বিভর্ম্যহমিন্দ্রাগ্নী অহমশ্বিনোভা॥ ১॥
>
> *ahaṁ rudrebhir vasubhiś carāmy aham ādityair uta viśvadevaiḥ |
> ahaṁ mitrāvaruṇobhā bibharmy aham indrāgnī aham aśvinobhā || 1 ||*

**In simple words:** I move with the Rudras and the Vasus, with the Adityas and all the gods; I carry both Mitra and Varuna, I carry Indra and Agni, I carry the two Ashvins.

> अहं सोममाहनसं बिभर्म्यहं त्वष्टारमुत पूषणं भगम् ।
> अहं दधामि द्रविणं हविष्मते सुप्राव्ये यजमानाय सुन्वते ॥ २ ॥
>
> অহং সোমমাহনসং বিভর্ম্যহং ত্বষ্টারমুত পূষণং ভগম্।
> অহং দধামি দ্রবিণং হবিষ্মতে সুপ্রাব্যে যজমানায় সুন্বতে॥ ২॥
>
> *ahaṁ somam āhanasaṁ bibharmy ahaṁ tvaṣṭāram uta pūṣaṇaṁ bhagam |
> ahaṁ dadhāmi draviṇaṁ haviṣmate suprāvye yajamānāya sunvate || 2 ||*

**In simple words:** I bear the swelling soma; I bear Tvashta, Pushan, Bhaga; it is I who bestow wealth on the sacrificer who presses the soma and pours the offering.

> अहं राष्ट्री संगमनी वसूनां चिकितुषी प्रथमा यज्ञियानाम् ।
> तां मा देवा व्यदधुः पुरुत्रा भूरिस्थात्रां भूर्यावेशयन्तीम् ॥ ३ ॥
>
> অহং রাষ্ট্রী সংগমনী বসূনাং চিকিতুষী প্রথমা যজ্ঞিয়ানাম্।
> তাং মা দেবা ব্যদধুঃ পুরুত্রা ভূরিস্থাত্রাং ভূর্যাবেশয়ন্তীম্॥ ৩॥
>
> *ahaṁ rāṣṭrī saṁgamanī vasūnāṁ cikituṣī prathamā yajñiyānām |
> tāṁ mā devā vy adadhuḥ purutrā bhūri-sthātrāṁ bhūry-āveśayantīm || 3 ||*

**In simple words:** I am the sovereign, the gatherer of treasures, the knowing one, first among those worthy of worship; the gods have set me in many places, dwelling in many things, entering into many things.

> मया सो अन्नमत्ति यो विपश्यति यः प्राणिति य ईं शृणोत्युक्तम् ।
> अमन्तवो मां त उप क्षियन्ति श्रुधि श्रुत श्रद्धिवं ते वदामि ॥ ४ ॥
>
> ময়া সো অন্নমত্তি যো বিপশ্যতি যঃ প্রাণিতি য ঈং শৃণোত্যুক্তম্।
> অমন্তবো মাং ত উপ ক্ষিয়ন্তি শ্রুধি শ্রুত শ্রদ্ধিবং তে বদামি॥ ৪॥
>
> *mayā so annam atti yo vipaśyati yaḥ prāṇiti ya īṁ śṛṇoty uktam |
> amantavo māṁ ta upa kṣiyanti śrudhi śruta śraddhivaṁ te vadāmi || 4 ||*

**In simple words:** Through me alone does anyone eat, see, breathe, or hear a spoken word; not knowing it, they yet live in me. Listen, you who are able to hear: what I tell you is worthy of trust.

> अहमेव स्वयमिदं वदामि जुष्टं देवेभिरुत मानुषेभिः ।
> यं कामये तंतमुग्रं कृणोमि तं ब्रह्माणं तमृषिं तं सुमेधाम् ॥ ५ ॥
>
> অহমেব স্বয়মিদং বদামি জুষ্টং দেবেভিরুত মানুষেভিঃ।
> যং কাময়ে তংতমুগ্রং কৃণোমি তং ব্রহ্মাণং তমৃষিং তং সুমেধাম্॥ ৫॥
>
> *aham eva svayam idaṁ vadāmi juṣṭaṁ devebhir uta mānuṣebhiḥ |
> yaṁ kāmaye taṁ-tam ugraṁ kṛṇomi taṁ brahmāṇaṁ tam ṛṣiṁ taṁ sumedhām || 5 ||*

**In simple words:** I myself declare this word, welcomed by gods and by mortals: whomever I favour, that one I make mighty — a knower of the sacred, a seer, a sage.

> अहं रुद्राय धनुरा तनोमि ब्रह्मद्विषे शरवे हन्तवा उ ।
> अहं जनाय समदं कृणोम्यहं द्यावापृथिवी आ विवेश ॥ ६ ॥
>
> অহং রুদ্রায় ধনুরা তনোমি ব্রহ্মদ্বিষে শরবে হন্তবা উ।
> অহং জনায় সমদং কৃণোম্যহং দ্যাবাপৃথিবী আ বিবেশ॥ ৬॥
>
> *ahaṁ rudrāya dhanur ā tanomi brahma-dviṣe śarave hantavā u |
> ahaṁ janāya samadaṁ kṛṇomy ahaṁ dyāvā-pṛthivī ā viveśa || 6 ||*

**In simple words:** It is I who stretch the bow for Rudra, so that his arrow may strike down the enemy of the sacred; I stir the battle-strength of the people; I have entered heaven and earth.

> अहं सुवे पितरमस्य मूर्धन्मम योनिरप्स्वन्तः समुद्रे ।
> ततो वि तिष्ठे भुवनानु विश्वोतामूं द्यां वर्ष्मणोप स्पृशामि ॥ ७ ॥
>
> অহং সুবে পিতরমস্য মূর্ধন্মম যোনিরপ্স্বন্তঃ সমুদ্রে।
> ততো বি তিষ্ঠে ভুবনানু বিশ্বোতামূং দ্যাং বর্ষ্মণোপ স্পৃশামি॥ ৭॥
>
> *ahaṁ suve pitaram asya mūrdhan mama yonir apsv antaḥ samudre |
> tato vi tiṣṭhe bhuvanānu viśvotāmūṁ dyāṁ varṣmaṇopa spṛśāmi || 7 ||*

**In simple words:** I give birth to the father at the summit of this world; my womb is in the waters, within the ocean; from there I spread through all the worlds, and with my height I touch yonder heaven.

> अहमेव वात इव प्र वाम्यारभमाणा भुवनानि विश्वा ।
> परो दिवा पर एना पृथिव्यैतावती महिना सं बभूव ॥ ८ ॥
>
> অহমেব বাত ইব প্র বাম্যারভমাণা ভুবনানি বিশ্বা।
> পরো দিবা পর এনা পৃথিব্যৈতাবতী মহিনা সং বভূব॥ ৮॥
>
> *aham eva vāta iva pra vāmy ārabhamāṇā bhuvanāni viśvā |
> paro divā para enā pṛthivyaitāvatī mahinā saṁ babhūva || 8 ||*

**In simple words:** Like the wind I blow forth, taking hold of all the worlds; beyond heaven, beyond this earth — so vast have I become in my greatness.

Set beside the *Ya devi* litany, this hymn does something structurally beautiful in the broadcast: in the litany, the gods address the Goddess in the third person; in the Devi Suktam, she answers in the first. The programme lets the community hear both directions of the conversation.

### "Jayanti Mangala Kali" and "Rupam Dehi" — the Argala Stotram in the broadcast

Two of the choral pieces come from the Argala Stotram, the "bolt-hymn" traditionally recited before the Chandi. The salutation verse the chorus sings:

> जयन्ती मङ्गला काली भद्रकाली कपालिनी ।
> दुर्गा शिवा क्षमा धात्री स्वाहा स्वधा नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> জয়ন্তী মঙ্গলা কালী ভদ্রকালী কপালিনী।
> দুর্গা শিবা ক্ষমা ধাত্রী স্বাহা স্বধা নমোঽস্তু তে॥
>
> *jayantī maṅgalā kālī bhadrakālī kapālinī |
> durgā śivā kṣamā dhātrī svāhā svadhā namo'stu te ||*

**In simple words:** Ever-victorious one, auspicious one, Kali, gracious Bhadrakali, bearer of the skull; Durga, benign one, forbearance, sustainer; Svaha and Svadha, the very calls of offering — homage to you. In Bengali households this single verse leads countless daily pranams; hearing a full chorus deliver it at 4 a.m. is one of the broadcast's quiet thrills.

And near the end, Pankaj Mullick himself leads the chorus in a piece built on the Argala Stotram's famous refrain — the line that hymn repeats, verse after verse, as its one insistent prayer:

> रूपं देहि जयं देहि यशो देहि द्विषो जहि ॥
>
> রূপং দেহি জয়ং দেহি যশো দেহি দ্বিষো জহি॥
>
> *rūpaṁ dehi jayaṁ dehi yaśo dehi dviṣo jahi ||*

**In simple words:** Give beauty, give victory, give glory — and destroy all hostility. After ninety minutes of battle narrative, the broadcast closes its Sanskrit on this: not triumphalism but a plea that the victory be shared with the listener.

### "Jatajuta-samayuktam" — the dhyana of the ten-armed goddess

The eleventh movement is choral singing of the great dhyana used across Bengal to visualize the ten-armed Mahishasuramardini — the same verse family a purohit recites before our own murti. Its opening verse:

> जटाजूटसमायुक्तामर्धेन्दुकृतशेखराम् ।
> लोचनत्रयसंयुक्तां पूर्णेन्दुसदृशाननाम् ॥
>
> জটাজূটসমাযুক্তামর্ধেন্দুকৃতশেখরাম্।
> লোচনত্রয়সংযুক্তাং পূর্ণেন্দুসদৃশাননাম্॥
>
> *jaṭājūṭa-samāyuktām ardhendu-kṛta-śekharām |
> locana-traya-saṁyuktāṁ pūrṇendu-sadṛśānanām ||*

**In simple words:** Her matted locks bound high, the half-moon set as her crown; endowed with three eyes, her face like the full moon. The dhyana continues through her golden complexion, her ten arms and their weapons, and the demon under her foot — the verbal blueprint of every Bengali protima.

### "Jaya jaya he Mahishasura-mardini" — and the honest word about "Ayi giri-nandini"

When the demon finally falls, the chorus erupts into the broadcast's title-line — the refrain of the medieval *Mahishasura Mardini Stotra*, the galloping hymn attributed to the Shankara tradition:

> जय जय हे महिषासुरमर्दिनि रम्यकपर्दिनि शैलसुते ॥
>
> জয় জয় হে মহিষাসুরমর্দিনি রম্যকপর্দিনি শৈলসুতে॥
>
> *jaya jaya he mahiṣāsura-mardini ramya-kapardini śaila-sute ||*

**In simple words:** Victory, victory to you, crusher of Mahishasura, of the lovely braided hair, daughter of the mountain!

An honest clarification, because the association confuses many listeners: the full stotra — the one that opens *ayi giri-nandini nandita-medini…* ("O daughter of the mountain, delight of the earth…") and races through some twenty verses in that rolling metre, familiar from countless South Indian and film renditions — is **not** recited in the AIR programme. Transcriptions of the broadcast confirm that what the chorus sings is the *jaya jaya he* refrain, set to Pankaj Mullick's own melody, not the complete hymn. The stotra and the programme share a title and a refrain, and each has amplified the other's fame, but if you sit through all ninety minutes waiting for "Ayi giri-nandini," it does not come.

## Voices and music

**Birendra Krishna Bhadra** (1905–1991) was not a priest but a staff artist of the Calcutta station — playwright, actor, announcer — and his chandipath is a dramatist's chandipath: Sanskrit delivered with the pacing, dynamics and emotional commitment of theatre, then folded into his own Bengali narration so seamlessly that many listeners cannot say where scripture ends and bhashya begins. Accounts of the recording sessions describe him arriving before dawn, bathed and dressed in traditional silk as if for a puja, insisting the performance was an offering rather than a broadcast; the AIR studio was set with incense and flowers. The most repeated anecdote — told by colleagues and retold in nearly every profile — is that at the climactic invocation calling the Mother awake, Bhadra genuinely broke down and wept at the microphone, and that the emotion audible in the recording is not acting. His famous reply to those who objected that a non-Brahmin should not recite the Chandi — that he was not merely reciting but worshipping — and the 1976 attempt to replace him with Uttam Kumar are told in full in the [Mahalaya chapter](04-mahalaya.md).

**Pankaj Kumar Mullick** (1905–1978), one of the founding figures of modern Bengali music, built the score as a bridge between worlds: raga-grounded melody (the Malkosh opening; classical settings under the Sanskrit choruses) carried by a hybrid orchestra of Indian and Western instruments — the recording credits name Bibekendra Deb Roy on violin, Aloke Nath Dey on flute and Kamal Mallick on sarod, among others. He composed the songs, trained and led the chorus, and sings on the "Rupam Dehi" movement himself. All India Radio's own retrospective preserves a detail worth repeating: several of the studio orchestra's musicians were Urdu-speaking and followed not the Bengali text but Bhadra's intonation — evidence of how completely the recitation itself conducted the room.

**Bani Kumar** (Baidyanath Bhattacharya) wrote the script — the Bengali bhashya and the song lyrics — and designed the programme's deepest idea: that a Puranic battle could be structured as radio, with scripture as its spine and song as its breath. The programme evolved through the early 1930s from a precursor called *Basanteshwari* into the Mahishasuramardini format; that disputed early history, too, is in the [Mahalaya chapter](04-mahalaya.md).

## Listening as ritual

For a probasi household the broadcast is the one "mantra" of the festival that requires no purohit: the ritual is simply to be awake, together, in the dark, when it begins. The classic discipline — alarm before four, lights low, radio on, no one talking over Bhadra — has transferred intact to YouTube and streaming apps, where the same 1966 recording plays to the diaspora at 4 a.m. in whatever time zone the family keeps. Some households bathe first and treat the ninety minutes as worship; some follow the Sanskrit with a printed Chandi open on a lap; children absorb the cadences years before they learn what the words mean.

If you are listening with this chapter in hand, three waves are worth waiting for: the opening conch dissolving into "Ya Chandi"; the litany's crescendo of *namas tasyai namas tasyai*; and the moment after the demon falls, when the chorus breaks into *jaya jaya he* and you understand why a radio programme has functioned, for nine decades, as an entire community's call to festival. The full story of the day itself — tarpan for the ancestors, the disputed greeting, the history and controversies of the broadcast — is in the [Mahalaya chapter](04-mahalaya.md); the complete scripture behind it is in [Mantras: The Chandi](20-mantras-the-chandi.md).
