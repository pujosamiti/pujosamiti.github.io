# Bengali Durga Puja

Durga Puja — *pujo*, as every Bengali says it — is the largest thing Bengalis
do together, on both sides of the border that divides Bengal. It is celebrated
with equal love in Kolkata's lanes and Dhaka's mandaps, in village courtyards
of Hooghly and Rajshahi, and in rented halls and society clubhouses wherever
Bengalis have settled across the world — including ours in Magarpatta City,
Pune. It is also the easiest festival to celebrate without ever quite knowing
*why*. Why is a bamboo pole worshipped months in advance? Why is a banana
plant wrapped in a saree and bathed before dawn? Why do the drums fall silent
for exactly forty-eight minutes spanning Ashtami and Nabami? Why does the
festival end not at the river, but days later, on the brightest full moon of
the year?

This book answers those questions properly. Not a pamphlet — an encyclopedia:
one chapter per day or theme, each going deep into what happens, step by step;
what every ritual means; and where it came from — the mythology that frames it
and the documented history that shaped it. It is written for all of us: for
children growing up far from Bengal for whom the pujo is the year's Bengali
immersion, for neighbours from every part of India who join our pandal and
deserve better answers than "tradition", and for ourselves, because performing
a ritual and understanding it are two different kinds of joy.

And because a pujo is finally made of words as much as of flowers, the last
part of the book is a mantra shelf: the mantras actually used in a Bengali
Durga Puja, each given in Devanagari script, then Bengali script, then roman
transliteration, and then a plain-English sense of what it says.

## Three threads to read the whole festival by

**The daughter comes home.** Beneath the theology, Bengal reads the pujo as a
family story: Uma, married to the ascetic Shiva on far Kailash, comes home to
her parents for four days a year. The *agomoni* songs of the season are a
mother waiting; Dashami's tears are a daughter leaving. Every family that has
ever sent a daughter away — and every family living far from home — knows
exactly what this feels like.

**The out-of-season awakening.** The "natural" season for worshipping the
Devi is spring (Basanti Puja still keeps it). Autumn worship required a
special waking — *akal bodhon* — first performed, the Bengali Ramayana of
Krittibas says, by Rama himself before his battle with Ravana. That is why
Shashthi's rituals are an awakening, and why the autumn festival carries a
note of urgency: this is worship summoned for a fight.

**The harvest under the theology.** The nine plants of the *nabapatrika*, the
paddy and durba grass of every blessing, the full-moon Lakshmi of the granary
— beneath the Sanskrit liturgy runs an older agrarian festival thanking the
earth at harvest. The pujo has always held both readings at once.

## How the mantras are printed

Wherever a mantra appears in this book, it follows one fixed format:

> देवनागरी — the Devanagari text
>
> বাংলা — the same text in Bengali script
>
> *roman transliteration, with long vowels marked*

**In simple words:** a plain-English rendering of the meaning — not scholarly
translation, just what the words are saying.

---

*A note on accuracy: rituals differ between family traditions (paddhati),
between regions of Bengal, between a household pujo and a community
(sarbojanin) one — and between what one purohit and another was taught. These
chapters describe the broadly common Bengali practice and flag major
variations honestly. Where your own priest or family does it differently,
your tradition wins. Corrections from readers who know better are not just
welcome; they are the point.*
