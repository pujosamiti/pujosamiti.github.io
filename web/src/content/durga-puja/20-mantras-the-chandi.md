## What the Chandi is

Every ritual of Durga Puja — the *bodhon* that wakes the goddess, the *anjali* of cupped palms, the forty-eight minutes of Sandhi Puja, the pre-dawn radio voice of Mahalaya — stands on a single book. Bengalis call it simply the *Chandi* (চণ্ডী), and the act of reciting it *Chandipath* (চণ্ডীপাঠ), "the reading of the Chandi." Its formal names are **Devi Mahatmya**, "The Glory of the Goddess," and **Durga Saptashati**, "the Seven Hundred [verses] of Durga" — in Bengal it is most often printed and shelved as *Shri Shri Chandi* (শ্রীশ্রীচণ্ডী). It is thirteen chapters embedded in a much older text, the *Markandeya Purana* (its chapters 81 to 93), and scholars generally place its composition around the fifth or sixth century CE. That makes it the oldest complete scripture in the Hindu tradition in which ultimate reality — the power that creates, sustains and dissolves the universe — is a woman.

The name *Saptashati* promises seven hundred *mantras*, and the traditional editions deliver exactly that — though by an accountant's logic that surprises newcomers. The count of 700 includes not only the full verses (535 of them in the standard reckoning) but also every half-verse, every speech-marker — each "*devī uvācha*," "the Goddess said," counts as one mantra — and a set of part-verses called *avadanas*. The colophons of the traditional text tally them chapter by chapter: 57 *uvachas*, 42 half-verses, 535 full verses, 66 *avadanas* — 700 in all. The point of the arithmetic is theological, not editorial: in a Chandipath, *every* utterance of the text, down to "she said," is treated as a mantra with power of its own.

### The three charitas

The thirteen chapters divide into three unequal episodes, called *charitas* (চরিত), and the division is not just narrative — each charita has its own presiding form of the Goddess, its own seer, and its own place in ritual:

| Charita | Chapters | Story | Presiding form | Seer (rishi) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Prathama* (first) | 1 | Madhu and Kaitabha | Mahakali | Brahma |
| *Madhyama* (middle) | 2–4 | Mahishasura | Mahalakshmi | Vishnu |
| *Uttama* (final) | 5–13 | Shumbha and Nishumbha | Mahasarasvati | Rudra |

In the **first charita**, the Goddess never lifts a weapon. She is *Yoganidra*, the cosmic sleep lying over Vishnu at the dissolution of the world; two demons born of his earwax, Madhu and Kaitabha, rise to kill Brahma, and the Goddess's role is simply to *withdraw* — to leave Vishnu's body so that he wakes and fights. She is here the power behind every other power: the lesson under the story is that even the great gods act only because she lets them.

In the **middle charita**, she is the radiant war-goddess Bengalis know from the pandal: born from the pooled *tejas* — the blazing energy — of all the gods together, armed with a duplicate of every god's weapon, riding a lion, slaying the shape-shifting buffalo demon Mahishasura. This is the episode the murti freezes in clay — the spear, the lion, the buffalo, the emerging demon — the image whose story is told in [The Goddess and Her Story](01-the-goddess-and-her-story.md).

The **final charita** is the longest and strangest: the Goddess as Parvati's own luminous sheath (Kaushiki), the demon brothers Shumbha and Nishumbha who want to *marry* her, the emergence of black Kali from her furrowed brow, the seven Mothers, the blood-seed demon Raktabija, and at the end a Goddess who withdraws all her emanations into herself and announces that she was only ever one.

### The frame story

The thirteen chapters are themselves a story told inside a story. A king named **Suratha** (সুরথ), defeated by enemies and betrayed by his own ministers, rides alone into the forest. There he meets a merchant, **Samadhi** (সমাধি), thrown out of his house by the wife and sons who wanted his money. Both men discover the same absurdity in themselves: they cannot stop loving and worrying about the very people who ruined them. They put the question to the forest sage **Medhas** (মেধস): why do even those who *know better* behave like this? The sage answers that this is no personal failing — it is *Mahamaya*, the Great Illusion, the Goddess herself, who hurls even the minds of the wise into attachment, and who alone, when gracious, grants release. The entire Devi Mahatmya is the sage's answer unfolding: three stories of who this Goddess is. At the end, king and merchant worship her for three years on a riverbank — with an image made of earth, which Bengali tradition has always heard as the first clay murti — and each receives what he actually wanted: the king his kingdom (and a future as Savarni, the eighth Manu), the merchant the knowledge that cuts attachment.

### Its place in Bengali pujo

The Chandi is not a book Bengalis meet once a year. Devout households keep a copy wrapped in red cloth on the shrine shelf and read from it daily or weekly through the year; reading the whole text in one sitting is a defined ritual act (a *path*, পাঠ) with its own preliminaries and seal. During Durga Puja the *purohit* (পুরোহিত) recites it across the festival days, and on **Ashtami** the Chandipath reaches its public peak — many samitis, ours included, arrange a full or partial reading on that day, and the Sandhi Puja that closes Ashtami is built directly on the Chandi's seventh chapter. And once a year the text belongs to absolutely everyone: the **Mahalaya** broadcast *Mahishasuramardini*, aired since 1931, is at its core a Chandipath — Birendrakrishna Bhadra's recitation weaves the Chandi's own Sanskrit through songs and narration, which is why the average Bengali, asked to quote scripture, will produce *"Ya devi sarvabhuteshu…"* in a 4 a.m. radio cadence. Nearly every mantra elsewhere in this book has its home address somewhere in this text or its appendices — the pranam verses, Chamunda's naming, the Navarna. (See [Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md) for the day itself, and [The Mahalaya Broadcast](21-the-mahalaya-broadcast.md) for what exactly the programme recites.)

### How this chapter quotes the text

A word about scope, so you know what you are holding. The full Saptashati is seven hundred mantras; transcribing it whole is a book, not a chapter. What this reference does instead: the **"Ya devi sarvabhuteshu" litany of chapter 5 is given in full**, because it is the passage every Bengali knows; the **protective preliminaries, the Navarna mantra, and the two great hymns are given substantially**, with their most-recited verses; and **each of the thirteen chapters gets a faithful summary plus one famous verse**. Every quoted verse follows this book's convention: Devanagari first, then the same Sanskrit in Bengali script — the letters in which most of us actually met these mantras — then a roman transliteration, then a plain-words gloss. All Sanskrit quoted here has been checked against two independent published sources of the text (the Sanskrit Documents collection and, verse by verse, second witnesses including Vaidika Vignanam, Drik Panchang and Sanskrit Wikisource). The glosses are our own plain-English renderings of the sense — they aim to be honest, not poetic, and they are not a substitute for a full translation.

## The protective preliminaries — Kavacham, Argala and Kilaka

A traditional Chandipath never opens the first chapter cold. Three short hymns — *Kavacham* (কবচ, "the armour"), *Argala* (অর্গলা, "the bolt" of a door), and *Kilaka* (কীলক, "the pin" or "peg") — are recited first, and each has a stated job. The tradition's own logic runs like this: the Chandi is a text of enormous voltage; before handling it, the reciter armours the body (Kavacham), unbolts the door to the text's power (Argala), and pulls the pin that Shiva himself is said to have placed to lock the text's efficacy away from careless use (Kilaka). Only then does the path proper begin.

### Devi Kavacham — the armour

The Kavacham presents itself as a secret Brahma confides to the sage Markandeya. Its opening exchange:

> ॐ यद्गुह्यं परमं लोके सर्वरक्षाकरं नृणाम् ।
> यन्न कस्यचिदाख्यातं तन्मे ब्रूहि पितामह ॥
>
> ब्रह्मोवाच ।
> अस्ति गुह्यतमं विप्र सर्वभूतोपकारकम् ।
> देव्यास्तु कवचं पुण्यं तच्छृणुष्व महामुने ॥
>
> ওঁ যদ্গুহ্যং পরমং লোকে সর্বরক্ষাকরং নৃণাম্ ।
> যন্ন কস্যচিদাখ্যাতং তন্মে ব্রূহি পিতামহ ॥
>
> ব্রহ্মোবাচ ।
> অস্তি গুহ্যতমং বিপ্র সর্বভূতোপকারকম্ ।
> দেব্যাস্তু কবচং পুণ্যং তচ্ছৃণুষ্ব মহামুনে ॥
>
> *oṃ yad guhyaṃ paramaṃ loke sarvarakṣākaraṃ nṛṇām |*
> *yan na kasyacid ākhyātaṃ tan me brūhi pitāmaha ||*
>
> *brahmovāca —*
> *asti guhyatamaṃ vipra sarvabhūtopakārakam |*
> *devyās tu kavacaṃ puṇyaṃ tac chṛṇuṣva mahāmune ||*

**In simple words:** "Tell me, Grandfather, that supreme secret of the world — the one that protects all people and has never been told to anyone." And Brahma answers: "There is indeed a most hidden thing, a benefactor of every being — the holy armour of the Goddess. Hear it, great sage."

What follows first is a list Bengalis will recognise from Navaratri all over India — the earliest known enumeration of the **nine Durgas**, the *Navadurga* (নবদুর্গা):

> प्रथमं शैलपुत्रीति द्वितीयं ब्रह्मचारिणी ।
> तृतीयं चन्द्रघण्टेति कूष्माण्डेति चतुर्थकम् ॥
> पञ्चमं स्कन्दमातेति षष्ठं कात्यायनी तथा ।
> सप्तमं कालरात्रिश्च महागौरीति चाष्टमम् ॥
> नवमं सिद्धिदात्री च नवदुर्गाः प्रकीर्तिताः ।
>
> প্রথমং শৈলপুত্রীতি দ্বিতীয়ং ব্রহ্মচারিণী ।
> তৃতীয়ং চন্দ্রঘণ্টেতি কূষ্মাণ্ডেতি চতুর্থকম্ ॥
> পঞ্চমং স্কন্দমাতেতি ষষ্ঠং কাত্যায়নী তথা ।
> সপ্তমং কালরাত্রিশ্চ মহাগৌরীতি চাষ্টমম্ ॥
> নবমং সিদ্ধিদাত্রী চ নবদুর্গাঃ প্রকীর্তিতাঃ ।
>
> *prathamaṃ śailaputrīti dvitīyaṃ brahmacāriṇī |*
> *tṛtīyaṃ candraghaṇṭeti kūṣmāṇḍeti caturthakam ||*
> *pañcamaṃ skandamāteti ṣaṣṭhaṃ kātyāyanī tathā |*
> *saptamaṃ kālarātriś ca mahāgaurīti cāṣṭamam ||*
> *navamaṃ siddhidātrī ca navadurgāḥ prakīrtitāḥ |*

**In simple words:** First Shailaputri, second Brahmacharini, third Chandraghanta, fourth Kushmanda; fifth Skandamata, sixth Katyayani, seventh Kalaratri, eighth Mahagauri; ninth Siddhidatri — these are proclaimed the nine Durgas.

Then the armour is put on, direction by direction — a different goddess guarding each of the ten quarters of space:

> प्राच्यां रक्षतु मामैन्द्री आग्नेय्यामग्निदेवता ।
> दक्षिणेऽवतु वाराही नैर्ऋत्यां खड्गधारिणी ॥
> प्रतीच्यां वारुणी रक्षेद्वायव्यां मृगवाहिनी ।
> उदीच्यां पातु कौबेरी ईशान्यां शूलधारिणी ॥
>
> প্রাচ্যাং রক্ষতু মামৈন্দ্রী আগ্নেয্যামগ্নিদেবতা ।
> দক্ষিণেঽবতু বারাহী নৈর্ঋত্যাং খড্গধারিণী ॥
> প্রতীচ্যাং বারুণী রক্ষেদ্বায়ব্যাং মৃগবাহিনী ।
> উদীচ্যাং পাতু কৌবেরী ঈশান্যাং শূলধারিণী ॥
>
> *prācyāṃ rakṣatu mām aindrī āgneyyām agnidevatā |*
> *dakṣiṇe'vatu vārāhī nairṛtyāṃ khaḍgadhāriṇī ||*
> *pratīcyāṃ vāruṇī rakṣed vāyavyāṃ mṛgavāhinī |*
> *udīcyāṃ pātu kauberī īśānyāṃ śūladhāriṇī ||*

**In simple words:** In the east may Aindri protect me, in the southeast the goddess of fire; in the south Varahi, in the southwest She who bears the sword; in the west Varuni, in the northwest She who rides the deer; in the north Kauberi, in the northeast She who bears the trident. (The following verse sets Brahmani above, Vaishnavi below, and Chamunda — riding a corpse — over all ten directions at once.) And around the reciter's own person:

> जया मामग्रतः पातु विजया पातु पृष्ठतः ।
> अजिता वामपार्श्वे तु दक्षिणे चापराजिता ॥
>
> জয়া মামগ্রতঃ পাতু বিজয়া পাতু পৃষ্ঠতঃ ।
> অজিতা বামপার্শ্বে তু দক্ষিণে চাপরাজিতা ॥
>
> *jayā mām agrataḥ pātu vijayā pātu pṛṣṭhataḥ |*
> *ajitā vāmapārśve tu dakṣiṇe cāparājitā ||*

**In simple words:** May Jaya — Victory — guard me in front, and Vijaya behind; Ajita — the Unconquered — on my left side, and Aparajita — the Undefeated — on my right. (Some editions read the first quarter as *jayā me cāgrataḥ pātu*; the sense is identical.)

**The body-armour litany, honestly described.** From here the Kavacham becomes something best characterised plainly: a head-to-toe inventory. For roughly twenty-five verses, a named goddess is assigned to every part of the reciter's body — topknot, skull, forehead, eyebrows, the space between the eyebrows, eyes, ears, cheeks, nostrils, upper lip, lower lip, tongue, teeth, throat, voice, neck, spine, shoulders, arms, hands, fingers, nails, chest, heart, belly, navel, hips, thighs, knees, shins, ankles, feet, toes, soles — and then *inward*: skin, blood, marrow, fat, flesh, bone, intestines, bile, phlegm, semen, and onward into the non-physical person: shadow, ego, mind, intellect, the five vital breaths, the senses of taste, sight, smell, sound and touch, the three gunas, lifespan, dharma, fame, wealth, family line, cattle, children, spouse, and even the road one travels on. Nothing is left out, because that is precisely the design — the closing verses say so in as many words:

> रक्षाहीनं तु यत्स्थानं वर्जितं कवचेन तु ।
> तत्सर्वं रक्ष मे देवि जयन्ती पापनाशिनी ॥
>
> রক্ষাহীনং তু যৎস্থানং বর্জিতং কবচেন তু ।
> তৎসর্বং রক্ষ মে দেবি জয়ন্তী পাপনাশিনী ॥
>
> *rakṣāhīnaṃ tu yat sthānaṃ varjitaṃ kavacena tu |*
> *tat sarvaṃ rakṣa me devi jayantī pāpanāśinī ||*

**In simple words:** And whatever spot is left unguarded — whatever this armour has missed — protect all of that, O Goddess Jayanti, destroyer of evil. It is a safety-net clause written into the mantra itself, and there is something very touching about it: the composer of the ultimate protective hymn assumes his own list is incomplete, and hands the gap to the Goddess.

### Argala — the bolt

The *Argala-stotram* is the most singable of the three preliminaries: some two dozen verses that each hail the Goddess by a deed or a title in the first half, and then repeat, without variation, the same second half — one of the most repeated refrains in all Sanskrit liturgy. The opening verse and the refrain:

> ॐ जय त्वं देवि चामुण्डे जय भूतापहारिणि ।
> जय सर्वगते देवि कालरात्रि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> मधुकैटभविध्वंसि विधातृवरदे नमः ।
> **रूपं देहि जयं देहि यशो देहि द्विषो जहि ॥**
>
> ওঁ জয় ত্বং দেবি চামুণ্ডে জয় ভূতাপহারিণি ।
> জয় সর্বগতে দেবি কালরাত্রি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> মধুকৈটভবিধ্বংসি বিধাতৃবরদে নমঃ ।
> **রূপং দেহি জয়ং দেহি যশো দেহি দ্বিষো জহি ॥**
>
> *oṃ jaya tvaṃ devi cāmuṇḍe jaya bhūtāpahāriṇi |*
> *jaya sarvagate devi kālarātri namo'stu te ||*
>
> *madhukaiṭabhavidhvaṃsi vidhātṛvarade namaḥ |*
> ***rūpaṃ dehi jayaṃ dehi yaśo dehi dviṣo jahi ||***

**In simple words:** Victory to you, Goddess Chamunda; victory, remover of affliction; victory, all-pervading one, Kalaratri — obeisance to you. Destroyer of Madhu and Kaitabha, granter of Brahma's boon, obeisance — and then the refrain that will return at the end of every following verse: ***give form, give victory, give fame; slay hostility.***

The four requests of the refrain are worth pausing on: *rupam* (inner and outer excellence), *jayam* (victory), *yashah* (good name), and *dviṣo jahi* — not "kill my enemies" so much as "destroy enmity, hostility itself"; commentators have always read the enemies inwardly as much as outwardly. The hymn works through the Goddess's victories — over Mahishasura, Dhumralochana, Chanda and Munda, Raktabija, Shumbha and Nishumbha — asking the same four things after each, a bolt hammered back again and again until the door stands open. One of its later verses is a small, complete prayer in itself, often taught to children as "the asking verse":

> देहि सौभाग्यमारोग्यं देहि मे परमं सुखम् ।
> रूपं देहि जयं देहि यशो देहि द्विषो जहि ॥
>
> দেহি সৌভাগ্যমারোগ্যং দেহি মে পরমং সুখম্ ।
> রূপং দেহি জয়ং দেহি যশো দেহি দ্বিষো জহি ॥
>
> *dehi saubhāgyam ārogyaṃ dehi me paramaṃ sukham |*
> *rūpaṃ dehi jayaṃ dehi yaśo dehi dviṣo jahi ||*

**In simple words:** Give good fortune and health; give me the highest happiness; give form, give victory, give fame — and slay hostility.

### Kilaka — the pin

The *Kilaka-stotram* is the least recited-about and the most curious. Its premise: Shiva himself placed a *kilaka* — a pin, a restraining peg — on the Chandi, so that its immense results cannot be harvested casually or by half-measures. The hymn states the rule:

> सर्वमेतद्विजानीयान्मन्त्राणामपि कीलकम् ।
> सोऽपि क्षेममवाप्नोति सततं जप्यतत्परः ॥
>
> সর্বমেতদ্বিজানীয়ান্মন্ত্রাণামপি কীলকম্ ।
> সোঽপি ক্ষেমমবাপ্নোতি সততং জপ্যতৎপরঃ ॥
>
> *sarvam etad vijānīyān mantrāṇām api kīlakam |*
> *so'pi kṣemam avāpnoti satataṃ japyatatparaḥ ||*

**In simple words:** Let one know this fully — it is the pin even of mantras; the one devoted to constant recitation attains well-being. The hymn goes on to say that for the committed reciter no other mantra and no herb is needed, and that one who "unpins" the Chandi (*niṣkīlāṃ vidhāya*) and recites it regularly prospers and is freed, while one who begins without this knowledge comes to grief. Whatever one makes of the tantric mechanics, the pastoral point underneath is clear enough, and priests still teach it: the Chandi asks for commitment, not sampling.

## The Navarna mantra

Book-ending every formal Chandipath — recited as *japa* before the text begins and again after it ends, and woven by the *purohit* through the puja itself — is the nine-syllable seed-mantra of the Goddess, the **Navarna** or **Navakshari** (নবার্ণ) mantra:

> ॐ ऐं ह्रीं क्लीं चामुण्डायै विच्चे ।
>
> ওঁ ঐং হ্রীং ক্লীং চামুণ্ডায়ৈ বিচ্চে ।
>
> *oṃ aiṃ hrīṃ klīṃ cāmuṇḍāyai vicce |*

**In simple words:** Om — aim, hrim, klim — to Chamunda, vicce. Three seed-sounds with no dictionary meaning, then a name, then a sealing syllable: the whole Saptashati folded into one breath, as the rest of this section unpacks.

The nine syllables (the opening *oṃ* is counted separately, as the universal frame) are: **aiṃ — hrīṃ — klīṃ — cā — muṇ — ḍā — yai — vic — ce**.

The first three are *bijas*, seed-syllables, each a compressed invocation with no dictionary meaning but a precise ritual identity:

- **ऐं / ঐং / aiṃ** — the *vag-bija*, the seed of speech, knowledge and learning, associated with Sarasvati's aspect of the Goddess;
- **ह्रीं / হ্রীং / hrīṃ** — the *maya-bija*, the seed of the Goddess's world-making power and sovereignty, associated with her Mahalakshmi aspect;
- **क्लीं / ক্লীং / klīṃ** — the *kama-bija*, the seed of desire and transforming will, associated with her dark, dissolving Mahakali aspect.

Together the three bijas are traditionally read as invoking the three great forms who preside over the three charitas — Mahakali, Mahalakshmi, Mahasarasvati — so that the mantra is the whole Saptashati in miniature. **चामुण्डायै / চামুণ্ডায়ৈ / cāmuṇḍāyai** is plain grammar: "to Chamunda," the Goddess in her fiercest, most protective form (whose naming the Chandi itself narrates in chapter 7). **विच्चे / বিচ্চে / vicce** is a seed-ending that the tradition deliberately leaves untranslated; commentators have connected it with *cit*, consciousness, but its authority in practice comes from transmission, not etymology. The mantra's classical *viniyoga* (assignment) names Brahma, Vishnu and Rudra as its three seers, Gayatri, Ushnik and Anushtup as its three metres, and Mahakali, Mahalakshmi and Mahasarasvati as its three deities — with *aiṃ* as its seed, *hrīṃ* as its power, and *klīṃ* as its pin.

One structural detail of the traditional printed text makes the "book-ending" idea visible on the page: in standard editions, chapter 1 opens with *oṃ aiṃ* and closes with *aiṃ oṃ*; chapter 2 opens with *oṃ hrīṃ* and the middle charita closes at chapter 4 with *hrīṃ oṃ*; chapter 5 opens with *oṃ klīṃ* and the whole text closes at chapter 13 with *klīṃ oṃ*. The three seed-syllables literally bracket the three charitas — the Navarna mantra is not pasted onto the Chandi; it is built into its architecture. This is also the "root-sound" that threads the Sandhi Puja rites, whose mantras are treated in [Mantras: Sandhi Puja](17-mantras-sandhi-puja.md).

## The "Ya devi sarvabhuteshu" litany

If one passage of the Chandi lives in every Bengali ear, it is this one. It comes early in chapter 5: stripped of their powers by Shumbha and Nishumbha, the gods climb to the Himalaya and praise the Goddess — first by her names, then in a long, swinging litany that finds her present *inside every living being*, one faculty at a time. This is the passage Birendrakrishna Bhadra's Mahalaya broadcast made into the collective alarm-clock of Bengal; ask anyone to recite "the Mahalaya mantra" and this is what comes out. It is presented here **in full**.

The hymn opens:

> नमो देव्यै महादेव्यै शिवायै सततं नमः ।
> नमः प्रकृत्यै भद्रायै नियताः प्रणताः स्म ताम् ॥
>
> নমো দেব্যৈ মহাদেব্যৈ শিবায়ৈ সততং নমঃ ।
> নমঃ প্রকৃত্যৈ ভদ্রায়ৈ নিয়তাঃ প্রণতাঃ স্ম তাম্ ॥
>
> *namo devyai mahādevyai śivāyai satataṃ namaḥ |*
> *namaḥ prakṛtyai bhadrāyai niyatāḥ praṇatāḥ sma tām ||*

**In simple words:** Obeisance to the Goddess, to the Great Goddess; to the Auspicious One, obeisance always; obeisance to Nature, to the Gracious One — disciplined, we have bowed to her.

Five more verses of names follow (Raudra, Nitya, Gauri, Dhatri; moonlight and the moon's very form; Kalyani, Riddhi, Siddhi; Nairriti, Lakshmi, Sharvani; Durga, "the farther shore of every difficulty"; Krishna and Dhumra, the dark and the smoke-grey — "most gentle and most terrible, to her obeisance again and again"). Then the litany proper begins. Its first two verses carry their own wording:

> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु **विष्णुमायेति** शब्दिता ।
> नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमो नमः ॥
>
> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु **चेतनेत्य**भिधीयते ।
> नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमो नमः ॥
>
> যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু **বিষ্ণুমায়েতি** শব্দিতা ।
> নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমো নমঃ ॥
>
> যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু **চেতনেত্য**ভিধীয়তে ।
> নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমো নমঃ ॥
>
> *yā devī sarvabhūteṣu **viṣṇumāyeti** śabditā |*
> *namas tasyai namas tasyai namas tasyai namo namaḥ ||*
>
> *yā devī sarvabhūteṣu **cetanety** abhidhīyate |*
> *namas tasyai namas tasyai namas tasyai namo namaḥ ||*

**In simple words:** The Goddess who in all beings is called *Vishnumaya* — obeisance to her, obeisance to her, obeisance to her, obeisance, obeisance. The Goddess who in all beings is spoken of as *consciousness* — obeisance to her…

From the third verse onward the frame settles into its fixed form, the one every reciter knows by heart — the same verse over and over, changing only one word:

> या देवी सर्वभूतेषु …रूपेण संस्थिता ।
> नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमस्तस्यै नमो नमः ॥
>
> যা দেবী সর্বভূতেষু …রূপেণ সংস্থিতা ।
> নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমস্তস্যৈ নমো নমঃ ॥
>
> *yā devī sarvabhūteṣu …rūpeṇa saṃsthitā |*
> *namas tasyai namas tasyai namas tasyai namo namaḥ ||*

**In simple words:** The Goddess who abides in all beings in the form of … — obeisance to her, obeisance to her, obeisance to her, obeisance, obeisance.

Into that blank the litany places, in canonical order (verified here against two independent editions of chapter 5), the following forms — twenty-one in the *ya devi* frame, counting the two special openers. **In simple words:** She who presides over the senses of every creature, and is in all beings always — to that pervading Goddess, obeisance, obeisance. She who, as pure consciousness, stands having filled this entire universe — obeisance to her, obeisance to her, obeisance to her, obeisance, obeisance.

**One flowing gloss of the whole litany.** The theology here is best taken in a single breath, the way the litany itself moves. The Goddess is not being praised as someone *out there* who is powerful; she is being located *in here*, in every creature, as the ordinary furniture of experience. She is the illusion that makes the world seem solid, and she is the consciousness that watches it; she is intelligence and she is sleep; she is hunger and its opposite, contentment; she is thirst — craving itself — and she is the patience that endures craving; she is memory and she is error; she is shame, peace, faith, beauty, wealth, work, compassion; she is motherhood; and she is the shadow every body throws. Notice what the list refuses to do: it does not sort her into the flattering faculties only. Sleep, hunger, craving and delusion stand in the litany with exactly the same refrain as faith, compassion and peace — because a Goddess who is truly *sarvabhuteshu*, in all beings, must be all of what beings are, not just the parts we are proud of. Nothing that moves in a living being is outside her. The two closing verses then step back for the widest possible frame: she presides over the senses of every creature, she *pervades* — and as *citi*, pure consciousness, she stands having filled this entire universe. Obeisance to her, again and again and again.

In Bengali practice this litany has a life of its own: sung, set to ragas, recited at anjali, repeated by a whole pandal after the purohit line by line — the closest the tradition comes to a spoken creed — and above all fixed forever in the Mahalaya broadcast, where its slow triple *namas tasyai* is, for millions, the actual sound of the festival beginning.

## Chapter by chapter

What follows is the whole Devi Mahatmya in thirteen summaries — each an honest condensation in our own words, each carrying one famous verse of the original in all three scripts.

### Chapter 1 — The slaying of Madhu and Kaitabha (madhu-kaiṭabha-vadha)

The sage Markandeya begins by promising the story of the future eighth Manu, Savarni — and immediately drops into the frame story. King Suratha, defeated abroad and robbed at home, rides into the forest; he meets the merchant Samadhi, expelled by his own family; both confess the same helpless attachment to those who wronged them, and the sage Medhas explains: this is Mahamaya, who drags even the minds of the knowing into delusion — and who, pleased, becomes the giver of liberation. Asked who this Goddess is, the sage tells the first tale: at the dissolution of the world, Vishnu sleeps on the serpent Shesha upon the single ocean; the demons Madhu and Kaitabha spring from the wax of his ears and rush to kill Brahma, who sits on the lotus of Vishnu's navel. Brahma, unable to wake Vishnu, does the only thing left — he praises the Goddess *as* the sleep lying over Vishnu, the *tamasi*, Yoganidra, hymning her as the maker, sustainer and devourer of the universe. She withdraws; Vishnu wakes and grapples with the demons for five thousand years; intoxicated by their own might (and befuddled by Mahamaya), they offer him a boon; he asks for their death. Tricked, they stipulate only that it happen "where the earth is not covered by water" — so Vishnu lifts them onto his own thigh and beheads them there. The chapter's most quoted verse is the sage's definition of the Goddess's power over every mind:

> ज्ञानिनामपि चेतांसि देवी भगवती हि सा ।
> बलादाकृष्य मोहाय महामाया प्रयच्छति ॥
>
> জ্ঞানিনামপি চেতাংসি দেবী ভগবতী হি সা ।
> বলাদাকৃষ্য মোহায় মহামায়া প্রয়চ্ছতি ॥
>
> *jñāninām api cetāṃsi devī bhagavatī hi sā |*
> *balād ākṛṣya mohāya mahāmāyā prayacchati ||*

**In simple words:** Even the minds of the wise — that blessed Goddess, Mahamaya, seizes them by force and delivers them to delusion. It is the Chandi's thesis statement: attachment is not a bug in creation; it is her doing, and therefore only she can undo it.

### Chapter 2 — The slaughter of Mahishasura's armies (mahiṣāsura-sainya-vadha)

The middle charita opens on a cosmic emergency: for a hundred years the gods have fought the buffalo demon Mahishasura, and lost. Mahisha now sits in Indra's seat; the gods walk the earth like mortals. When they bring their story to Vishnu and Shiva, anger blazes out of the assembled gods' faces as light — *tejas* — and the separate fires flow together into a single mountain of flame that resolves into the body of a woman: face from Shiva's light, hair from Yama's, arms from Vishnu's, waist from Indra's, and so on, feature by feature. Each god then hands her a duplicate of his own weapon — trident from Shiva, discus from Vishnu, conch from Varuna, thunderbolt from Indra, a lion from Himalaya, a drinking-cup from Kubera — the scene every Bengali child has heard narrated over the radio in the dark of Mahalaya morning. She laughs a world-shaking laugh, the seas boil, and the demons pour out to investigate; the rest of the chapter is the annihilation of Mahisha's generals and their uncountable armies, the Goddess multiplying her own forces from her breath. The verse to know is the moment of her assembling:

> अतुलं तत्र तत्तेजः सर्वदेवशरीरजम् ।
> एकस्थं तदभून्नारी व्याप्तलोकत्रयं त्विषा ॥
>
> অতুলং তত্র তত্তেজঃ সর্বদেবশরীরজম্ ।
> একস্থং তদভূন্নারী ব্যাপ্তলোকত্রয়ং ত্বিষা ॥
>
> *atulaṃ tatra tat tejaḥ sarvadevaśarīrajam |*
> *ekasthaṃ tad abhūn nārī vyāptalokatrayaṃ tviṣā ||*

**In simple words:** That matchless blaze, born from the bodies of all the gods, gathered into one — and it became a woman, filling the three worlds with her light. Note the direction of the theology: she is not assembled *by* the gods as their tool; their pooled power simply *is* her, momentarily visible. Chapter 4 will make the point explicit.

### Chapter 3 — The slaying of Mahishasura (mahiṣāsura-vadha)

The duel itself. Mahisha's remaining generals — Chikshura, Chamara and the rest — fall one by one, and then the buffalo demon enters the battle in his own form, terrorising the Goddess's hosts, tossing mountains with his horns, lashing the ocean with his tail. What follows is the shape-shifting sequence the murti-makers freeze in clay: snared by her noose he abandons the buffalo form and becomes a lion; she beheads the lion and he is a swordsman; she cuts him down and he is an elephant; she severs the trunk and he is the buffalo again. Then the Goddess pauses — laughs, drinks from her cup, her eyes reddening — and delivers the Chandi's most theatrical line before leaping onto the demon, pinning his neck under her foot and running him through; as he struggles half-emerged from the buffalo's mouth in his own true shape, she beheads him. The taunt is the verse everyone quotes:

> गर्ज गर्ज क्षणं मूढ मधु यावत्पिबाम्यहम् ।
> मया त्वयि हतेऽत्रैव गर्जिष्यन्त्याशु देवताः ॥
>
> গর্জ গর্জ ক্ষণং মূঢ় মধু যাবৎপিবাম্যহম্ ।
> ময়া ত্বয়ি হতেঽত্রৈব গর্জিষ্যন্ত্যাশু দেবতাঃ ॥
>
> *garja garja kṣaṇaṃ mūḍha madhu yāvat pibāmy aham |*
> *mayā tvayi hate'traiva garjiṣyanty āśu devatāḥ ||*

**In simple words:** Roar, fool — roar for a moment more, while I drink this wine. When I have slain you, it is the gods who will roar — here, at once. In the pandal, look at the murti's face on Ashtami: that serene half-smile above the spear is this verse.

### Chapter 4 — The praise by Indra and the gods (śakrādi-stuti)

The battle won, the gods led by Indra (Shakra) pour out the first of the Chandi's two great hymns, the **Shakradaya Stuti** — treated at length in "The great hymns" below. It is the text's central theology in lyric form: she is the power by which the world is made and held, beyond the telling even of Vishnu, Brahma and Shiva; she is fortune in the houses of the good and ruin in the houses of the wicked; and — the hymn's most striking idea — even her killing is grace, since demons slain by her weapons are purified and go to heaven. Pleased, the Goddess offers a boon; the gods ask only that she return whenever they remember her, and that she bless whoever praises her with these hymns. She consents and vanishes. The most beloved verse of the hymn:

> या श्रीः स्वयं सुकृतिनां भवनेष्वलक्ष्मीः
> पापात्मनां कृतधियां हृदयेषु बुद्धिः ।
> श्रद्धा सतां कुलजनप्रभवस्य लज्जा
> तां त्वां नताः स्म परिपालय देवि विश्वम् ॥
>
> যা শ্রীঃ স্বয়ং সুকৃতিনাং ভবনেষ্বলক্ষ্মীঃ
> পাপাত্মনাং কৃতধিয়াং হৃদয়েষু বুদ্ধিঃ ।
> শ্রদ্ধা সতাং কুলজনপ্রভবস্য লজ্জা
> তাং ত্বাং নতাঃ স্ম পরিপালয় দেবি বিশ্বম্ ॥
>
> *yā śrīḥ svayaṃ sukṛtināṃ bhavaneṣv alakṣmīḥ*
> *pāpātmanāṃ kṛtadhiyāṃ hṛdayeṣu buddhiḥ |*
> *śraddhā satāṃ kulajanaprabhavasya lajjā*
> *tāṃ tvāṃ natāḥ sma paripālaya devi viśvam ||*

**In simple words:** You who are prosperity itself in the homes of the good, and misfortune in the homes of the wicked; intelligence in hearts made wise, faith in the honest, and modesty in the well-born — to you we have bowed. Protect the universe, O Goddess. The chapter closes with the sage's pivot to the third tale: how she was born again, this time from Gauri's own body, to destroy Shumbha and Nishumbha.

### Chapter 5 — The Goddess's conversation with the envoy (devyā dūta-saṃvāda)

The final charita opens with new tyrants: the brothers Shumbha and Nishumbha have taken the three worlds and the gods' shares of sacrifice. Remembering the boon of chapter 4, the gods go to the Himalaya and sing the "Ya devi sarvabhuteshu" litany given in full above. As they sing, Parvati arrives to bathe in the Ganga, asks whom they are praising — and from the sheath (*kosha*) of her own body a radiant goddess emerges, who is therefore called **Kaushiki**; Parvati, left dark, becomes Kalika. Shumbha's servants Chanda and Munda spot Kaushiki's unbearable beauty and advise their master, collector of all the universe's treasures, to collect her too. Shumbha sends the envoy Sugriva with a proposal of marriage — "choose me or my brother." The Goddess answers with regret that she once made a foolish vow: she can only marry the one who defeats her in battle. Let Shumbha come, then, and win her. The verse to carry away is her origin — the reason a whole tradition calls her Kaushiki:

> शरीरकोशाद्यत्तस्याः पार्वत्या निःसृताम्बिका ।
> कौशिकीति समस्तेषु ततो लोकेषु गीयते ॥
>
> শরীরকোশাদ্যত্তস্যাঃ পার্বত্যা নিঃসৃতাম্বিকা ।
> কৌশিকীতি সমস্তেষু ততো লোকেষু গীয়তে ॥
>
> *śarīrakośād yat tasyāḥ pārvatyā niḥsṛtāmbikā |*
> *kauśikīti samasteṣu tato lokeṣu gīyate ||*

**In simple words:** Because Ambika came forth from the sheath of Parvati's body, she is sung in all the worlds as Kaushiki.

### Chapter 6 — The slaying of Dhumralochana (dhūmralocana-vadha)

The shortest chapter, and almost comic in its economy. The refused Shumbha sends the general Dhumralochana — "Smoke-Eye" — with sixty thousand demons and one instruction: drag her here by the hair. Confronted, the Goddess replies with mock helplessness — "you are strong, your army is vast; if you take me by force, what can I possibly do?" — and then does this:

> इत्युक्तः सोऽभ्यधावत्तामसुरो धूम्रलोचनः ।
> हुङ्कारेणैव तं भस्म सा चकाराम्बिका तदा ॥
>
> ইত্যুক্তঃ সোঽভ্যধাবত্তামসুরো ধূম্রলোচনঃ ।
> হুঙ্কারেণৈব তং ভস্ম সা চকারাম্বিকা তদা ॥
>
> *ity uktaḥ so'bhyadhāvat tām asuro dhūmralocanaḥ |*
> *huṅkāreṇaiva taṃ bhasma sā cakārāmbikā tadā ||*

**In simple words:** So addressed, the demon Dhumralochana rushed at her — and with a single syllable of contempt, *huṁ*, Ambika turned him to ash. Not a weapon raised: one sound. Her lion then annihilates the sixty thousand. The chapter ends with Shumbha, lip trembling with rage, dispatching Chanda and Munda with the same order.

### Chapter 7 — The slaying of Chanda and Munda (caṇḍa-muṇḍa-vadha)

The chapter Bengal has built a ritual around. Chanda and Munda find the Goddess smiling gently on her golden mountain peak; when they move to seize her, her face darkens with wrath — literally, the text says her countenance turned ink-black — and from her knotted brow springs **Kali**: gaunt, terrible, sword and noose in hand, wearing a garland of skulls and a tiger's skin, her tongue lolling, her eyes sunken and red, filling the directions with her roar. What follows is the Chandi's most nightmarish battle scene: Kali eats the army — elephants with their riders and bells, chariots with their horses — crunching weapons in her teeth. She beheads Chanda, fells Munda, and delivers the two heads to Chandika as battle-trophies, an offering the text itself grimly calls a *yuddha-yajña*, a sacrifice-of-war. Chandika's reply gives the goddess of the *sandhi* moment her name:

> यस्माच्चण्डं च मुण्डं च गृहीत्वा त्वमुपागता ।
> चामुण्डेति ततो लोके ख्याता देवी भविष्यसि ॥
>
> যস্মাচ্চণ্ডং চ মুণ্ডং চ গৃহীত্বা ত্বমুপাগতা ।
> চামুণ্ডেতি ততো লোকে খ্যাতা দেবী ভবিষ্যসি ॥
>
> *yasmāc caṇḍaṃ ca muṇḍaṃ ca gṛhītvā tvam upāgatā |*
> *cāmuṇḍeti tato loke khyātā devī bhaviṣyasi ||*

**In simple words:** Because you have come to me bearing Chanda and Munda, you shall be famed in the world as the goddess **Chamunda**. This is the verse behind Sandhi Puja: the juncture of Ashtami and Nabami worships precisely this moment and this form — see [Mantras: Sandhi Puja](17-mantras-sandhi-puja.md) for the rite and its words.

### Chapter 8 — The slaying of Raktabija (raktabīja-vadha)

Shumbha now empties his empire — clan after clan of demons is named and mobilised. Against them, the Goddess is joined by the **Matrikas**, the Mothers: the *shaktis* of the great gods step out of the gods' own bodies, each with her mount and weapon — Brahmani on her swan with waterpot, Maheshvari on the bull with trident, Kaumari with Kartikeya's spear and peacock, Vaishnavi on Garuda with conch and discus, Varahi in the boar-form, Narasimhi the lion-woman who shakes stars from the sky with her mane, and Aindri with the thunderbolt. From the Goddess's own body comes the terrifying Shivaduti — so called because she sends *Shiva himself* as her messenger to Shumbha. Then the chapter's real problem enters: **Raktabija**, whose name means Blood-Seed:

> रक्तबिन्दुर्यदा भूमौ पतत्यस्य शरीरतः ।
> समुत्पतति मेदिन्यां तत्प्रमाणो महासुरः ॥
>
> রক্তবিন্দুর্যদা ভূমৌ পতত্যস্য শরীরতঃ ।
> সমুৎপততি মেদিন্যাং তৎপ্রমাণো মহাসুরঃ ॥
>
> *raktabindur yadā bhūmau pataty asya śarīrataḥ |*
> *samutpatati medinyāṃ tatpramāṇo mahāsuraḥ ||*

**In simple words:** Whenever a drop of blood falls from his body to the ground, from the earth there springs up a demon of the same measure. Every wound multiplies him; soon the world is filled with Raktabijas, and the gods despair. Chandika's solution is the image at the heart of Kali worship: she orders Chamunda-Kali to open her mouth wide and *drink* — to catch every drop before it lands, and swallow the duplicates as they rise. Bled dry into Kali's mouth, Raktabija finally falls. The Mothers dance, drunk on the blood of the slain. If you have ever wondered why Kali's tongue is out: this chapter is the oldest scriptural answer.

### Chapter 9 — The slaying of Nishumbha (niśumbha-vadha)

King Suratha — the frame story's listener, and ours — interrupts to say he must hear what happened next, and the sage obliges with the double duel: Shumbha and Nishumbha together against the Goddess, arrows meeting arrows like storm-clouds colliding. Nishumbha's weapons are dismantled one after another — sword, shield, spear, mace, axe — until the Goddess drives her trident through his chest. And then the Chandi produces one of its strangest images:

> भिन्नस्य तस्य शूलेन हृदयान्निःसृतोऽपरः ।
> महाबलो महावीर्यस्तिष्ठेति पुरुषो वदन् ॥
>
> ভিন্নস্য তস্য শূলেন হৃদয়ান্নিঃসৃতোঽপরঃ ।
> মহাবলো মহাবীর্যস্তিষ্ঠেতি পুরুষো বদন্ ॥
>
> *bhinnasya tasya śūlena hṛdayān niḥsṛto'paraḥ |*
> *mahābalo mahāvīryas tiṣṭheti puruṣo vadan ||*

**In simple words:** From the heart of him whom the trident had torn, there came forth another — mighty, valorous — a man crying "*Stop!*" A second self climbs out of the dying demon's chest, still commanding the Goddess to halt; she laughs and beheads it. The commentarial tradition has always read this as more than gore: the ego, pierced, produces one more ego, which must also fall. The lion, Kali and Shivaduti finish the army; the Mothers mop up what remains.

### Chapter 10 — The slaying of Shumbha (śumbha-vadha)

Alone now, Shumbha levels the accusation that has set up the Chandi's most important verse: "You fight puffed up with borrowed strength — the strength of all these others" — meaning Kali, the Mothers, the shaktis around her. The Goddess's reply is the theological summit of the entire text:

> एकैवाहं जगत्यत्र द्वितीया का ममापरा ।
> पश्यैता दुष्ट मय्येव विशन्त्यो मद्विभूतयः ॥
>
> একৈবাহং জগত্যত্র দ্বিতীয়া কা মমাপরা ।
> পশ্যৈতা দুষ্ট ময্যেব বিশন্ত্যো মদ্বিভূতয়ঃ ॥
>
> *ekaivāhaṃ jagaty atra dvitīyā kā mamāparā |*
> *paśyaitā duṣṭa mayy eva viśantyo madvibhūtayaḥ ||*

**In simple words:** I am alone in this universe — who else is there beside me? Watch, wicked one, as these my own manifestations enter back into me. And they do: Brahmani, Kali, all the Mothers dissolve into her body, and she stands single. Every goddess the text has shown us — and, the tradition adds, every goddess anywhere — was only ever her. The final duel is fought in earnest: weapon against weapon, then hand to hand, then *in the sky*, a wrestling match between demon and Goddess above the astonished siddhas and sages, until she dashes him to earth and drives her spear through his chest. The world exhales: skies clear, rivers return to their courses, the sun brightens, and the gods sing.

### Chapter 11 — The praise of Narayani (nārāyaṇī-stuti)

The gods' second great hymn, the **Narayani Stuti** — given substantially in the next section. Two things every reader should know are anchored here. First: this chapter is the home of the most beloved *pranam* mantra in Bengali worship, the verse recited at every anjali and every bow —

> सर्वमङ्गलमाङ्गल्ये शिवे सर्वार्थसाधिके ।
> शरण्ये त्र्यम्बके गौरि नारायणि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> সর্বমঙ্গলমাঙ্গল্যে শিবে সর্বার্থসাধিকে ।
> শরণ্যে ত্র্যম্বকে গৌরি নারায়ণি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> *sarvamaṅgalamāṅgalye śive sarvārthasādhike |*
> *śaraṇye tryambake gauri nārāyaṇi namo'stu te ||*

**In simple words:** O auspiciousness of all that is auspicious, O gracious one, accomplisher of every aim, refuge, three-eyed Gauri — Narayani, obeisance to you. Bengalis meet this verse as a free-standing prayer; it is worth knowing that it *lives* here, as verse ten of the Chandi's eleventh chapter. Second: the chapter ends with the Goddess's promises of future descents — as the destroyer of future Shumbhas born in Nanda's house, as the red-toothed Raktadantika, as Shatakshi of the hundred eyes and **Shakambhari** who feeds a famined world with vegetables from her own body, as the slayer of the demon Durgama — "whereby my name shall be **Durga**" — as fierce Bhima, and as the bee-swarm Bhramari. Whenever demons arise, she says, I shall descend. This is the promise the whole tradition rests on: she will always come back.

### Chapter 12 — The Goddess's word on the fruits of recitation (bhagavatī-vākya / phalaśruti)

The Goddess herself now prescribes her own liturgy — the *phalashruti*, the "hearing of the fruits." Whoever recites these hymns with a collected mind, she promises, will be freed of every affliction; recitation guards against enemies, robbers, kings, weapons, fire and flood; it is to be heard on the eighth, ninth and fourteenth days of the lunar fortnight — the seed of the Ashtami and Nabami Chandipath. She promises her literal presence wherever the Mahatmya is read: "I shall never forsake that place." And then comes the verse on which the entire institution of Durga Puja leans, the Chandi's own charter for the autumn festival:

> शरत्काले महापूजा क्रियते या च वार्षिकी ।
> तस्यां ममैतन्माहात्म्यं श्रुत्वा भक्तिसमन्वितः ॥
> सर्वाबाधाविनिर्मुक्तो धनधान्यसमन्वितः ।
> मनुष्यो मत्प्रसादेन भविष्यति न संशयः ॥
>
> শরৎকালে মহাপূজা ক্রিয়তে যা চ বার্ষিকী ।
> তস্যাং মমৈতন্মাহাত্ম্যং শ্রুত্বা ভক্তিসমন্বিতঃ ॥
> সর্বাবাধাবিনির্মুক্তো ধনধান্যসমন্বিতঃ ।
> মনুষ্যো মৎপ্রসাদেন ভবিষ্যতি ন সংশয়ঃ ॥
>
> *śaratkāle mahāpūjā kriyate yā ca vārṣikī |*
> *tasyāṃ mamaitan māhātmyaṃ śrutvā bhaktisamanvitaḥ ||*
> *sarvābādhāvinirmukto dhanadhānyasamanvitaḥ |*
> *manuṣyo matprasādena bhaviṣyati na saṃśayaḥ ||*

**In simple words:** And at the **great annual worship performed in autumn** — whoever hears this my Mahatmya there with devotion shall, by my grace, be freed from every trouble and endowed with wealth and grain: of this there is no doubt. *Sharatkale mahapuja* — "the great autumn worship" — is, for Bengal, this festival. When the purohit reads the Chandi at your pujo, this verse is the reason.

### Chapter 13 — The granting of boons to Suratha and the merchant (suratha-vaiśya-varapradāna)

The sage's tale is done, and the frame story returns to complete itself. His counsel to the shattered king:

> तामुपैहि महाराज शरणं परमेश्वरीम् ।
> आराधिता सैव नृणां भोगस्वर्गापवर्गदा ॥
>
> তামুপৈহি মহারাজ শরণং পরমেশ্বরীম্ ।
> আরাধিতা সৈব নৃণাং ভোগস্বর্গাপবর্গদা ॥
>
> *tām upaihi mahārāja śaraṇaṃ parameśvarīm |*
> *ārādhitā saiva nṛṇāṃ bhogasvargāpavargadā ||*

**In simple words:** Go to her for refuge, great king, the supreme sovereign Goddess: worshipped, it is she who grants human beings enjoyment, heaven, and final freedom.

King and merchant go to a riverbank, fashion an image of the Goddess out of earth (*mūrtiṃ mahīmayīm* — the scriptural ancestor of every clay protima) and worship for three years with flowers, incense, fire, austerity, and offerings marked with their own blood. Chandika appears in person and grants each his wish — and the wishes are the whole point. The king, still king, asks for his kingdom back and an unfallen kingdom in the next life; she grants it, adding that he will be reborn as Savarni, the eighth Manu — closing the loop with the text's very first verse. The merchant, wiser for his wounds, asks for the knowledge that dissolves "mine" and "I"; she grants that too. The Chandi ends with both boons honoured equally: the text refuses to rank the man who wanted the world above the man who wanted out of it. She is, as the hymns keep saying, *bhukti-mukti-pradāyinī* — giver of enjoyment *and* of liberation.

## The great hymns

Four hymns inside and around the Chandi carry most of its devotional life. Two — the Brahma Stuti of chapter 1 (Brahma's address to Yoganidra) and the "Ya devi" litany of chapter 5 (given in full above) — have been covered. The two grandest remain, plus the Vedic hymn that traditionally closes the path.

### The Shakradaya Stuti (chapter 4)

"The praise beginning with Shakra [Indra]" — twenty-odd verses in a long, rolling metre, sung by the gods over Mahishasura's body. Its most-recited opening run:

> देव्या यया ततमिदं जगदात्मशक्त्या
> निःशेषदेवगणशक्तिसमूहमूर्त्या ।
> तामम्बिकामखिलदेवमहर्षिपूज्यां
> भक्त्या नताः स्म विदधातु शुभानि सा नः ॥
>
> यस्याः प्रभावमतुलं भगवाननन्तो
> ब्रह्मा हरश्च न हि वक्तुमलं बलं च ।
> सा चण्डिकाखिलजगत्परिपालनाय
> नाशाय चाशुभभयस्य मतिं करोतु ॥
>
> দেব্যা যয়া ততমিদং জগদাত্মশক্ত্যা
> নিঃশেষদেবগণশক্তিসমূহমূর্ত্যা ।
> তামম্বিকামখিলদেবমহর্ষিপূজ্যাং
> ভক্ত্যা নতাঃ স্ম বিদধাতু শুভানি সা নঃ ॥
>
> যস্যাঃ প্রভাবমতুলং ভগবাননন্তো
> ব্রহ্মা হরশ্চ ন হি বক্তুমলং বলং চ ।
> সা চণ্ডিকাখিলজগৎপরিপালনায়
> নাশায় চাশুভভয়স্য মতিং করোতু ॥
>
> *devyā yayā tatam idaṃ jagad ātmaśaktyā*
> *niḥśeṣadevagaṇaśaktisamūhamūrtyā |*
> *tām ambikām akhiladevamaharṣipūjyāṃ*
> *bhaktyā natāḥ sma vidadhātu śubhāni sā naḥ ||*
>
> *yasyāḥ prabhāvam atulaṃ bhagavān ananto*
> *brahmā haraś ca na hi vaktum alaṃ balaṃ ca |*
> *sā caṇḍikākhilajagatparipālanāya*
> *nāśāya cāśubhabhayasya matiṃ karotu ||*

**In simple words:** The Goddess by whose own power this universe is spread out — she whose visible form was the gathered powers of all the hosts of gods — to that Mother, worshipped by every god and great seer, we bow in devotion; may she ordain what is good for us. Her greatness and her strength not even endless Vishnu, nor Brahma, nor Shiva can adequately tell; may that Chandika set her mind on protecting the whole world and destroying the fear of evil.

Then comes *yā śrīḥ svayaṃ* — "prosperity in the homes of the good, ruin in the homes of the wicked" — quoted under chapter 4 above; and the hymn's middle turns over its boldest thought: that her violence is mercy. The demons she kills are cleansed by her weapons and go to the bright worlds; "even toward enemies," the gods marvel, "her compassion is on display." Its most personal verse, recited to this day by people in trouble:

> दुर्गे स्मृता हरसि भीतिमशेषजन्तोः
> स्वस्थैः स्मृता मतिमतीव शुभां ददासि ।
> दारिद्र्यदुःखभयहारिणि का त्वदन्या
> सर्वोपकारकरणाय सदार्द्रचित्ता ॥
>
> দুর্গে স্মৃতা হরসি ভীতিমশেষজন্তোঃ
> স্বস্থৈঃ স্মৃতা মতিমতীব শুভাং দদাসি ।
> দারিদ্র্যদুঃখভয়হারিণি কা ত্বদন্যা
> সর্বোপকারকরণায় সদার্দ্রচিত্তা ॥
>
> *durge smṛtā harasi bhītim aśeṣajantoḥ*
> *svasthaiḥ smṛtā matim atīva śubhāṃ dadāsi |*
> *dāridryaduḥkhabhayahāriṇi kā tvadanyā*
> *sarvopakārakaraṇāya sadārdracittā ||*

**In simple words:** Remembered in danger, O Durga, you take away the fear of every creature; remembered by the untroubled, you grant a mind still more auspicious. Remover of poverty, sorrow and fear — who is there other than you, whose heart is always wet with compassion, always bent on helping all?

The hymn ends with the gods asking to be guarded by her spear, her sword, the clang of her bell and the twang of her bowstring, in every direction — east, west, south and north — by her gentle forms and her terrible ones alike.

### The Narayani Stuti (chapter 11)

Sung after the final victory, the Narayani Stuti is the Chandi's fullest litany of the Goddess's forms, each verse sealed with the refrain *nārāyaṇi namo'stu te* — "Narayani, obeisance to you." (Narayani: the feminine of Narayana; she who is to Vishnu's name what the power is to the god.) Its most-recited run:

> सर्वस्य बुद्धिरूपेण जनस्य हृदि संस्थिते ।
> स्वर्गापवर्गदे देवि नारायणि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> कलाकाष्ठादिरूपेण परिणामप्रदायिनि ।
> विश्वस्योपरतौ शक्ते नारायणि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> सर्वमङ्गलमाङ्गल्ये शिवे सर्वार्थसाधिके ।
> शरण्ये त्र्यम्बके गौरि नारायणि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> सृष्टिस्थितिविनाशानां शक्तिभूते सनातनि ।
> गुणाश्रये गुणमये नारायणि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> शरणागतदीनार्तपरित्राणपरायणे ।
> सर्वस्यार्तिहरे देवि नारायणि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> সর্বস্য বুদ্ধিরূপেণ জনস্য হৃদি সংস্থিতে ।
> স্বর্গাপবর্গদে দেবি নারায়ণি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> কলাকাষ্ঠাদিরূপেণ পরিণামপ্রদায়িনি ।
> বিশ্বস্যোপরতৌ শক্তে নারায়ণি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> সর্বমঙ্গলমাঙ্গল্যে শিবে সর্বার্থসাধিকে ।
> শরণ্যে ত্র্যম্বকে গৌরি নারায়ণি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> সৃষ্টিস্থিতিবিনাশানাং শক্তিভূতে সনাতনি ।
> গুণাশ্রয়ে গুণময়ে নারায়ণি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> শরণাগতদীনার্তপরিত্রাণপরায়ণে ।
> সর্বস্যার্তিহরে দেবি নারায়ণি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> *sarvasya buddhirūpeṇa janasya hṛdi saṃsthite |*
> *svargāpavargade devi nārāyaṇi namo'stu te ||*
>
> *kalākāṣṭhādirūpeṇa pariṇāmapradāyini |*
> *viśvasyoparatau śakte nārāyaṇi namo'stu te ||*
>
> *sarvamaṅgalamāṅgalye śive sarvārthasādhike |*
> *śaraṇye tryambake gauri nārāyaṇi namo'stu te ||*
>
> *sṛṣṭisthitivināśānāṃ śaktibhūte sanātani |*
> *guṇāśraye guṇamaye nārāyaṇi namo'stu te ||*
>
> *śaraṇāgatadīnārtaparitrāṇaparāyaṇe |*
> *sarvasyārtihare devi nārāyaṇi namo'stu te ||*

**In simple words:** You who dwell in every person's heart as intelligence, giver of heaven and of liberation; you who, as the smallest divisions of time — the *kalā*, the *kāṣṭhā* — bring on all change, and who have the power to end the universe; auspiciousness of all that is auspicious, gracious accomplisher of every aim, refuge, three-eyed Gauri; eternal one who are the power behind creation, preservation and destruction, ground of the gunas and made of them; you who are wholly given over to rescuing the distressed and the destitute who come to you for shelter, remover of everyone's pain — Narayani, obeisance to you. The middle verse of this run is, of course, the pranam mantra of every Bengali anjali — and hearing it in its home, framed by time-that-devours on one side and shelter-for-the-broken on the other, deepens it.

The hymn then walks through every form the final charita has shown — Brahmani on her swan, Maheshvari with trident and moon, Kaumari with her peacock, Vaishnavi with the conch and discus, Varahi who lifted the earth on her tusks, Narasimhi, Aindri, Shivaduti, skull-garlanded Chamunda; then Lakshmi, Lajja, Mahavidya, Shraddha, Pushti, Svadha, the Great Night, the Great Illusion — each hailed, each folded into the same refrain, restating in worship what chapter 10 proved in battle: all of them are one. It gathers into a plea:

> सर्वस्वरूपे सर्वेशे सर्वशक्तिसमन्विते ।
> भयेभ्यस्त्राहि नो देवि दुर्गे देवि नमोऽस्तु ते ॥
>
> সর্বস্বরূপে সর্বেশে সর্বশক্তিসমন্বিতে ।
> ভয়েভ্যস্ত্রাহি নো দেবি দুর্গে দেবি নমোঽস্তু তে ॥
>
> *sarvasvarūpe sarveśe sarvaśaktisamanvite |*
> *bhayebhyas trāhi no devi durge devi namo'stu te ||*

**In simple words:** You whose form is everything, sovereign of everything, endowed with every power — save us from our fears, O Goddess; Durga, Goddess, obeisance to you. The closing verses bless her weapons themselves — may the trident, the bell, the sword protect us — and end: "protect us, as a mother protects her children" (*pāhi no devi pāpebhyo naḥ sutān iva* — literally, "guard us from evils as your own sons").

### The Devi Suktam (Rig Veda 10.125)

Appended to every traditional Saptashati, and recited in the Mahalaya programme, is a hymn far older than the Chandi itself — the **Devi Suktam** or *Vak Suktam*, hymn 125 of the tenth book of the Rig Veda, attributed to the woman seer **Vak, daughter of Ambhrina**. It is one of the oldest texts on earth in which the divine speaks in the first person as a woman — eight verses, every one beginning *aham*, "I." The opening three:

> ॐ अहं रुद्रेभिर्वसुभिश्चराम्यहमादित्यैरुत विश्वदेवैः ।
> अहं मित्रावरुणोभा बिभर्म्यहमिन्द्राग्नी अहमश्विनोभा ॥
>
> अहं सोममाहनसं बिभर्म्यहं त्वष्टारमुत पूषणं भगम् ।
> अहं दधामि द्रविणं हविष्मते सुप्राव्ये यजमानाय सुन्वते ॥
>
> अहं राष्ट्री सङ्गमनी वसूनां चिकितुषी प्रथमा यज्ञियानाम् ।
> तां मा देवा व्यदधुः पुरुत्रा भूरिस्थात्रां भूर्यावेशयन्तीम् ॥
>
> ওঁ অহং রুদ্রেভির্বসুভিশ্চরাম্যহমাদিত্যৈরুত বিশ্বদেবৈঃ ।
> অহং মিত্রাবরুণোভা বিভর্ম্যহমিন্দ্রাগ্নী অহমশ্বিনোভা ॥
>
> অহং সোমমাহনসং বিভর্ম্যহং ত্বষ্টারমুত পূষণং ভগম্ ।
> অহং দধামি দ্রবিণং হবিষ্মতে সুপ্রাব্যে যজমানায় সুন্বতে ॥
>
> অহং রাষ্ট্রী সঙ্গমনী বসূনাং চিকিতুষী প্রথমা যজ্ঞিয়ানাম্ ।
> তাং মা দেবা ব্যদধুঃ পুরুত্রা ভূরিস্থাত্রাং ভূর্যাবেশয়ন্তীম্ ॥
>
> *oṃ ahaṃ rudrebhir vasubhiś carāmy aham ādityair uta viśvadevaiḥ |*
> *ahaṃ mitrāvaruṇobhā bibharmy aham indrāgnī aham aśvinobhā ||*
>
> *ahaṃ somam āhanasaṃ bibharmy ahaṃ tvaṣṭāram uta pūṣaṇaṃ bhagam |*
> *ahaṃ dadhāmi draviṇaṃ haviṣmate suprāvye yajamānāya sunvate ||*
>
> *ahaṃ rāṣṭrī saṅgamanī vasūnāṃ cikituṣī prathamā yajñiyānām |*
> *tāṃ mā devā vyadadhuḥ purutrā bhūristhātrāṃ bhūryāveśayantīm ||*

**In simple words:** I move with the Rudras and the Vasus, with the Adityas and all the gods; I carry both Mitra and Varuna, Indra and Agni, and both the Ashvins. I bear the pressed soma, and Tvashtri, Pushan and Bhaga; I bestow wealth on the sacrificer who presses the soma and pours the offering. I am the sovereign queen, the gatherer of treasures, the first of those worthy of worship, the one who knows; the gods have set me in many places — dwelling in many stations, entering into many forms.

The hymn rises through astonishing claims — "the one who eats, who sees, who breathes, who hears, does so through me, though they know me not"; "whom I love, I make mighty — a formulator, a seer, a sage"; "I bend the bow for Rudra"; "I gave birth to the father of this world; my womb is in the waters, in the sea" — to its vast final verse:

> अहमेव वात इव प्र वाम्यारभमाणा भुवनानि विश्वा ।
> परो दिवा पर एना पृथिव्यैतावती महिना सं बभूव ॥
>
> অহমেব বাত ইব প্র বাম্যারভমাণা ভুবনানি বিশ্বা ।
> পরো দিবা পর এনা পৃথিব্যৈতাবতী মহিনা সং বভূব ॥
>
> *aham eva vāta iva pra vāmy ārabhamāṇā bhuvanāni viśvā |*
> *paro divā para enā pṛthivyaitāvatī mahinā saṃ babhūva ||*

**In simple words:** I blow like the wind, taking hold of all the worlds — beyond the sky, beyond this earth: so great have I become in my grandeur. When the Devi Suktam is chanted at the end of a Chandipath, the effect is deliberate: after thirteen chapters *about* her, the Goddess gets the last word, and it is "I."

## How a path is performed

A formal Chandipath is a ritual with a shape, not just a read-through. What follows is the common order taught in the Bengali tradition; family and lineage practice varies in the details, and where it does, your purohit's instruction outranks this page.

**Sankalpa.** Every path begins with *sankalpa* (সংকল্প) — the formal declaration of intent: sipping water (*achamana*), then stating aloud the date by the ritual calendar, one's own name and gotra, and the purpose for which the recitation is undertaken — for the wellbeing of a family, the fulfilment of a wish, or simply for the pleasure of the Goddess (*śrī-jagadambā-prītyartham*, "for the delight of the Mother of the world," as the viniyoga formulas put it). The tradition regards a path without sankalpa as a rehearsal; the sankalpa is what makes it an act.

**The reading order.** The full classical sequence runs:

1. **Navarna japa** — the nine-syllable mantra, recited a prescribed number of times to open;
2. **The three preliminaries** — in Bengali practice most commonly *Kavacham, then Argala, then Kilaka* (armour on, bolt drawn, pin removed), each with its own brief viniyoga;
3. **The dhyana** — the meditation verse visualising Chandika; many editions give the beautiful *yā caṇḍī madhukaiṭabhādi-daitya-dalanī…*, which folds all three charitas into a single verse;
4. **The thirteen chapters**, each opened with its charita's viniyoga and dhyana where prescribed, read without conversation or interruption — traditionally one does not rise mid-charita, and if the reading must be split across days, it is split at charita boundaries;
5. **The closing limbs** — commonly the *Aparadha-kshamapana-stotram* (the "forgiveness for offences" hymn, which touchingly begs pardon even for mispronounced vowels and dropped anusvaras: whatever was read wrongly, "may it become complete by your grace"), the **Devi Suktam**, and a final round of **Navarna japa**, sealing the path with the same mantra that opened it.

Fuller tantric performances add more limbs — the Ratri Suktam before, nyasa sequences, the Rahasya texts after — and the Bengali household version often trims to Kavacham-Argala-Kilaka, the path, and a pranam. Both are traditional; scale is a matter of adhikara and time, not of validity.

**Why some read only the madhyama charita.** A complete path in one sitting takes a practiced reciter two to three hours, and the tradition itself provides sanctioned shorter forms. The most common is to read the **middle charita only** — chapters 2 to 4, the Mahishasura episode. The logic is threefold: it is a complete story with its own presiding form (Mahalakshmi) and its own viniyoga, so it functions ritually as a whole; it is the episode Durga Puja itself enacts — Mahishasuramardini is the deity on the altar; and chapter 4 ends with the gods' hymn and the Goddess's boon, giving the short path a natural liturgical close. Others follow schedules that spread the thirteen chapters across the days of Navaratri, or recite a single designated chapter (chapter 12 alone, with its promises, is a recognised minimal path). The one thing the tradition consistently discourages is stopping at an arbitrary point mid-charita.

**The Ashtami and Sandhi connection.** The Chandi itself (chapter 12) singles out the eighth, ninth and fourteenth lunar days for its recitation — which is why the *Ashtami chandipath* is a fixture of Bengali pujo: at many mandaps the purohit or a dedicated reciter reads the Saptashati (in full, or the madhyama charita) during Ashtami, and the sound of the recitation over the mandap loudspeaker is as much a part of the day as the dhak. And the festival's single most charged ritual is a Chandi chapter made flesh: **Sandhi Puja**, in the juncture between Ashtami and Nabami, worships Chamunda at the precise mythic moment of chapter 7 — the darkening of the Goddess's face, the springing forth of Kali, the fall of Chanda and Munda. The 108 lamps and 108 lotuses of sandhi belong to that verse of naming quoted above; for the ritual's mantras and mechanics, see [Mantras: Sandhi Puja](17-mantras-sandhi-puja.md).

**And Mahalaya.** Finally, the loop every probasi Bengali knows in the body: the reason a scripture this size feels intimate to millions who have never opened it is ninety minutes of radio. The *Mahishasuramardini* broadcast is, in structure, a Chandipath for a whole civilisation — Chandi verses (the "Ya devi" litany above all), the Devi Suktam, and the story of the three charitas carried on Birendrakrishna Bhadra's voice and Pankaj Mullick's music into the pre-dawn dark of Mahalaya. Its famous opening song, *Ya Chandi*, invokes precisely the text you have just read about, and the Navarna mantra threads the recitation as its root-sound; a listener who carries the three-story map from this chapter can follow the programme scene by scene. What exactly it recites, movement by movement, is the subject of [Mahalaya](04-mahalaya.md) and [The Mahalaya Broadcast](21-the-mahalaya-broadcast.md).

**Reading it yourself.** A full ritual Chandipath is a discipline, but the text has always also been read devotionally — in any language, by anyone; no initiation is required to meet the Goddess on the page. A workable rhythm for a lay household: the "Ya devi" litany of chapter 5 on any day of the pujo; chapter 7 before Sandhi Puja; chapter 11 for its promise of return; and the whole text, unhurried, once a year in Bengali or English translation — ideally in the week after Mahalaya, while the broadcast is still in the ear. Seven hundred mantras sounds like a mountain, but it is shorter than this little book — and it is the one book of which the Goddess's whole festival is a performance.

Seven hundred mantras, three stories, two great hymns, one nine-syllable seed — and at the centre of it all, a Goddess who tells a defeated king and a disillusioned merchant, through a forest sage, that she is the reason the world binds us and the one who unbinds it. That is the Chandi. When the purohit begins on Ashtami morning, or the radio crackles at four on Mahalaya, you now know what is being read.
